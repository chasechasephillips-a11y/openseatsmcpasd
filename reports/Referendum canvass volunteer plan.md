# Knock the drop-offs, hang the rest

The MCPASD Yes Committee should split its two weekends between two kinds of turf. In the six or seven walkable turfs with the most multi-voter "drop-off" households (people who voted in November 2020 or 2024 but skipped the 2022 midterm), volunteers who live there should knock **about 365 doors** and leave a hanger at every one of them. Everywhere else the committee should **hang about 1,080 doors without knocking**: the remaining walkable turfs plus the two or three best drive turfs. It should keep **55 hangers in reserve** and deliberately **skip about 650 list doors**: people who already voted, dormant registrants, mixed-support households, and the lower-ranked drive turfs. Two hard limits force this split. The committee has about **52 volunteer-hours** (13 people × 2 hours × 2 weekends) and **1,500 hangers** for 2,102 doors. Knocking every door reaches only about 500–900 doors. Hanging every door uses up the hangers in about 16 shifts and leaves ten shifts idle. The hybrid uses both resources fully, and across the plausible range of hanger effects it has the smallest worst case. The evidence works like this. A face-to-face conversation raises a contacted voter's turnout by roughly **2.5–3 points** in high-salience elections, but only **25–30% of doors answer**. A hanger alone yields anywhere from **zero to about 12 extra voters per 1,000 doors**, depending on the study. Who to prioritize follows from turnout. Dane County turned out **80% of registered voters in the 2022 midterm**, so reliable voters will vote anyway, and the voters a volunteer can actually move are low- and mid-propensity supporters. By my own derived arithmetic, the whole door program yields about **4–20 extra voters and 3–12 net yes votes**. That is decisive only in a close race. A close race is more plausible than in 2022, because this year's ask is **$118.5M over four years**, against a 2022 question that passed with 62.9%. The cheapest extra lever is personal texting by volunteers. One caveat covers everything that follows. The research team could not open the full text of any source, so every figure comes from search-engine extracts. The report labels each figure as primary-source, secondhand, derived or assumed, and the last section collects those labels in one table.

## Fifty-two volunteer-hours and 1,500 hangers make a hybrid the safest bet

Experiments consistently find face-to-face conversation the most reliable get-out-the-vote contact, but it costs a lot of time. Green, McGrath & Aronow's meta-analysis of US experiments puts the precision-weighted canvassing effect at **about 2.5 percentage points per voter actually contacted**, against 0.78 points in Europe. That figure is secondhand, taken from Townsley's summary table rather than the meta-analysis itself ([Townsley 2018](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/political-science-research-and-methods/article/is-it-worth-doorknocking-evidence-from-a-united-kingdombased-get-out-the-vote-gotv-field-experiment-on-the-effect-of-party-leaflets-and-canvass-visits-on-voter-turnout/757F1AF7D16F43A444BDDE55181471B5)). The newest meta-analysis that splits results by election salience finds canvassing averages **4.58 points per contact in low-salience elections but 3.05 in high-salience ones**. It also finds every tactic loses roughly **33–76%** of its effect as salience rises ([Mann & Haenschen 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0261379423001518)). A November midterm with an open governor's race belongs in the high-salience group. Note that a hosted copy of that paper shows a 23% drop for canvassing rather than 33%, so the versions disagree ([VoteAmerica copy](https://research.voteamerica.org/a-meta-analysis-of-voter-mobilization-tactics-by-electoral-salience/)). Older benchmarks look better because they come from sleepy elections. Gerber & Green's 1998 New Haven experiment found **6.3 points per contact, about one vote per 16 conversations** ([Gerber & Green 1999](https://www.uvm.edu/~dguber/POLS234/articles/gerber.pdf)). The 2001 six-city study found **+7.1 points among people reached** but only +2.1 points per targeted voter, because canvassers reached just **14–45%** of households ([J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/getting-out-vote-local-elections-united-states)). Canvassing does not always work: about **20% of the 71 canvass experiments** in the meta-analysis produced negative estimates ([Bhatti et al. supplement](https://static.cambridge.org/content/id/urn:cambridge.org:id:article:S0007123416000521/resource/name/S0007123416000521sup001.pdf)).

Door hangers make the opposite trade: almost no time, a weaker effect. The closest match to this election is the Michigan Democratic Party's 2002 experiment, run during a midterm governor's race. Hangers raised turnout **1.2 points** in Nickerson's write-up ([Nickerson 2005](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/anname/v601y2005i1p10-27.html)). The journal version reports **1.6 points (SE 0.7), or about 12 extra votes per 1,000 hangers** ([Nickerson, Friedrichs & King](https://sites.temple.edu/nickerson/files/2017/07/Nickerson_Friedrichs_King.PRQ2005.pdf)). The authors concluded that hangers, volunteer phone calls and visits had **"similar cost-effectiveness"** ([HKS working paper](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ecl/harjfk/rwp04-018.html)). Pooled results are weaker. Green & Gerber's 2004 benchmark is one extra vote per 200 people leafleted, a secondhand figure from a news report on the book ([Chronicle of Philanthropy](https://www.philanthropy.com/news/cost-of-door-to-door-canvassing-is-19-per-vote-research-finds/)). The meta-analytic estimate for partisan leaflets and mail is **−0.2 points, not significant**, also secondhand via Townsley ([Townsley 2018](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/political-science-research-and-methods/article/is-it-worth-doorknocking-evidence-from-a-united-kingdombased-get-out-the-vote-gotv-field-experiment-on-the-effect-of-party-leaflets-and-canvass-visits-on-voter-turnout/757F1AF7D16F43A444BDDE55181471B5)). Evidence on combining the two is thin and comes from abroad. In a UK experiment, a canvass visit after a leaflet added only **0.6 points (not significant)** on top of the leaflet's 4.3. That hints that a household reached once gains little from a second touch of the same kind ([Townsley 2018](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/political-science-research-and-methods/article/is-it-worth-doorknocking-evidence-from-a-united-kingdombased-get-out-the-vote-gotv-field-experiment-on-the-effect-of-party-leaflets-and-canvass-visits-on-voter-turnout/757F1AF7D16F43A444BDDE55181471B5)). No US experiment has compared knock-only, hang-only and knock-plus-hang head to head.

These effects run into the committee's two limits. Twenty-six two-hour shifts can knock only about **730 doors** at a realistic 28 per shift. They could hang roughly **2,500 doors**, but there are only 1,500 hangers. The table runs each strategy through three hanger-effect scenarios drawn from the studies above. Its assumptions are mine and are labeled derived. A shift covers 28 knock doors or 95 walkable hang doors, the midpoints of the planning ranges in the timing-and-turf section. The contact rate is 27.5%, with 1.6 target voters per door. A conversation is worth 3.05 points, plus Nickerson's 60% spillover to housemates, or about one extra voter per 24 conversations. Hangers yield 0, 8 or 12 extra voters per 1,000 doors.

| Strategy (26 two-hour shifts) | Doors reached | Hangers used | Extra voters if hangers do nothing (meta-analytic null) | …if hangers match Green & Gerber's 1 vote per 200 people | …if hangers match Michigan 2002 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Knock every door, hanger at each | ~730 | ~730 | ~8 | ~13 | ~15 |
| Hang only, stop when hangers run out (~16 shifts) | 1,500 | 1,500 | 0 | ~12 | ~18 |
| **Hybrid: ~365 knocked + ~1,080 hung, 55 in reserve** | **~1,445** | **1,500** | **~4** | **~15** | **~20** |

The hybrid wins in two of the three scenarios. In the third, where hangers turn out to be worthless, it trails knocking everything by about four voters, the smallest worst case of any option. The logic gives a working rule. **Hangers cap how many doors the committee can cover, so any volunteer time beyond what hanging 1,500 doors needs should go into knocking.** At the midpoint pace that trade settles at about 400 knock doors. My plan uses ~365 because drive-turf doors take longer per door. If volunteers move faster or slower than the midpoint, the right number ranges from roughly 165 to 660, which is why weekend one should be measured and weekend two re-planned. The list holds likely supporters, so the expected net yes from each extra voter is 2*s* − 1. At an assumed support rate of *s* = 0.8 that is 0.6, so the hybrid nets **roughly 3–12 yes votes** (derived). Michigan 2002 also gives volunteers who cannot walk something to do. Volunteer phone calls in the same experiment raised turnout **3.2 points** ([Nickerson 2005](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/anname/v601y2005i1p10-27.html)). That makes calling skipped drive-turf households a reasonable job for anyone with a phone and an hour.

## In an 80%-turnout midterm, drop-off voters are the margin

Who benefits most from a knock depends on how exciting the election is. Arceneaux & Nickerson re-analyzed 11 canvassing experiments. They found that canvassing moves only people "near the indifference threshold," and that **"low-propensity voters can be effectively mobilized only in high-turnout elections"** ([ISPS abstract](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps09-030)). A teaching summary turns this into a rule of thumb: target rare voters in highly visible races and regular voters in obscure ones ([Adam Brown summary](https://adambrown.info/p/notes/arceneaux_and_nickerson_who_is_mobilized_to_vote_a_re_analysis_of_11_field_experiments)). Enos, Fowler & Vavreck pooled 24 experiments and found two-thirds mobilized high-propensity voters more than low-propensity ones ([Enos, Fowler & Vavreck 2014](https://scholar.harvard.edu/files/renos/files/enosfowlervavreck.pdf)). Their sample, however, is dominated by low-salience elections and mail. The appendix result reported to flip that pattern once control turnout tops 50% is unverified. A secondhand blog summary of Florian Foos's 122-experiment analysis says voters with a **40–60% propensity** respond best ([Campaign Lab](https://campaignlab.uk/project/blog-the-truth-about-voter-turnout-what-can-we-actually-learn-from-over-100-voter-turnout-experiments/)). This November is about as high-salience as local elections get. Dane County cast **302,459 ballots on 376,531 registrations in 2022, or 80.3%** ([Gigafact/Wisconsin Watch](https://gigafact.org/fact-briefs/did-dane-county-have-88-voter-turnout-in-2022-tied-with-the-2018-midterms)). The threshold model therefore puts the people a volunteer can move among irregular supporters, not habitual ones. That is an extrapolation, because no experiment had baseline turnout that high.

The best irregular voters to target are midterm drop-offs. My derived comparison of Dane's 2022 ballots with its roughly 365,800 presidential votes in 2024 shows about **17% fewer ballots in the midterm** ([Verona Press](https://www.veronapress.com/news/verona-dane-county-election-results/article_81c1b0bc-9dd9-11ef-8da8-7b6142df701a.html)). Pew's validated-voter study found that 2018 voters who skipped 2022 had favored Democrats **64%–33%** ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2023/07/12/republican-gains-in-2022-midterms-driven-mostly-by-turnout-advantage/)). In Dane County that lean probably goes along with yes votes on school funding, though no one has measured it for MCPASD. Turnout scores can mislead for new registrants whose vote history is thin ([States Win white paper](https://stateswin.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Postcard-Postmark-Whitepaper.pdf)). For that reason the bands below use plain vote history, which volunteers can read in MiniVAN, rather than WEAC's modeled score, whose cut points are not public.

| Band (VAN vote history) | Definition | Action |
|---|---|---|
| A: drop-off | Voted Nov 2020 and/or Nov 2024, but not Nov 2022; or registered since 2022 with only a presidential-general vote | Knock first, especially doors with two or more |
| B: occasional | Voted in 1–2 of the last four November generals, including 2022 | Knock after A |
| C: reliable | Voted Nov 2022 and Nov 2024, plus most other recent elections | Hang only; never knock |
| D: dormant | No vote in 4+ years | Skip (likely moved) |
| Already voted | MiniVAN "Voted" icon or early/absentee flag | Skip; re-pull lists every 1–3 days |
| Mail ballot not returned | Requested but not returned, per VAN or Badger Voters | Ballot-chase message: hand-deliver it, because the clerk must receive it by 8 p.m. Nov 3 |

Household size matters as much as vote history. In Nickerson's two-voter-household experiment, the person who answered the door gained **9.8 points** and the housemate who didn't gained **6.0**. In other words, **about 60% of the effect passes to the other voter** ([Nickerson 2008](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/abs/is-voting-contagious-evidence-from-two-field-experiments/8C2E64552D946C87FD062DD2CCD9054E); [Adam Brown summary](https://adambrown.info/p/notes/nickerson_is_voting_contagious_evidence_from_two_field_experiments)). A door with two A/B voters is therefore worth about 1.6 single-voter doors. Effects do not spread across households: a 70,000-person Chicago study found within-household effects but no spillover between homes, according to a secondhand summary ([ISPS](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps12-025)). Knocking non-list neighbors therefore mobilizes nobody on the list. Spillover cuts both ways. Foos & de Rooij found it was **strongest in politically divided households** ([AJPS](https://ajps.org/2016/09/08/all-in-the-family/)), so a "Vote YES" knock at a home with a known opponent can turn out a no vote. The net-vote arithmetic makes the targeting stakes concrete (derived). An extra voter who matches the 2022 district average (*s* = 0.629) is worth only **+0.26 net yes**. A likely supporter (*s* = 0.8) is worth **+0.6**. A toss-up voter is worth nothing. A lean-no voter costs votes. Blanket coverage of mixed blocks is therefore a poor use of hangers.

Early voting thins the list for free, provided the committee keeps re-pulling it. In Dane County in 2022, **110,836 absentee ballots, 48,264 of them cast in person**, were back by the eve of the election ([Channel 3000](https://www.channel3000.com/more-than-110000-people-in-dane-co-have-already-voted-ahead-of-election-day)). That is about **36.6% of all ballots** (derived). A "needs a nudge" list will vote early at a lower rate, but a meaningful share of it will drop off by October 31. NGP VAN's GOTV guide says list pulls can **exclude early and absentee voters with a checkbox**, and that MiniVAN shows a "Voted" icon. How often those flags update depends on the instance administrator ([NGP VAN](https://ngpvan.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/GOTV-with-MiniVAN-OBP.pdf)). If the WEAC instance does not carry absentee status, Badger Voters sells it at **$25 plus $5 per 1,000 records** with no restrictions on use, roughly $36 for this list (derived) ([Badger Voters FAQ](https://badgervoters.wi.gov/faq)). The school-election research points the same way. Kogan, Lavertu & Peskowitz matched more than 10,000 school tax referenda to voter files and found that election timing mainly changes the electorate's **age**, with the elderly most overrepresented in low-turnout elections ([Kogan et al. 2018](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/amposc/v62y2018i3p637-651.html)). Wisconsin school referenda passed at **77.5% in fall 2024 against 60.2% in spring** ([Wisconsin Policy Forum](https://wispolicyforum.org/research/wisconsin-sets-more-referenda-records-to-fund-schools/)). Most of the turnout boost comes from the November date itself. The committee's marginal contacts should add the younger, presidential-only voters who lean yes.

## Hang in the morning, knock in the afternoon, finish before Sunday dark

No peer-reviewed source reports how many doors per hour volunteers cover, so the pace figures come from vendors and practitioners. Qomon puts knock-and-talk at **15–20 doors per hour ideally, 10–15 once travel and breaks are counted**. Suburban streets with driveways run 15–18 and rural areas 8–12 ([Qomon](https://qomon.com/resources/blog/article/canvassing-doors-per-hour)). Indivisible's MiniVAN guide says an average pair knocks **40–45 doors in a 2–2.5-hour shift** ([Indivisible](https://indivisible.org/resource/minivan/)). NGP VAN recommends **30–50 doors per packet**, no more than about a block between doors, and no rivers, highways or other barriers inside a turf ([NGP VAN turf-cutting guide](https://www.ngpvan.com/wp-content/uploads/Turf-Cutting-OBP.pdf)). The only lit-drop benchmark is a rule of thumb: a volunteer can hit **"3–4 times as many homes"** as when knocking ([Local Victory](https://www.localvictory.com/grassroots/door-to-door-campaign-guide.html)). My planning figures, all assumptions, follow from those. In a two-hour shift with 15 minutes of check-in, a volunteer covers **20–35 knock doors**, **75–120 walkable hang doors**, or **50–80 drive-and-drop doors**. The existing turfs average **about 47 doors** (2,102 ÷ 45, derived). A walkable hang turf is therefore about half a shift, so give each hang volunteer two. A knock turf is about one shift for a pair working opposite sides of the street.

Contact rates set how many conversations a knock actually buys. In the six-city study, canvassers reached **28.1% of households in Bridgeport, 14.3% in Columbus, 30.9% in Detroit, 18.5% in Minneapolis and 44.6% in Raleigh** ([Green, Gerber & Nickerson 2003](https://sites.temple.edu/nickerson/files/2017/07/Green_Gerber_Nickerson.JOP_.2003.pdf)). Green & Gerber warn that a single pass can reach as few as **one voter in six doors**, and that getting above 25% usually takes repeat visits ([Get Out the Vote](https://groups.io/g/psychologyonline/attachment/219/0/Get%20out%20the%20vote%20%20how%20to%20increase%20voter%20turnout.pdf)). The only large time-of-day dataset comes from commercial sales, not politics: about 237,000 knocks. In it, answer rates climb from **31.5% in the 9 a.m. hour to 46.2% at 8 p.m.**, and **Saturday is the weakest day at 34.7%** ([Scoutdata](https://scoutdata.com/guides/door-knocking-statistics)). The practical upshot is to put hang-only shifts in the morning, when answer rates are lowest and little is lost by not knocking, and save late afternoon for knocking. Daylight caps the afternoon. Sunset in Madison is **6:05 p.m. on Oct 22 and 5:51 p.m. on Oct 31** ([General Blue](https://www.generalblue.com/sunrise-sunset-in-madison-wisconsin-usa)). Clocks fall back at 2 a.m. on Sunday Nov 1, which puts that day's sunset near **4:50 p.m. CST**, a figure I interpolated. October normals are a **59.6°F high and 39.8°F low** ([UW State Climatology Office](https://climatology.nelson.wisc.edu/first-order-station-climate-data/madison-climate)). Rain has fallen on about **40%** of recorded Nov 1 dates ([WeatherOnThisDay](https://weatheronthisday.com/53701/11/1)), so pack hangers in plastic sleeves.

The timing evidence favors contact close to the vote but does not settle it. Nickerson's 2007 phone study found final-week calls most effective, a finding reported secondhand. Panagopoulos's 2011 test found **no significant difference** among calls four weeks, two weeks and three days out, with a pooled effect of only about 0.9 points ([Adam Brown summary](https://adambrown.info/p/notes/panagopoulos_timing_is_everything_primacy_and_recency_effects_in_voter_mobilization_campaigns)). Kalla & Broockman find that persuasion from early contact **decays** before Election Day ([eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt103775sx/qt103775sx.pdf)). Every planned shift falls inside both the early-voting window (**Tue Oct 20 – Sun Nov 1**) and the last two weeks before Nov 3. Same-day registration is available at the polls, and in-person registration at the clerk runs through **Fri Oct 30 at 5 p.m.** ([FOX6](https://www.fox6now.com/news/wisconsin-election-voter-deadlines-november-2026)). That supports a split. Weekend one should **front-load most hang-only turfs**, so the hanger's early-voting information is in hand for the whole window and responders drop off weekend two's lists. **Knock turfs split across both weekends**, with weekend two aimed at A-band doors that still have not voted. Panagopoulos's unverified hint that low-propensity voters respond better to later contact points the same way.

Route design and a few legal rules prevent wasted minutes and bad press. Pairs splitting the two sides of a street keep most of the safety benefit at close to solo pace. One guide reports two people covering about 100 homes on a 10-block walk that way ([SC Women Lead](https://scwomenlead.net/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Guide-to-Canvassing.pdf)). Loops that start and end at the car, and cul-de-sacs worked by one partner while the other continues along the through street, are standard practice, not tested findings. Locked apartment buildings should be marked no-access unless management agrees in advance ([SC Women Lead safety guide](https://scwomenlead.net/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Strategies-for-Safety-While-Canvassing.pdf)). **Nothing goes in a mailbox.** 18 U.S.C. § 1725 bars unstamped matter in letterboxes, and secondary sources say *USPS v. Council of Greenburgh* (1981) upheld it. The statute's penalty figures conflict across sources and should be checked at the [LII text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1725) ([LegallyExplained](https://legallyexplained.com/is-it-illegal-to-put-flyers-in-mailboxes/)). In *Watchtower v. Stratton* the Supreme Court struck down permit requirements for door-to-door advocacy, and it pointed to residents' **"No Solicitation" signs and their right to refuse** as the proper protection ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/536/150)). Skipping posted doors is legally prudent, and it is cheap insurance for a school campaign's reputation.

## Neighbors, voting plans and accurate facts beat clever copy

Who delivers the contact matters more than its wording. Sinclair, McConnell & Michelson found that canvassers from the voter's own neighborhood were more effective. Their exact effect sizes are unverified ([Political Communication 2013](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10584609.2012.737413)). MoveOn's 2004 neighbor-to-neighbor canvass raised turnout among contacted voters by **about 9 points**, even in a high-salience battleground election, though a 2007 report of the working paper said about 7% ([Middleton & Green 2008](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps08-005)). Michelson's Fresno work shows the shared-identity advantage works mainly by **getting more doors opened** ([Social Science Quarterly](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/socsci/v87y2006i5p1188-1206.html)). That is a direct argument for putting local parents on the knock turfs and newcomers on the hang turfs. On delivery, the evidence (mostly from phone studies) favors a natural, unhurried conversation over a memorized script. The one strongly supported script element is a **voting plan**. Nickerson & Rogers's plan-making call raised turnout **4.1 points** among those contacted, and **9.1 points in single-voter households**. It was a phone study and is largely unreplicated ([Psychological Science 2010](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797609359326)).

The printed hanger is mostly fixed, but a pen can still add the cheap, low-risk features the research supports. **Gratitude** framing has the best mail evidence. Pre-election postcards thanking people for past voting raised turnout **2.4–3.1 points** across three experiments; those figures come from secondhand summaries ([ISPS](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps11-017)). **High-turnout norms** ("most of your neighbors vote") beat low-turnout framing, but only on self-reported intention ([Gerber & Rogers 2009](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps09-33)). Cards that are partly preprinted, with a **handwritten closing and signature**, performed as well as fully handwritten ones ([Blue Wave Analytics](https://actionnetwork.org/user_files/user_files/000/037/479/original/Blue_Wave_Analytics_Postcard_Best_Practices_Data_and_Analysis.pdf)). Expect small effects from handwriting in big elections: Vote Forward's letters fell to about **0.2 points among low-propensity voters in 2024** ([Vote Forward](https://votefwd.org/blog/vote-forwards-2024-results-show-that-to-win-elections-its-time-to-rethink-voter-outreach-based-on-research-amp-transparency)). Never mention early voting alone. In a 7.1-million-voter texting experiment, **early-in-person-only messages added 0.04 points (not significant)**, against 0.32 for messages that covered Election Day plus early voting. Early-only messages "just prompted the same number of voters to vote earlier" ([Haenschen, Mann & DeMora 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11109-026-10121-2); [Northeastern News](https://news.northeastern.edu/2026/08/11/text-messages-get-out-the-vote/)). Skip social pressure. Mailers showing neighbors' voting records raised turnout **8.1 points** in a 2006 Michigan primary ([J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/social-pressure-and-voter-turnout-united-states)), but they drew documented backlash in the **2012 Wisconsin recall** ([Rogers et al.](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/social-pressure-and-voting-field-experiment-conducted-high-salience-election)) and in Colorado in 2019 ([Colorado Sun](https://coloradosun.com/2019/11/13/social-pressure-mailers-politics-coloradans-for-prosperity/)).

No one has run a randomized turnout or persuasion experiment on a US school levy, bond or operating referendum, so the school-specific guidance rests on neighboring evidence. Kalla & Broockman find campaign contact has **zero average persuasive effect in candidate general elections** ([APSR](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/abs/minimal-persuasive-effects-of-campaign-contact-in-general-elections-evidence-from-49-field-experiments/753665A313C4AB433DBF7110299B7433)). The research notes disagree on whether the abstract itself names ballot measures as an exception, so treat that exception as unconfirmed. Precinct-randomized voter-guide mail in Oregon did move **how people voted on initiatives, not whether they voted**, and may have flipped two outcomes ([Rogers & Middleton](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/are-ballot-initiative-outcomes-influenced-campaigns-independent-groups-precinct)). Persuasion can also backfire. A canvass of **56,000 Wisconsin voters** in 2008 "possibly reduced candidate support and almost certainly did not increase it" ([Bailey, Hopkins & Rogers](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/unresponsive-and-unpersuaded-unintended-consequences-voter-persuasion-efforts)). Volunteers should therefore thank no voters and move on rather than argue. Message framing matters: adding property-tax information to school bond questions lowered support **6–9 points** in a survey experiment, a secondhand figure ([Brunner et al. summary](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/421821)). That argues for leading with what the money does. Accuracy limits how far the "renewal" framing can go, though. The 2026 question totals **$118.5M over four years**. It starts at the current $24.9M and rises to **$34.6M**, and it is the state's second-largest November ask ([Wisconsin Examiner](https://wisconsinexaminer.com/2026/09/21/middleton-referendum-discussion-highlights-tension-between-property-taxes-and-school-funding/)). WKOW put the cost at about **$237 over four years per $100,000** of property value ([WKOW](https://www.wkow.com/news/middleton-cross-plains-school-district-asks-taxpayers-for-more-money-in-november-referendum/article_a1c6ba6d-441d-4075-bc4f-e46c4110d69e.html)). "Renewal" is accurate for year one only, so volunteers must not say "no tax increase."

Recent Wisconsin results show why a few votes can matter. In April 2026, **46 of 75** school questions passed, time-limited operating requests passed only **32 of 56**, and **11 questions were decided by 83 votes or fewer** ([Wisconsin Examiner](https://wisconsinexaminer.com/2026/04/09/wisconsin-schools-struggling-under-funding-system-consider-next-steps-after-referendum-results/)). MCPASD's 2022 question passed **14,592–8,606** ([Channel 3000](https://www.channel3000.com/news/politics/elections/middleton-cross-plains-area-school-district-referendum-passes/article_1d33d875-2226-504c-a797-f9ea84c6890a.html)). WEAC, an advocacy source, reports that **29 of 32** referenda its locals recommended passed in November 2025 ([WEAC](https://weac.org/more-than-90-of-weac-backed-referenda-pass/)). Wisconsin law also shapes the operation. The district may provide only factual information. Advocacy belongs to a private committee, and public resources are construed broadly enough to raise misconduct-in-office risk ([Renning, Lewis & Lacy](https://law-rll.com/referendum-advocacy-what-can-school-districts-school-district-officials-do/)). Consultants in neighboring Verona advised against letting advocacy groups use school facilities ([Verona Press](https://www.veronapress.com/archives/advocacy-policies/article_579c7a31-6968-5ff8-99ba-75c852fc2a11.html)). A committee that exceeds **$10,000** in a calendar year must file a CF-1 registration within 10 business days ([Wisconsin Ethics Commission](https://ethics.wi.gov/Resources/Campaign%20Finance%20Overview%20-%20Referendum%20Committees.pdf)).

## Personal asks fill shifts, and personal texts finish the job

Recruiting and confirming volunteers works through personal contact, not email blasts. In Sister District's experiments with Katherine Haenschen, personal contact raised **RSVPs 168% and attendance 177%** compared with email alone. A confirmation call raised **attendance 51%** compared with an email confirmation, and call-plus-text was the only arm that produced significantly more attendees. The organization itself calls both studies underpowered ([Sister District](https://sisterdistrict.com/research/personal-contact-works-better-than-just-email)). Haenschen's peer-reviewed paper confirms that **SMS recruitment raised both sign-ups and attendance** compared with email ([Interest Groups & Advocacy 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41309-022-00157-0)). A later Sister District randomization found that once Mobilize's **automated text reminder** goes out, a personal text adds nothing ([Sister District 2021](https://sisterdistrict.com/b/research/text-message-reminders-work/)). There is no trustworthy baseline no-show rate. The only figure found, that SMS confirmations lift show-up from **40–55% to 75–85%**, is a vendor marketing claim ([EZ Texting](https://www.eztexting.com/use-cases/nonprofits/political-government)). The committee should measure its own no-show rate on weekend one. For growth, Hahrie Han's field experiments show that a relational context makes people likelier to **recruit others and attend meetings** ([Han 2016](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/46EDE94DFF1B659CA1DB2D29C078B8C3)). Her book finds that chapters which give volunteers real responsibility, not just repeated asks, build more activists ([Commons Library review](https://commonslibrary.org/how-organizations-develop-activists/)). The event structure practitioners recommend is consistent: set up 15 minutes early, visit turf ahead of time, pair new volunteers with experienced ones, and debrief ([Indivisible](https://indivisible.org/resource/minivan/)).

The best follow-up is a second, personal contact with the yes voters found at the door. Second-round reminder calls raised turnout **1.2 points** among people already reached once, in a study of 139,153 people ([Gerber, Huber, Fang & Reardon 2020](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps20-16)). Earlier work found the second contact especially powerful for people who had **said they intended to vote** ([J-PAL-hosted paper](https://povertyactionlab.org/media/file-research-paper/when-does-increasing-mobilization-effort-increase-turnout-new-theory-and)). Texting after a canvass produced modest gains in Virginia in 2025 ([Center for Campaign Innovation](https://campaigninnovation.org/research/testing-text-messaging-as-a-complement-to-door-to-door-canvassing)). Friend-to-friend contact carries the largest and least certain estimates. Vote Rev's practitioner meta-analysis puts a friend's GOTV contact at **2.5–3 points**, but its methods were not visible ([Vote Rev](https://voterev.helpjuice.com/en_US/research-and-evidence-faq/questions-about-the-research-and-evidence-behind-vote-rev-and-vote-rev-action-fund-tactics)). Outvote's 2018 randomized test found **8.3 points (95% CI 1.2–15.3)** ([Schein et al.](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~blei/papers/ScheinVafaSridharVeitchQuinnMoffetBleiGreen2021.pdf)), but a 2020 replication found much smaller effects with an interval that includes zero ([Schein 2020](https://aaronschein.owlstown.net/publications/5974.pdf)). Stranger peer-to-peer texts average only **0.25 points** ([Haenschen, Mann & DeMora 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11109-026-10121-2)). The arithmetic still favors relational texting (derived). If Vote Rev's figure holds, 13 volunteers each texting 20 friends adds about **7 extra voters** for roughly six volunteer-hours, about one voter per hour. The hybrid door program, at its mid-to-high estimates, adds about 0.3–0.4 per hour. Two cautions apply. The research is weaker, and the effect shrinks if those friends are habitual Middleton voters. Volunteers should therefore text friends who vote less often: younger people, renters, and families new to town.

## Conclusion: a 13-step operating plan for the co-chair

The analysis yields two findings that are not obvious. First, **the hanger count, not the volunteer count, caps how many doors get covered**. New recruits therefore add conversations and follow-up texts, not reach, and the measure of a good shift on a knock turf is the number of conversations, not doors. Second, **the door program is insurance for a close race and a pipeline for follow-up**. Its direct yield is a few dozen voters at most. Its larger value is the confirmed yes voters it hands to personal texters, the list it shrinks as supporters vote early, and the volunteers it turns into recruiters for the final weekend. The budget below exhausts all 1,500 hangers in about 25 of 26 shifts, and the steps after it say how to run and adjust the plan.

| Bucket | Weekend 1 (Oct 24–25) | Weekend 2 (Oct 31–Nov 1) | Hangers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Knock, with a hanger at every door (top ~7 walkable turfs) | 6 shifts (3 pairs), ~170 doors | 7 shifts, ~195 doors | ~365 |
| Hang-only, walkable turfs | 6 shifts, ~570 doors | 4 shifts, ~380 doors | ~950 |
| Hang-only, top 2–3 drive turfs | 1 shift, ~65 doors | 1 shift, ~65 doors | ~130 |
| Flex (shift captain/data, or extra knocking) | — | 1 shift | 0 |
| Reserve (rain damage, Mon Nov 2, late corrections) | — | — | ~55 |
| **Total** | **13 shifts, ~805 hangers** | **13 shifts, ~640 hangers** | **1,500** |

1. **Prep the data by Thu Oct 22.** Ask the VAN administrator which models are in the instance, how often early and absentee flags update, and whether the WEAC instance's terms allow use by a referendum committee. If absentee status is missing, buy it from Badger Voters (about $36). Tag every list voter A/B/C/D by vote history, count A/B voters per household, and flag any household with a voter identified or modeled as no.
2. **Rank and label the 45 turfs.** Mark the ~7 walkable turfs with the most households holding two or more A/B voters as KNOCK, about 330 doors before skips. Mark the other 28 walkable turfs as HANG. Rank the 10 drive turfs by A/B count and plan only the top 2–3 as DRIVE-HANG.
3. **Apply these door rules.** On knock turfs, knock A/B doors and leave one hanger at each: hand it over if someone answers (it doubles as their plan card), hang it if not. Hang C-only doors without knocking. On hang and drive turfs, hang every list door. Everywhere, skip anything with a MiniVAN "Voted" icon, D-only households, households with an identified no voter, "No Soliciting" or "No Trespassing" doors, locked buildings, and all non-list doors.
4. **Follow the hanger budget above and move freed hangers down the rankings.** Every hanger saved by a "Voted" skip goes to the next-ranked drive turf. Once all 1,500 are committed, any extra volunteer time goes to knocking without a hanger, phoning skipped drive-turf households, or relational texting. A reprint makes sense only if new volunteers add several shifts and the hangers can arrive by Oct 29.
5. **Send people first to the top-ranked knock turfs and the top-ranked hang turfs.** Any volunteer free Thu–Fri Oct 22–23 should knock 4:00–6:00 p.m. or hang in pairs after dark. On weekend one, run hang waves in the morning and knock waves 3:30–5:30 p.m., following the schedule table.
6. **Re-pull every list on Fri Oct 30 with early and absentee voters excluded.** Weekend two's knock shifts go to the remaining knock turfs and to A-band doors that still have not voted. Finish Saturday before trick-or-treat hours. On Sunday Nov 1, end knocking by 4:30 p.m. and stress "vote Tuesday," because early voting closes that day.
7. **Structure every shift the same way.** Stage at a private home or park, never a school building. Allow 15 minutes for arrival, a briefing and a role-play, about 95 minutes on doors, and 10 minutes for a debrief and MiniVAN sync. Put local parents on their own neighborhoods' knock turfs as pairs splitting street sides. Put newcomers on hang turfs. Drive turfs get a driver plus a runner where possible. After dark, work in pairs only.
8. **Keep the door conversation to about two minutes.** Open with "I live on ___, my kids go to ___." Give one accurate fact: year one continues current funding and the amount rises after that, so never say "no tax increase." Then ask for a voting plan: early at the clerk by Sun Nov 1, or Nov 3 at their polling place, and what time. Thank people who are against it and move on.
9. **Hand-write on every hanger.** Add "Sorry we missed you, ___ from ___ St." and "Thank you for being a voter." Make sure the hanger shows both the early-voting dates and Nov 3, and add MyVote.wi.gov ([City of Madison Clerk](https://www.cityofmadison.com/news/2026-09-08/absentee-ballots-are-in-the-mail)). Never put a hanger in or on a mailbox. Confirm the printed "Paid for by" attribution and the committee's CF-1 status with the treasurer.
10. **Run a personal-ask recruiting sprint, Oct 19–23.** Each of the 13 calls or texts, never emails, 3–5 specific people: fellow parents, neighbors, retired staff. Staff may help only on personal time and through personal channels. Make each ask for a specific shift with a partner. Confirm every sign-up with a call plus a text 24–48 hours ahead and an automated text on the day.
11. **Measure weekend one and re-plan weekend two.** Record doors per shift, contact rate, hangers used and no-shows (confirmed minus completed). Over-book weekend two by the no-show rate you observed. Re-balance knock and hang shifts with the rule in the first section. If show-ups fall short, cut drive shifts first and protect the knock turfs, because they are the only part of the plan that pays off even if hangers do nothing. At the debrief, ask each volunteer to bring one person and give returners real roles: shift captain, data, turf.
12. **Follow up with every yes voter you identify.** At the door, record a support ID and the voter's plan, and ask permission to text a reminder. On Mon Nov 2 evening and Tue Nov 3 morning, the volunteer who met them sends a personal text restating their plan and polling place. Voters with unreturned mail ballots get a separate message: hand-deliver to the clerk, because it must arrive by 8 p.m. Nov 3.
13. **Ask every volunteer for 30 minutes of relational texting, Oct 29–Nov 2.** Each sends about 20 personal texts to less-regular voters they know, mentioning both early voting and Election Day. After Nov 3, thank volunteers personally and keep the list of identified yes voters for future campaigns. A post-election thank-you has no tested turnout effect; it is for goodwill and retention.
