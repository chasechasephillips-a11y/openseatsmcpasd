# Tax-weary Democrats thinned Middleton's 2026 referendum cushion

*A research paper on the Middleton-Cross Plains Area School District operating referendums of Nov. 8, 2022 and Nov. 3, 2026. Prepared Nov. 5, 2026. All 2026 vote figures are unofficial until the county canvass, due by Nov. 17, 2026. Bracketed numbers in citations refer to the numbered reference list at the end.*

## Abstract

The Middleton-Cross Plains Area School District (MCPASD) operating referendum of Nov. 3, 2026 passed with **60.1% (14,263 Yes, 9,457 No, unofficial)**, 2.8 points below the **62.9% (14,596 to 8,608, official)** that the Nov. 8, 2022 question won. The four-year ask grew from $65.5M to **$118.5M**, although only about **$18.9M** of it was new money above the existing $24.9M level ([Dane County 2026 [4]](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Precincts-Result/197/0040); [Dane County 2022 [2]](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Precincts-Result/145/0042)). Yes lost 333 votes and No gained 849, almost all in the City of Middleton (+261), the Madison wards (+260), the Village of Cross Plains (+203) and the Town of Middleton (+139); the three towns that voted No added no new No votes on net. Party still sorted the wards, but the relationship flattened, and Yes fell further behind the Democratic vote: from **13.7 to 17.1 points** behind the governor's race and from **11.4 to 17.4 points** behind the attorney-general rematch. That arithmetic requires that **at least 22% of Democratic-voting participants voted No** ([Dane County governor 2026 [8]](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Precincts-Result/197/0004)). Roll-off on the back-of-ballot question reached **7.2%**, concentrated in the City and Town of Middleton. Property data explain the slide in the Village of Cross Plains, which had the fastest appreciation, a 0.65 assessment ratio and typical-home school taxes up about 27% in two years, and in the small rural towns. They do not explain the City of Middleton, which had the slowest school-tax growth in the district and the most new No votes. There, the likeliest tax trigger was a revaluation that raised assessment notices by about a third weeks before the vote without changing anyone's share of the school levy ([DOR TVC data [23]](https://www.revenue.wi.gov/SLFReportscotvc/tvc25.xlsx); [City of Middleton [26]](https://www.cityofmiddleton.us/DocumentCenter/View/20472/Middleton-Revaluation-Overview)). The district's own tax figures changed from $44/$65/$64/$64 to $0/$21/$40/$59 per $100,000 between August and September, switched basis from 2022's assessed value to fair market value, and reached voters in at least four incompatible forms. Waunakee passed its operating and bond questions on the same ballot with only **52.6% and 51.7%**. Its electorate is 11 points less Democratic, which explains the lower share, but its operating support has fallen in every cycle since 2020, the trajectory that MCPASD's 2030 renewal, starting at $34.6M, must avoid. For 2030 the analyst should treat MCPASD's majority as a tax-trust coalition resting on a Democratic base: persuade the Democratic homeowners who defected, publish one dated tax table with an explicit basis and a fail scenario, close the roll-off gap, and archive the data the county does not keep.

## 1. Introduction: a familiar question at a bigger price

MCPASD has asked its voters three times in eight years to let it exceed the state revenue limit for operations. Each time it asked for more and kept a little less support. The 2018 operating question won **69.2%** ([Channel 3000, 2018 [59]](https://www.channel3000.com/news/local-news/largest-school-referendum-in-the-state-passes-in-middleton/article_3d70481f-fba4-56ed-ad87-923a4783d0cf.html)), the 2022 question **62.9%** and the 2026 question **60.1%**. The 2026 measure was built as a renewal. It re-authorizes the $24.9M that the 2022 referendum reaches in 2026-27 and then steps up to $34.6M by 2030-31, under a purpose clause identical word for word to 2022's ([What's on the Ballot, Nov. 2026 [15]](https://elections.countyofdane.com/documents/pdf/What-s-on-the-Ballot-Nov-2026.pdf); [MCPASD 2022 presentation [31]](https://clubrunner.blob.core.windows.net/00000006121/en-ca/files/homepage/m-cp-school-district-2022-referendum/Nov-2022-Referendum-M-CP-area-school-district-Presentation.pdf)). It ran in a hostile climate, with the statewide November school pass rate down from about 79% to about 57% ([WPF via WNA [78]](https://wnanews.com/?p=52191); [Civic Media, Nov. 4 [73]](https://civicmedia.us/news/2026/11/04/majority-of-school-referendums-pass-in-2026-general-election)). It also ran in a Democratic wave: David Crowley won the governorship by just over eight points and Democrats took both legislative chambers ([Wikipedia, 2026 gubernatorial election [95]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Wisconsin_gubernatorial_election)). Next door, the Waunakee Community School District put a permanent operating increase and a $97.5M bond question on the same ballot ([Waunakee CSD [35]](https://www.waunakee.k12.wi.us/community/2026referendum)).

This paper answers seven research questions. **RQ1:** How did the 2026 result compare with 2022 overall and by ward and municipality? **RQ2:** How did turnout and roll-off change? **RQ3:** How did support correlate with Democratic and Republican voting, and did the gap between Yes and the Democratic vote grow? **RQ4:** How much did property values, assessed values and school taxes rise in the communities that voted No or added No votes, including the City of Middleton revaluation and the Village of Cross Plains? **RQ5:** What is the full, sourced record of the district's tax-impact figures, and what does the move between an assessed-value basis and a market-value basis teach? **RQ6:** How did MCPASD compare with Waunakee's questions on the same ballot? **RQ7:** What should a political analyst preparing for the next referendum ask and do differently?

The argument in brief runs as follows. The 2026 slip was not partisan polarization. It was Democratic softening, concentrated in high-value Democratic wards and in the fast-appreciating Village of Cross Plains, at the same time as a Democratic wave raised turnout without raising Yes. Tax experience explains part of the pattern, but through different mechanisms in different places: rising bills in the Village and the rural towns, and a mid-campaign revaluation notice in the City. The district's tax message muddied an argument it could have won, because the referendum rate record since 2022 is better than projected. Waunakee shows what serial operating asks do to a coalition over four cycles. The stakes are concrete. The 2026 authority expires after 2030-31, the next question will start from a $34.6M ceiling, and the 4,806-vote cushion was earned against no organized opponent.

## 2. Data and methods: one county feed, 29 reporting units and a stack of caveats

### 2.1 Sources and the status of the counts

All election returns come from the Dane County Clerk. The 2022 figures are the official canvass, labeled "Official Canvass – Finalized On: 11/15/2022 2:57 PM" in the county's export ([Dane County 2022 [2]](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Precincts-Result/145/0042)). The 2026 figures are unofficial. The precinct export reads "All Precincts Reported On: 11/3/2026 11:27 PM," the county API carries a null finalized date, and the results page states that municipal and school-district results on it are unofficial ([Dane County 2026 results [3]](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Election-Result/197); [Dane County API [14]](https://api.danecounty.gov/api/v1/elections/precinctresults/197/0040)). Wisconsin county canvass boards must convene by Nov. 10 and certify by **Nov. 17, 2026** ([Campaign Legal Center [18]](https://campaignlegal.org/sites/default/files/2026-08/2026%20Wisconsin%20Certification%20Guide_Final.pdf)). Canvass corrections usually move a handful of votes, so the 4,806-vote margin is secure, but individual ward figures may shift. Which body certifies the school question in 2026 is not settled in the sources: the county disclaimer implies districts do, while in 2022 the county itself labeled this contest "Official Canvass."

Two count discrepancies in secondary material are resolved here. Some campaign notes carried the 2022 result as 14,592 to 8,606, apparently a pre-canvass count. This paper uses the official 14,596 to 8,608, so its year-over-year changes (−333 Yes, +849 No) differ by a few votes from figures circulated earlier (−329, +851). Channel 3000's election-night count of 14,248 to 9,445 is exactly the final total minus Madison Ward 95 (15 Yes, 12 No), which had not yet reported. It is a timing artifact, not a conflicting count.

Property and school-finance data come from four public layers, supplemented by municipal assessment documents and AccessDane parcel records. The Department of Public Instruction (DPI) publishes each district's levy, its equalized value with tax-increment districts excluded ("TID-out") and its mill rate ([DPI equalized tax rates [19]](https://sfs.dpi.wi.gov/safr_ro/all_mill_rate.asp?year=2026)). The Department of Revenue (DOR) publishes the MCPASD portion of each municipality's equalized value ([DOR school certification [20]](https://www.revenue.wi.gov/SLFReportsschoolspec/2026sche.pdf)), each municipality's residential value and its "economic change," which is market appreciation excluding new construction ([DOR Statement of Changes [21]](https://www.revenue.wi.gov/SLFReportsassessor/2026socdane.PDF)), the county apportionment ([DOR apportionment [22]](https://www.revenue.wi.gov/SLFReportsassessor/2026coapprt.PDF)), and assessment levels and school taxes by municipality ([DOR TVC data [23]](https://www.revenue.wi.gov/SLFReportscotvc/tvc25.xlsx)). The Dane County Treasurer's 2025 mill-rate sheet gives the school rate printed on bills in each municipality and its four-decimal assessment ratio ([Dane County Treasurer [25]](https://treasurer.danecounty.gov/documents/pdf/CurrentYear-DaneCountyMillRatesByDistrict-NOT-C-Madison.pdf)). Zillow's mid-tier home value index supplies typical home values by city and ZIP code ([Zillow ZHVI [30]](https://files.zillowstatic.com/research/public_csvs/zhvi/City_zhvi_uc_sfrcondo_tier_0.33_0.67_sm_sa_month.csv)). DOR keeps only three years of municipal reports online, and the Wayback Machine was unreachable, so no 2022 municipal-level value file could be retrieved. Municipal comparisons therefore start in 2023 for whole municipalities and in 2024 for MCPASD portions.

Messaging evidence comes from the district's 2022 presentation, an image-only PDF that was OCR'd and visually checked ([MCPASD 2022 presentation [31]](https://clubrunner.blob.core.windows.net/00000006121/en-ca/files/homepage/m-cp-school-district-2022-referendum/Nov-2022-Referendum-M-CP-area-school-district-Presentation.pdf)), from news coverage, from Waunakee's district materials and from a commercial referendum tracker. The MCPASD website sat behind a JavaScript challenge throughout the research, so the district's 2026 tax-impact page was never viewed directly; its figures are known from news reproductions. Claims known only from search-engine summaries are labeled secondhand in the text.

Two features of the feed shape the analysis. First, "ballots cast" (race 0002) counts every ballot in a reporting unit, including voters who live in another school district, so whole-unit turnout is not MCPASD turnout in split units. Second, under-votes and over-votes are published only in a fixed-width raw results file, and only for the current election ([Dane County raw results file [13]](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Election-RawResults)). That file shows **1,834 under-votes and 5 over-votes** on the MCPASD question in 2026, and its Yes and No counts match the Excel exports exactly for every unit. Because the county does not offer the raw file for past elections, 2022 roll-off can be estimated only as ballots cast minus Yes and No, and only in units wholly inside the district.

### 2.3 Matching wards across the post-2024 ward changes

MCPASD votes came from the same eight municipalities in both years, from 28 reporting units in 2022 and 29 in 2026 ([Dane County 2022 [2]](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Precincts-Result/145/0042); [Dane County 2026 [4]](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Precincts-Result/197/0040)). Units changed in four of them. The City of Middleton merged Wards 21 and 22 into neighboring units and added Ward 24. The Town of Middleton went from one unit (Wards 1–8) to four (1, 3–4; 2, 4A; 5–8; and 8A, which had no ballots). The Village of Cross Plains added Ward 7. In Madison, Wards 113, 139, 140 and 141 dropped off the list, Wards 120, 134 and 135 appeared, and Ward 118's ballots grew from 798 to 1,264. Every 2026 split falls exactly on an assembly-district line ([What's on the Ballot [15]](https://elections.countyofdane.com/documents/pdf/What-s-on-the-Ballot-Nov-2026.pdf)), which points to the legislative maps first used in 2024, though no municipal ward ordinance was checked.

Comparisons are therefore made at matched comparison units. City Wards 5–8 plus Ward 21 in 2022 are compared with 2026's combined 5–8/21 unit. City Wards 9–13/19 plus Ward 22 are compared with 2026's 9–13/19/22/24, so the 2026 figure includes the new Ward 24. The Town of Middleton is compared only as a whole, the Village with and without Ward 7, and Madison mainly as an aggregate, with its west-side wards combined (118 plus 139/140/141 in 2022; 118 plus 120/134/135 in 2026) to hold geography constant. A unit counts as fully inside the district when its 2026 ballots cast equal Yes plus No plus under- and over-votes. That status is assumed to hold in 2022 for units with the same definition.

### 2.4 Partisan baselines and split units

The partisan baseline is the Democratic two-party share, D ÷ (D + R), in the governor's race (Evers–Michels in 2022, Crowley–Tiffany in 2026), with the attorney-general race as a check. AG is the cleanest year-over-year measure, because Josh Kaul and Eric Toney faced each other in both years, while the governor's race moved from a strong incumbent to a first-time nominee ([Dane County AG 2022 [9]](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Precincts-Result/145/0005); [AG 2026 [10]](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Precincts-Result/197/0005)). Area aggregates weight each unit's partisan share by its referendum votes.

The main limitation is split units. The governor count covers the whole reporting unit, while the referendum count covers only its MCPASD slice. A unit is flagged as split when its referendum votes are under 85% of its ballots cast. The flag covers all four rural towns, where MCPASD's share of ballots runs from about 8% in Westport to about 72% in Springfield; most Madison wards, where it runs from 2% to 37%; and City of Middleton Wards 14–18. Non-split units carry **73% of 2022 and 76% of 2026 referendum votes**. Unit-level regressions of Yes share on Democratic share are weighted by referendum votes and restricted to units with at least 20 votes (24 units each year), with a non-split subset of 11 units in 2022 and 10 in 2026. Uncertainty in the slope comes from 4,000 bootstrap resamples of units.

### 2.5 Ecological inference: what ward data can and cannot say

Every partisan finding here relates ward aggregates to ward aggregates. None of it observes an individual ballot. Two tools translate aggregates into statements about voters, and both carry assumptions. The **method of bounds** asks what the aggregates require in the most favorable case. If every Republican voted No, all Yes votes came from Democrats, and the shortfall between the Yes share and the Democratic share becomes a *minimum* Democratic No rate. **Goodman ecological regression** fits Yes share against Democratic share across units and reads the fitted values at 0% and 100% Democratic as party-specific Yes rates. It assumes those rates are constant across wards, an assumption that fails if, for example, City Democrats and Town Democrats behave differently. With 10 to 24 units, Goodman estimates are illustrative only. Neither tool can separate 2022 Yes voters who switched to No from new voters who voted No or from 2022 Yes voters who skipped the question in 2026. The defection figures in this paper are bounds and model estimates, not observations.

### 2.6 Derived calculations

Several quantities are computed rather than published; all arithmetic was done in Python on the cited figures. Yes share is Yes ÷ (Yes + No). Roll-off is under-votes plus over-votes divided by ballots carrying the question in 2026, and ballots cast minus Yes and No divided by ballots cast in 2022 (fully-inside units only). A referendum's cost per $100,000 is the authority divided by TID-out equalized value, times 100,000. A typical home's gross school tax, before state credits, is its value times the DPI equalized rate, using either Zillow's January value or a 2023 value indexed by DOR's municipal economic change. A school rate printed per $1,000 of assessed value converts to a rate per $1,000 of market value when multiplied by the municipality's assessment ratio. The jump in an assessment notice caused by a revaluation is approximated as the new ratio divided by the old ratio, times appreciation. Associations between municipal value or tax growth and vote change use the eight municipalities, unweighted, with Pearson and Spearman coefficients. With eight data points, these describe patterns; they do not test hypotheses.

The renewal label was accurate in dollars and misleading in shape. Four years at the existing $24.9M account for **$99.6M of the $118.5M (84%)**, so only about **$18.9M** is new money above the current level. The district said the proposal "would continue funding authorized under the district's 2022 operational referendum while adding annual adjustments to address rising costs" ([Channel 3000, Aug. 12, 2026 [44]](https://www.channel3000.com/news/middleton-cross-plains-school-board-approves-34-6m-referendum-for-nov-3-election/article_1db2aab8-7a6b-4944-aab3-394bc0a8ac26.html)). But the authority grows 12.0%, 11.5% and 11.3% a year, not the "about 3% each year" the Wisconsin Examiner reported; by derived arithmetic, 3% matches the roughly $3M annual increments as a share of a $101M levy ([Wisconsin Examiner via Civic Media [47]](https://civicmedia.us/news/2026/09/21/middleton-referendum-discussion-highlights-tension-between-property-taxes-and-school-funding)). The final-year ceiling sits **39% above 2022's**. The Wisconsin Policy Forum ranked the measure the **seventh-largest operating referendum proposed in state history** ([Cap Times, Oct. 20 [49]](https://captimes.com/news/education/two-dane-county-school-districts-ask-voters-to-raise-property-taxes/article_56b581ca-9603-48fd-ad2e-566c6748b5bc.html)). It was the second-largest operating ask on the November ballot, behind Green Bay's $158M and ahead of Janesville's $98M, and all three were non-recurring ([WPR preview [75]](https://www.wpr.org/news/wisconsin-school-districts-referendums-nov-3-election)). Board president Bob Hesselbein said the board weighed "about six different referendum scenarios" and "didn't want to ask for more because everyone's hurting" ([Civic Media [47]](https://civicmedia.us/news/2026/09/21/middleton-referendum-discussion-highlights-tension-between-property-taxes-and-school-funding)), while conceding that "we initially hoped to ask for a higher request" ([WMTV, Oct. 22 [50]](https://www.wmtv15news.com/2026/10/22/middleton-cross-plains-voters-weigh-school-referendum-district-faces-nearly-25m-potential-cuts/)).

Measured against the state, MCPASD held. Wisconsin voters approved **45 of 79** school questions on Nov. 3, about 57% in unofficial returns ([WEAU [74]](https://www.weau.com/2026/11/04/voters-reject-34-wisconsin-school-referendums-approve-45/); [Civic Media [73]](https://civicmedia.us/news/2026/11/04/majority-of-school-referendums-pass-in-2026-general-election)), compared with **64 of 81 (79%)** in November 2022 ([WPF via WNA [78]](https://wnanews.com/?p=52191)) and 46 of 75 (61.3%) in April 2026 ([WPR, April 2026 [88]](https://www.wpr.org/politics/referendums/wisconsin-vote-75-school-referendums)). Wisconsin Watch put the spring rate below the 70% average of 2020 to 2025 ([Wisconsin Watch [80]](https://wisconsinwatch.org/2026/04/wisconsin-voters-school-district-referendum-funding-support-property-tax-election-2026/)), and the Policy Forum had already called 2024's 70.1% approval rate the lowest in a midterm or presidential year since 2014 ([WPF via WNA [79]](https://wnanews.com/?p=64407)). November 2026 thus ran *below* the spring, the reverse of the usual pattern. Only **3 of 8 recurring operating questions** passed, while limited-term operating questions in larger cities and suburbs, MCPASD among them, did better ([Civic Media [73]](https://civicmedia.us/news/2026/11/04/majority-of-school-referendums-pass-in-2026-general-election)). Green Bay's renewal squeaked through at about 51% in early returns ([WBAY [76]](https://www.wbay.com/2026/11/04/wisconsin-voters-decide-79-school-referendum-questions-including-four-northeast-wisconsin/)). Elmbrook's $100M capital question passed 61–39, and Whitefish Bay's $90M question won 63%, which FOX6 called the widest school-funding margin in southeast Wisconsin ([FOX6 [77]](https://www.fox6now.com/news/wisconsin-election-school-referendum-results)). MCPASD's 60.1% belongs in that top tier.

The district's own trend is the concern. Support has gone from 69% to 63% to 60% across three cycles, each a larger ask, in a state where the Policy Forum's Sara Shaw says "run-of-the-mill districts are going to referendum very frequently," and where pass rates have fallen from about 90% in 2018 to about 60% in spring 2026 ([Cap Times, Oct. 20 [49]](https://captimes.com/news/education/two-dane-county-school-districts-ask-voters-to-raise-property-taxes/article_56b581ca-9603-48fd-ad2e-566c6748b5bc.html)).

In each of them, the Waunakee question had lower roll-off than the MCPASD question: **6.8% against 12.8%** in Westport, **6.6% against 10.1%** in City of Middleton Wards 14–18, and 3.8% against 5.0% in Springfield ([Dane County raw results file [13]](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Election-RawResults)). ### 7.1 District-wide, the school rate fell 13% while the levy rose 27%

The levy jumped 8.4% and then 11.0% in the two years *before* this vote, coinciding with the 2022 referendum's year-3 step, the state's $325-per-pupil revenue-limit increases without new general aid, and slower value growth. And levy growth from 2022-23 to 2025-26 (+$19.6M) almost exactly equals that year's referendum authority ($19.435M), a figure any future opponent can quote.

The state backdrop explains why the levy, not state aid, absorbed rising costs. The 2022 ask followed two straight years of $0 per-pupil revenue-limit increases and 4.7% inflation, plus $6.9M in one-time COVID money that the district warned would create "a significant fiscal cliff" ([MCPASD 2022 presentation [31]](https://clubrunner.blob.core.windows.net/00000006121/en-ca/files/homepage/m-cp-school-district-2022-referendum/Nov-2022-Referendum-M-CP-area-school-district-Presentation.pdf)). By 2026, districts were receiving $325 per pupil a year, which Evers's partial veto extended through 2425 and the state Supreme Court upheld in April 2025 ([Wisconsin Examiner, Apr. 18, 2025 [89]](https://wisconsinexaminer.com/2025/04/18/wi-supreme-court-upholds-gov-evers-partial-veto-extending-school-funding-increases-for-400-years/)). The 2025-27 budget added no general aid, so that increase falls on local property taxes ([WSAA [91]](https://wsaa.org/archives/19868); [DPI Act 15 summary [92]](https://dpi.wi.gov/administrators/biweekly-mailing/summary-changes-2025-act-15-2025-27-biennial-budget)), and special-education reimbursement, budgeted at 42% for 2025-26 ([WEAC [93]](https://weac.org/state-budget-what-an-educator-needs-to-know/)), came in at a 35% interim rate ([Wisconsin Examiner, Nov. 21, 2025 [90]](https://wisconsinexaminer.com/2025/11/21/special-education-reimbursement-payments-to-schools-fall-far-below-estimate/)). That matters more in MCPASD than in most districts, because its leaders describe it as about **80% property-tax funded** and "one of the highest in Dane County for property values to students" ([Civic Media [47]](https://civicmedia.us/news/2026/09/21/middleton-referendum-discussion-highlights-tension-between-property-taxes-and-school-funding); [WKOW, Aug. 12 [46]](https://www.wkow.com/news/middleton-cross-plains-school-district-asks-taxpayers-for-more-money-in-november-referendum/article_a1c6ba6d-441d-4075-bc4f-e46c4110d69e.html)).

### 7.5 The City of Middleton: slowest tax growth, a notice shock mid-campaign

The City cuts against a simple "bigger bills, more No votes" story. Its aggregate MCPASD school tax rose only **6.9%** from the 2024 to the 2025 bills, the slowest in the district, and DOR measured its homes as appreciating least among the large units (+17.8% from 2023 to 2026). Yet it added the most No votes. What the City had that others did not was a revaluation timed into the campaign. The City "contracted with Associated Appraisal Consultants (AAC) to perform a revaluation of all property for the 2026 assessment cycle," with "the purpose... to bring all assessed values to 100% market value" ([City of Middleton Revaluation Overview [26]](https://www.cityofmiddleton.us/DocumentCenter/View/20472/Middleton-Revaluation-Overview)). The City's notice, known only secondhand because the page now returns an error, reported that total assessed value rose about 32% and stated that revaluation "redistributes how the tax levy is shared among properties; it does not, by itself, generate additional tax revenue." Board of Review met Sept. 22, 2026, six weeks before the vote ([City of Middleton notice, secondhand [27]](https://cityofmiddleton.us/CivicSend/ViewMessage/message/297551)); a City parcel on AccessDane carries a 2026 valuation date of Sept. 21 ([AccessDane [28]](https://accessdane.danecounty.gov/070803454202)).

### 7.6 The Village of Cross Plains: every measure lines up

The Village is the one place where every tax-related measure points the same way as the vote. It had the fastest residential appreciation in the district (**+28.1%** by DOR's index from 2023 to 2026; +38.5% in residential value including new construction), the second-fastest growth in its aggregate MCPASD school tax (+17.6% from the 2024 to the 2025 bills), and a typical-home school tax up about **27.5%** from the 2023 to the 2025 bills by the DOR index, or about 16% from 2022 to 2025 by Zillow ([DOR [21]](https://www.revenue.wi.gov/SLFReportsassessor/2026socdane.PDF); [DOR TVC [23]](https://www.revenue.wi.gov/SLFReportscotvc/tvc25.xlsx)). Its assessment ratio fell from 0.81 to 0.65 in two years, so its bills printed the highest school rate in the district, **13.25 per $1,000 of assessed value** against 8.9–9.2 in the revalued towns ([Dane County Treasurer [25]](https://treasurer.danecounty.gov/documents/pdf/CurrentYear-DaneCountyMillRatesByDistrict-NOT-C-Madison.pdf)). The Village's 2026 roll carried a July 22 valuation date with Open Book starting Aug. 3, 2026, a late schedule that fits a revaluation but does not prove one ([AccessDane [28]](https://accessdane.danecounty.gov/070702190011)). If the Village did revalue from 0.65 to market, its notices would have jumped about 53% for an unchanged home. Its No votes rose 22%, and its core fell to 51.8% Yes, the closest large unit in the district.

### 7.7 The No-voting towns and the Madison wards

The three towns that voted No did see above-average school-tax growth. Springfield's aggregate MCPASD tax rose **23.5%** in a single year after DOR estimated a 17.9% residential gain and the town revalued; its typical-home school tax rose about 30.5% from 2023 to 2025. The Town of Cross Plains rose 16.6% and Berry 13.6% in that year, with typical-home increases of about 24% and 26% since 2023 ([DOR TVC [23]](https://www.revenue.wi.gov/SLFReportscotvc/tvc25.xlsx)). Springfield's and the Town of Cross Plains's revaluations implied notice jumps of more than 50%, and Berry, never revalued in this period, printed a 12.94 school rate on its bills. Yet those towns added almost no No votes (−11, −1, +8); their Yes shares fell because Yes voters dropped out. DOR then *cut* Springfield's and Berry's 2026 values, so their shares of December 2026 school taxes will fall. On the other side of the district, the Madison wards added nearly as many No votes (+260, +21%) on middling value and tax growth (+20.2% city appreciation; +13.5% MCPASD-portion school tax), with annual market-level assessment and therefore no notice shock. Value and tax growth do not explain Madison well, and ward renumbering and split-unit geography make its decline the hardest to interpret.

### 8.2 What each set means

The final $0/$21/$40/$59 figures are **levels above today's bill**, not year-over-year steps. The new authority above $24.9M is $3.0M, $6.2M and $9.7M in years two through four; dividing those amounts by $21, $40 and $59 per $100K implies equalized values of about $14.3B, $15.5B and $16.4B, roughly 7% annual growth from the 2025 base, which is consistent with recent history (derived). Had the figures been steps, year four would sit $120 per $100K above today, and financing a $9.7M increment at that rate would require an equalized value of only about $8B, below today's base. On a $500,000 home, the referendum therefore costs nothing extra in year one, about $295 a year by year four, and about $600 in total over the four years. The district said it presented per-$100K figures "so individuals could do the math easily depending on the cost of the house" ([Civic Media [47]](https://civicmedia.us/news/2026/09/21/middleton-referendum-discussion-highlights-tension-between-property-taxes-and-school-funding)).

The August $44/$65/$64/$64 set cannot be a referendum-only figure, because it charges $44 in a year when the referendum amount is flat. It most likely folded in other levy growth, such as the $325-per-pupil revenue-limit increase, which NBC15 later noted the final figures excluded along with "property valuation changes" ([WMTV [50]](https://www.wmtv15news.com/2026/10/22/middleton-cross-plains-voters-weigh-school-referendum-district-faces-nearly-25m-potential-cuts/)). That reading is unconfirmed, because the district's page and its explanation of the change were never available to this research. The switch happened between the Aug. 12 release, still unchanged when Channel 3000 edited its story on Sept. 3, and the Sept. 17 information session. None of the outlets noted that the numbers had changed, and a commercial tracker kept publishing the superseded set into late October ([BadgerHomeowner [41]](https://www.badgerhomeowner.com/school-referendums-dane-county/)). NBC15's "$600 more annually by the fourth year" doubles the year-four figure. The Cap Times' "$120 per $100,000... over the next four years" is correct but sat beside Waunakee's "$210 per year" on a $500,000 home, inviting readers to compare a four-year cumulative figure per $100K with an annual figure per home ([Cap Times, Oct. 20 [49]](https://captimes.com/news/education/two-dane-county-school-districts-ask-voters-to-raise-property-taxes/article_56b581ca-9603-48fd-ad2e-566c6748b5bc.html)). Expressed on the same basis, MCPASD's year-four cost was the *higher* of the two: $59 per $100K a year against Waunakee's $42, or about $295 against $210 on a $500,000 home.

The skeptics' questions at the Sept. 17 session, attended by about 20 people, show how these numbers landed. A retiree asked, "Who has a $100,000 house in Middleton?... I'd rather see the average house." A realtor and parent wanted "a side-by-side comparison of budgets, one of if the referendum passes and one if it fails" ([Civic Media [47]](https://civicmedia.us/news/2026/09/21/middleton-referendum-discussion-highlights-tension-between-property-taxes-and-school-funding)). The district's survey of non-supporters had already flagged "property tax fatigue," a "preference for structural or state-level solutions," and a desire for "greater fiscal discipline, and greater transparency" ([MCPASD board recap, secondhand [32]](https://www.mcpasd.k12.wi.us/o/mcpasd/article/2975849)).

### 8.3 The counterfactual nobody stated

"No added cost in year one" is accurate relative to the 2026-27 bill. Its mirror image went unmentioned. Had the question failed, the $24.9M would have dropped out of the 2027-28 levy limit. By derived arithmetic, that is about **$212 per $100K at 2025 equalized value, or about $191 at 2026 value**, which is roughly **$950–$1,060 a year on a $500,000 home**, as a tax *cut*. The district framed failure as about $24.9M in cuts, roughly 20% of the operating budget, which Hesselbein called "catastrophic" and said "could lead to the closure of an elementary school" ([Cap Times, Oct. 20 [49]](https://captimes.com/news/education/two-dane-county-school-districts-ask-voters-to-raise-property-taxes/article_56b581ca-9603-48fd-ad2e-566c6748b5bc.html); [WMTV [50]](https://www.wmtv15news.com/2026/10/22/middleton-cross-plains-voters-weigh-school-referendum-district-faces-nearly-25m-potential-cuts/)). An organized opposition with a budget would have led with the household saving. WPR's 2020 reporting on Wausau notes that a failed replacement referendum lets taxes fall, which makes any retry a visible tax increase ([WPR, Nov. 2020 [86]](https://www.wpr.org/wisconsin-voters-approved-84-percent-school-referendums-election)).

### 8.4 Assessed versus market value: the basis is part of the number

The basis of "per $100,000" changed between the two campaigns, and no source found explains the change to voters. The 2022 deck's tax slide was titled "Tax Impact: $24.9 million, non-recurring, 4-year period, **$100,000 assessed value**" ([MCPASD 2022 presentation [31]](https://clubrunner.blob.core.windows.net/00000006121/en-ca/files/homepage/m-cp-school-district-2022-referendum/Nov-2022-Referendum-M-CP-area-school-district-Presentation.pdf)). The 2026 release spoke of "a property with a **fair market value** of $100,000," and NBC15 quoted "a home with a fair market value of $500,000" ([Channel 3000, Aug. 12 [44]](https://www.channel3000.com/news/middleton-cross-plains-school-board-approves-34-6m-referendum-for-nov-3-election/article_1db2aab8-7a6b-4944-aab3-394bc0a8ac26.html); [WMTV [50]](https://www.wmtv15news.com/2026/10/22/middleton-cross-plains-voters-weigh-school-referendum-district-faces-nearly-25m-potential-cuts/)). Other outlets used vaguer phrases: "$100,000 worth of property," "$100,000 of property value" and "a $100,000 home."

The 2026 wording is the technically correct one, because school levies are computed on equalized (market) value. By derived arithmetic, the 2022 steps also match the referendum amounts divided by *equalized* value ($53 and $40 per $100K in years two and three, against claims of $53 and $39), which suggests the 2022 "assessed value" label was shorthand rather than the actual basis. The difference matters in a district where ratios ranged from 0.65 to 0.98. A City of Middleton owner who applied a market-value figure to the assessed value on a pre-revaluation bill (78% of market) would have understated the cost by about 22%; in the Village of Cross Plains (65%) the understatement would be about 35%. The correct base was the bill's "estimated fair market value" line, which DOR describes as "calculated by dividing the total assessed value by the average assessment ratio," while noting that "property taxes are levied on the total assessed value" ([DOR PB-060 [24]](https://www.revenue.wi.gov/pubs/slf/pb060.pdf)). After the City's 2026 revaluation, assessed value roughly equals market value there, so the two bases coincide for City owners for the first time in years. They still do not coincide in the Village or Berry.

The lesson for 2030 is that a per-$100K figure is only half a number. Without a stated basis, a stated baseline and a stated home, it invites three errors: applying it to the wrong value, reading levels as steps, and comparing it with another district's per-home figure. The same figure also hides that the City's typical home pays the most dollars. At Zillow's September 2026 values, the year-four referendum cost works out to about **$362 a year** on a typical City of Middleton home ($614,311) and about $301 on a typical Village home ($510,868) (derived from [Zillow [30]](https://files.zillowstatic.com/research/public_csvs/zhvi/City_zhvi_uc_sfrcondo_tier_0.33_0.67_sm_sa_month.csv)).

### 9.4 Lower roll-off, lower stakes and softer tax numbers

Waunakee's question was completed at least as fully as MCPASD's. In the Village, school-question roll-off (3.1–3.2%) equaled that on the three statewide questions (3.0–3.6%), so roll-off did not drive its narrow margin ([raw results file [13]](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Election-RawResults)). Its messaging differed from MCPASD's in three ways. First, it lowered the stakes. The FAQ said the board "has not created a budget reduction list" and, if the operating question failed, would "likely consider another operational referendum for April 2027" ([Waunakee FAQ [35]](https://www.waunakee.k12.wi.us/community/2026referendum/faq)). MCPASD, by contrast, warned of "catastrophic" 20% cuts and a possible school closure ([Cap Times, Oct. 20 [49]](https://captimes.com/news/education/two-dane-county-school-districts-ask-voters-to-raise-property-taxes/article_56b581ca-9603-48fd-ad2e-566c6748b5bc.html)). Second, it leaned on credentials: second-lowest spending per student among Dane County peers ($16,106 against a regional average of $16,839), a debt refinancing that lowered the levy by about $2.39M, and a 2025-26 levy $2.3M under what was allowed, after the board had faced a projected 10.47% levy increase ([Waunakee Tribune, 2025 [69]](https://www.hngnews.com/tribune_enterprise/news/waunakee-school-board-considers-strategies-for-tax-levy-reduction/article_37cc13aa-1b19-4eee-827c-507b74803ed5.html); [Waunakee fact sheet [36]](https://www.waunakee.k12.wi.us/fs/resource-manager/view/54156bdc-8bac-43e4-8464-c6c34af2a164); [Waunakee presentation [38]](https://www.waunakee.k12.wi.us/fs/resource-manager/view/ed84ad2b-2a12-45b9-a894-107102763cc2)). Third, it ran an information-only campaign with seven drop-in sessions, high school tours, virtual sessions, a mailed newsletter with a Spanish version, and no organized Yes committee that the research could find ([Waunakee referendum page [35]](https://www.waunakee.k12.wi.us/community/2026referendum)). In 2022, by contrast, a "coalition of residents" had mailed fliers calling the $175M plan "excessive" ([Waunakee Tribune, Oct. 2022 [68]](https://www.hngnews.com/waunakee_tribune/news/government/school-district-responds-to-vote-no-information/article_595a0172-4e5f-11ed-9cd7-2f213b9f8a0f.html)).

Waunakee's tax claims look soft against its own ballot amounts. By derived arithmetic, the new operating levy above 2026-27 would be $2.1M, $4.2M, $6.3M and $8.4M in 2027-28 through 2030-31. On the equalized-value path in the district's own financing plan ($5.52B growing 4.2% a year), that is about $0.35, $0.67, $0.97 and $1.24 per $1,000 if levied in full, against the claimed $0.17. The claim equals only about $1.0M of levy, roughly half the first year's new money. The business director said "in the first two years, the full $2.1 million may not be needed," which may explain part of the difference, but the derivation was never published ([Waunakee Tribune, summer 2026 [61]](https://www.hngnews.com/tribune_enterprise/news/waunakee-school-board-picks-97-5-million-referendum-option-for-high-school-improvements/article_90cac93d-23e2-4c4b-aa11-491f4174f1a9.html)). On the bonds, the PMA Securities plan linked from the FAQ shows the debt-service rate rising from $1.31 in the 2026 levy year to $2.27 by 2029 and $2.20–$2.35 through 2048, a printed "Impact: $0.80" against the 2025-26 base. New-debt-only levy runs about $0.28 per $1,000 in 2027, rising to $0.70–$0.75 in 2029–2031 (derived), well above the flat $0.25 claimed ([PMA financing plan [40]](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1a6wr9TiG0BIGDVOu5zMEQY7pncvRA5O_/view)). These are flagged discrepancies, not proven errors. Waunakee has made rate-based promises before: in 2022 it said its rate would hold steady even with a $150M-plus referendum, and DPI shows the rate then fell from 10.89 to 9.69 ([Waunakee Tribune, Feb. 2022 [72]](https://www.hngnews.com/waunakee_tribune/news/local/waunakee-school-districts-financial-strategies-keep-tax-rate-consistent-even-with-a-150-million-referendum/article_365eaad2-94c0-11ec-a781-d749ff61e35f.html); [DPI [19]](https://sfs.dpi.wi.gov/safr_ro/all_mill_rate.asp?year=2023)).

Both districts entered 2026 with essentially identical school rates: 8.60 in Waunakee and 8.62 in MCPASD for 2025-26. Waunakee's levy rose 26.9% from 2021-22 to 2025-26, almost exactly MCPASD's 27.1%, but its equalized value grew 60.6% against MCPASD's 46.6%, so its rate fell further, from 10.89 to 8.60 (−21%) ([DPI [19]](https://sfs.dpi.wi.gov/safr_ro/all_mill_rate.asp?year=2026)). Waunakee's July 2026 annual meeting adopted a draft 2026-27 rate of about 8.80, an estimated $200 increase on a $500,000 home before any referendum effect ([Waunakee Tribune, July 2026 [63]](https://www.hngnews.com/tribune_enterprise/news/51-million-26-27-tax-levy-approved-at-waunakee-school-districts-annual-meeting/article_791dd8e9-ed47-4637-9b8d-97de24353522.html)). Differences in support between the districts are therefore unlikely to reflect different current burdens per dollar of value.

### 9.5 What Waunakee's surveys and structure teach MCPASD

Waunakee's spring survey measured 61.5% weighted support for extending $2.1M "for four more years." Voters were then shown a permanent increase to $10.5M, which won 52.6%, about nine points below the survey. Its capital question, surveyed at 51.1%, won 51.7% ([Waunakee Tribune, May 2026 [62]](https://www.hngnews.com/tribune_enterprise/news/local/waunakee-school-district-survey-results-show-support-for-operational-capital-building-referendums/article_a023290f-ea63-426c-9fea-d71f6ffaab56.html); [Waunakee Tribune, summer 2026 [61]](https://www.hngnews.com/tribune_enterprise/news/waunakee-school-board-picks-97-5-million-referendum-option-for-high-school-improvements/article_90cac93d-23e2-4c4b-aa11-491f4174f1a9.html)). MCPASD's survey erred in the opposite direction: 66% raw support and about 47% after likely-voter weighting, according to a secondhand summary, against a 60.1% result ([MCPASD board recap [32]](https://www.mcpasd.k12.wi.us/o/mcpasd/article/2975849); [Citizen Portal, secondhand [34]](https://citizenportal.ai/articles/8140470/wisconsin/school-districts/middleton-cross-plains-area-school-district/survey-finds-mixed-community-backing-for-november-operational-referendum-weighted-support-about-47)). The 2022 MCPASD survey had tested dose-response directly, finding 64% support at $20M, 58% at $24.9M and 52% at $30M before the $24.9M question won 62.9% ([MCPASD 2022 presentation [31]](https://clubrunner.blob.core.windows.net/00000006121/en-ca/files/homepage/m-cp-school-district-2022-referendum/Nov-2022-Referendum-M-CP-area-school-district-Presentation.pdf)). The transferable lesson is twofold: test the question in the structure it will actually take, and read a district's survey against its own past calibration rather than as a forecast.

The results allow the competing explanations to be ranked. The table summarizes the evidence for and against each; the prose that follows weighs them together.

Sources for the polling entries: [Marquette Law School Poll, Feb. 2026 [82]](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/2026/02/25/mlsp87-release/); [Marquette Today, June 2026 [83]](https://today.marquette.edu/2026/06/insight-from-the-marquette-law-school-poll-goes-beyond-election-contests/). WPR, citing a later Marquette reading, reported 48% for and 51% against ([WPR preview [75]](https://www.wpr.org/news/wisconsin-school-districts-referendums-nov-3-election)); the figures differ by wave, but the direction is the same.

The polarization story fails every test the ward data allow. If Republicans had driven the slide, the slope of Yes on Democratic share would have steepened, the rural towns would have added No votes and the Republican vote would have grown enough to account for the new No votes. None of that happened. The ambient explanations fit better. Marquette's February 2026 poll found only **43%** of Wisconsin voters would back a school tax referendum, down from 52% in June 2025, and **60%** preferred cutting property taxes to raising school spending, the highest share in 26 polls since 2013 and up from 42–46% in fall 2022 ([Marquette Law School Poll [82]](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/2026/02/25/mlsp87-release/)). Marquette's own summary describes a trend "toward opposing local school referendum proposals" ([Marquette Today [83]](https://today.marquette.edu/2026/06/insight-from-the-marquette-law-school-poll-goes-beyond-election-contests/)). Serial-ask fatigue adds a cumulative layer: MCPASD's three-cycle slide, Waunakee's four-cycle slide and the opponents' "continuous ask" language all point the same way. The Wisconsin Association of School Boards' Dan Rossmiller described the mechanism in 2020: when a non-recurring referendum expires, "very typically the school district is coming to ask for a bigger amount in the subsequent ask" ([WPR, Nov. 2020 [86]](https://www.wpr.org/wisconsin-voters-approved-84-percent-school-referendums-election)).

Ambient forces predict a decline everywhere. They do not explain why the Town of Middleton held while the City of Middleton, the Madison wards and the Village slid. Local tax experience supplies part of that map. In the Village and the rural towns, the pattern is the conventional one: fast appreciation shifted levy share toward those places, bills printed the district's highest rates or followed large revaluations, and Yes eroded, through new No votes in the Village and lost Yes votes in the towns. In the City, the bill story fails and the notice story fits the timing, but it rests on one well-placed coincidence. Two further City facts matter. The City has the highest Democratic share in the district, so it had the most Democratic support to lose, and it had the highest roll-off, which is consistent with conflicted Democrats moving from Yes to the blank line as well as to No. And because its homes are the most valuable, the same per-$100K figure means the largest dollar amounts there. Madison remains the least explained. Its wards had no revaluation, middling tax growth and the largest drop in Yes share, and the uncertainty about split-ward partisanship and renumbering is largest there.

The strongest alternative explanations deserve a direct hearing. One is regression from an unusually favorable 2022: that year's case was easy to make, because the state had given districts $0 per pupil for two years and COVID money was about to run out, while by 2026 the state was giving $325 a year and levies were already rising 8–11%. That reading fits the data and reinforces the main conclusion, since it is another way of saying that the tax-fairness argument got harder among Democrats who follow state politics. A second is demographic change, such as aging long-tenured homeowners or newcomer renters, which the research could not test because Census data were unreachable. A third is campaign effects. MCPASD had an organized, parent-led Yes committee; Waunakee had none that the research could find, and Waunakee lost more against its own 2022 baseline. Partisanship and campaign structure are confounded across the two districts, so the comparison cannot isolate the committee's effect.

The synthesis is a coalition model. MCPASD's Yes vote is a Democratic base multiplied by tax trust: trust that the bill is fair, that the district is frugal and that the state, not the district, is to blame for the gap. Party identity sets the ceiling; trust determines how much of it is reached. The 2026 wave confirmed the model from the other side, because more Democrats voted and Yes did not rise. Every mechanism that eroded support in 2026 worked through trust among Democrats: bills in the Village, notices in the City, numbers that changed mid-campaign, and a fourth consecutive ask in Waunakee. That is good news for 2030 in one respect. Several of those mechanisms, including the tax table, its timing, the fail scenario, the receipts and the ballot reminder, are under the district's and the campaign's control.

## 11. Limitations

The 2026 counts are unofficial until the canvass, which is due by Nov. 17, 2026. The ward tables should be re-downloaded and every derived figure re-run once the official results post. The margin will not change, but individual ward figures, and especially the under-vote counts that drive the roll-off findings, may.

Every partisan finding is ecological. The defection figures are bounds and model estimates under stated assumptions, not observations of how any Democrat or Republican voted. A quarter of MCPASD's referendum votes come from split units, where the partisan share describes a whole town or Madison ward rather than its MCPASD slice; the Berry, Springfield, Town of Cross Plains, Westport and Madison split-ward gaps are approximations. The unit counts are small (10 to 24 units for the regressions, eight municipalities for the property correlations), so slope changes and correlations are directional.

Roll-off before 2026 cannot be measured directly, because Dane County publishes under-votes only for the current election, so 2022 roll-off is estimated from fully-inside units. No ballot style from either year was inspected, which leaves the layout explanation for the City and Town of Middleton untested. Ward changes, and Madison's renumbering in particular, make some single-unit comparisons unreliable.

The property and tax data have specific gaps. No 2022 municipal-level DOR file survives online, so municipal comparisons begin in 2023 or 2024. Whole-municipality DOR measures stand in for the MCPASD slices of split towns. Zillow's ZIP codes straddle municipal lines, and DOR's single-year town estimates swing widely on few sales. The 2026-27 levy and rate, the 2026 assessment ratios and any December 2026 bill were not yet public. Whether the Village of Cross Plains revalued for 2026 is unconfirmed, and the City's notice text is known only secondhand.

The messaging record has its own gaps. The district's 2026 tax page, the baseline of its August figures and the reason the figures changed were never available, because the district site blocked automated access. The 47% weighted survey figure rests on a secondhand summary. Waunakee's derivation of its $0.17 and $0.25 claims was not published, so the discrepancies flagged here are not proven errors. Field-campaign facts from the Yes committee's internal tracker were not independently verified. Finally, nothing here separates causes with confidence. One election with ward-level data cannot isolate fatigue, structure, stakes framing, notices and roll-off from one another; that requires individual-level data the campaign does not yet have.

## 12. Implications and recommendations for 2030

### 12.1 The starting position: a $34.6M floor, a midterm date and a new state government

The 2026 authority covers 2027-28 through 2030-31, so new authority must be in place for 2031-32 ([WKOW, Nov. 4 [52]](https://www.wkow.com/news/middleton-cross-plains-school-district-referendum-passes/article_a78a2ddc-8a60-40f1-a2e3-96bff2443aa3.html)). The natural renewal date is **Nov. 5, 2030**, another midterm. The best Wisconsin evidence favors it. Kogan, Lavertu and Peskowitz, matching more than 10,000 school measures in four states to voter files, find no significant passage penalty for Wisconsin midterms relative to presidential elections (−0.07), but a **23-point lower passage probability in April spring elections** (p < .001), whose electorates hold about 10 points more voters aged 65 and over and 4 points fewer voters with children at home ([Kogan, Lavertu & Peskowitz 2018 [84]](https://joeornstein.github.io/pols-4641/readings/Kogan%20et%20al.%20-%202018%20-%20Election%20Timing,%20Electorate%20Composition,%20and%20Policy%20Outcomes.pdf)). The study's data end around 2016, so current risks in every election type are likely larger. April 2, 2030 makes sense only as a deliberate first try with November as the fallback, and a November 2030 failure would leave only an April 2031 retry, after budgets are set. Nov. 7, 2028, the highest-turnout and most parent-heavy electorate available, is the only plausible date for converting part of the base to recurring authority, and that option is speculative (dates computed).

The ask will start where 2026 ended, at **$34.6M**, and Waunakee shows that repeated asks erode support by a few points a cycle. A 60% baseline should not be assumed. The state is the swing variable. Democrats now hold the governorship and both legislative chambers ([Wikipedia, 2026 gubernatorial election [95]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Wisconsin_gubernatorial_election)), and Senate Democratic Leader Dianne Hesselbein, the board president's spouse, promised special-education and general-aid action "in January" ([Civic Media [47]](https://civicmedia.us/news/2026/09/21/middleton-referendum-discussion-highlights-tension-between-property-taxes-and-school-funding)). A meaningful revenue-limit or aid increase in the 2027–29 budget would shrink the 2030 ask. A token one would weaken the campaign's central argument, that the state will not fix the gap, while leaving most of the ask in place. Governance moves first. Two Area board seats convert to open, any-resident seats in April 2027, after electors approved the change 60–46 at the Sept. 22, 2026 annual meeting ([Open Seats MCPASD [42]](../index.html)). The board those races produce will set the size and structure of the 2030 question.

### 12.3 What to do differently

**Build a persuasion universe of Democratic homeowners, not only a turnout list.** The 2026 field plan worked 45 turfs covering 2,102 doors drawn from lists of likely supporters ([Referendum canvass volunteer plan [43]](Referendum%20canvass%20volunteer%20plan.md)). That is the right list for turnout and the wrong one for the votes that slipped away. A 2030 program needs a second universe of Democratic-voting, long-tenured homeowners in City Wards 5–8/21 and 9–13/19/22/24 and Village Wards 1–6, reached with a different script and different messengers, such as retirees, longtime neighbors and realtors. The scale argument is simple. The City of Middleton casts **47% of all referendum votes**, so a three-point recovery there adds about **670 votes to the margin**, more than any plausible movement in the rural towns combined. Older voters who resist state school spending are far more willing to fund their *own* district when home values and local ties are at stake ([Balsdon & Brunner 2003 [85]](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2003-24.pdf)), so the message to them should lead with home values and neighbors rather than inflation charts.

**Publish one tax table, with its basis, baseline and a real house, before the board votes.** The 2026 record shows what happens otherwise. The table should state the basis in the voter's language ("per $100,000 of the *estimated fair market value* printed on your tax bill, not your assessed value"), the baseline ("compared with your 2030-31 bill"), year-by-year levels and the four-year total, the figure for the typical home in each municipality (in 2026 terms, about $362 a year by year four in the City and about $301 in the Village), and the fail scenario. It should carry a date, and any later change should be announced with a reason. Hand it to reporters on the day of the board vote, correct errors within 24 hours, and ask commercial trackers to update superseded figures. An online calculator that asks for the fair-market line on the voter's own bill answers "Who has a $100,000 house?" before anyone asks it.

**Time the message around the assessment calendar.** DOR requires every municipality's average assessment to sit within 10% of market at least once every five years ([DOR PB-060 [24]](https://www.revenue.wi.gov/pubs/slf/pb060.pdf)). The Village of Cross Plains (0.65) and the Town of Berry (0.67) were far outside that band in 2025, so revaluations there are likely before or near 2030 (inference). The campaign should collect every municipality's revaluation schedule by 2029, work with assessors on a short "your notice is not your bill" explainer, and avoid a 2030 in which notices jump a third during the campaign without explanation. Where a revaluation or TID closure lowers the printed school rate, as the City's did in 2026, say so plainly.

**State the counterfactual before the opposition does.** The 2026 Yes coalition never faced an organized opponent, and No still reached 40% ([Civic Media [47]](https://civicmedia.us/news/2026/09/21/middleton-referendum-discussion-highlights-tension-between-property-taxes-and-school-funding)). A funded 2030 opposition would lead with the **roughly $1,000 a year** that failure would save a $500,000 home. The district should publish the side-by-side pass and fail budgets that skeptical voters asked for, and the campaign should test its answer early: what $34.6M in cuts would do to class sizes, buildings, programs and home values.

**Make "flip the ballot" standard with all eight clerks, in writing, by August.** Waunakee printed "Make Sure to Flip the Ballot!" in its mailed materials, and in every reporting unit the two districts shared, its question had lower roll-off than MCPASD's ([Waunakee newsletter [37]](https://www.waunakee.k12.wi.us/fs/resource-manager/view/1da25b04-b199-4671-b104-b2d50023302b)). In 2030 the campaign should ask all eight municipal clerks early for neutral reminder language on ballot instructions, absentee envelopes and poll-worker scripts, put "it's on the back" on every sign rider, hanger and post, and request the ballot styles so the layout hypothesis can finally be tested.

**Offer Waunakee's levy-less pledge, and post receipts every year.** Waunakee's board directed a policy to lower annual referendum amounts if the state budget provides resources ([Waunakee presentation [38]](https://www.waunakee.k12.wi.us/fs/resource-manager/view/ed84ad2b-2a12-45b9-a894-107102763cc2)). A similar public pledge from the MCPASD board, tied to the 2027–29 state budget, would turn "the continuous ask" into a shared goal and give tax-weary Democrats a way to be pro-school and anti-levy at the same time. Pair it with an annual "what the referendum bought, what we cut, what the rate did" report. It should track the promised $5M in cuts and tell the true and favorable rate story: through year three, the district projected +$99 per $100K and the total rate moved −$27.

**Archive the data the county does not keep, starting now.** Dane County publishes under-votes only for the current election, so the 2026 raw results file should be downloaded again after the canvass and stored before the next election replaces it ([Dane County raw results file [13]](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Election-RawResults)). The same applies to the official ward tables, DOR's municipal reports (which drop off after three years), the City's parcel-level assessment changes and the ACS district tables. The campaign should also build a 2026-to-2030 ward crosswalk now. Barring annexations, the November 2030 election should use the same post-2024 ward framework, because rewarding after the 2030 Census comes later (inference), which would make 2026-to-2030 comparisons cleaner than this paper's. By the campaign's internal tracker, which was not independently verified, only 25 of the 45 turfs were claimed, about 40% of targeted doors went unvisited and locked apartment buildings were skipped. Once WEC voter history updates, comparing turnout in worked and unclaimed turfs is the only local evidence the campaign will have on whether its doors moved votes.

**Survey the structure that will be on the ballot, and calibrate against 2026.** Waunakee's operating question ran about nine points below its survey after its structure changed from a four-year renewal to a permanent increase ([Waunakee Tribune, May 2026 [62]](https://www.hngnews.com/tribune_enterprise/news/local/waunakee-school-district-survey-results-show-support-for-operational-capital-building-referendums/article_a023290f-ea63-426c-9fea-d71f6ffaab56.html)). MCPASD's weighted survey understated its result by about 13 points. The 2029 survey should test the exact structures under consideration, including a straight non-recurring renewal from $34.6M and a partial recurring conversion, and its weights should be calibrated against 2026 ward results and turnout rather than read as a forecast.

**Study the Town of Middleton and watch the Village of Cross Plains.** The Town is the one large community that held its partisan baseline while its ballots grew 14%, and its Wards 5–8 ran almost seven points ahead of the partisan fit. Whatever explains that, whether new subdivisions with young families, proximity to schools, or the smaller and earlier 2026 notices, is the best local model for what works, and a dozen interviews there would be worth more than another statewide poll. The Village is the opposite case: the closest large unit, the fastest-appreciating municipality and the highest printed school rate. It needs local listening sessions well before 2030.

**Weigh the recurring option with Waunakee's caution.** A recurring conversion would end the four-year cycle that voters now name as a grievance. But only 3 of 8 recurring operating questions passed statewide in November 2026 ([Civic Media [73]](https://civicmedia.us/news/2026/11/04/majority-of-school-referendums-pass-in-2026-general-election)), and Waunakee's recurring question passed with its smallest operating margin. The board should model the levy paths of both options before choosing, and should keep the Yes coalition organized through the April 2027 board races so that whichever structure it chooses has a campaign behind it.

## 13. Conclusion

The 2026 vote changes how MCPASD's referendum risk should be understood. The danger is not a Republican backlash; the town-and-country No vote did not grow, and the Democratic wave added voters without adding Yes votes. The danger is a slow loss of trust inside the district's own Democratic majority, expressed differently in different places: through bills in the Village, through notices and blank ballots in the City, and through an ask that keeps getting bigger everywhere. That diagnosis is more hopeful than the topline suggests, because most of the trust mechanisms are controllable. The district's actual rate record since 2022 beats its promises, yet the campaign never told that story. The tax table switched mid-campaign. The cost of failure was never stated. And a cheap ballot reminder that worked next door was left mostly unused.

The comparison with Waunakee adds urgency. A district can keep passing operating questions while its margin erodes cycle after cycle, until a fourth ask passes by 673 votes. MCPASD's 2030 question will be its third straight four-year renewal and will start from a $34.6M ceiling. The analyst's most valuable work is not the 2030 field plan but the four years before it: archiving data that will otherwise disappear, finding out from individual voters what the ward data can only bound, and putting the receipts, the tax table and the state's budget choices in front of Middleton's skeptical Democrats long before anyone prints a yard sign.

## Appendix A. Counterfactual: the referendum without a blue wave

**Question.** Would the 2026 question have done better, worse or about the same in a midterm without a national Democratic wave?

**Short answer.** About the same, and possibly better. Under every scenario modeled here the question still passes. The partisan tide moves MCPASD's result by only one to three points. The variable that could sink a future question is the share of Democratic-leaning voters who vote Yes, not the partisan mix of the electorate.

### A.1 What the wave did inside MCPASD

The 2026 wave showed up in MCPASD mainly as extra turnout, not as a more Democratic electorate. The district-wide Democratic two-party share for governor rose only from 76.6% (Evers, 2022) to 77.3% (Crowley, 2026) [7][8]. In reporting units fully inside the district, 1,492 more ballots were cast than in 2022 [5][6]. Crowley ran 1,265 votes ahead of Evers there, but the referendum gained only 175 Yes votes against 801 No votes, and 516 more ballots left the question blank [4][13]. These are net changes. They cannot show whether the new voters themselves voted No, or whether returning 2022 voters switched while the newcomers split evenly. Either way, the extra turnout produced few new Yes votes.

### A.2 Method

The scenarios use a simple two-group ecological model (Goodman regression). Each reporting unit's Yes share is modeled as a weighted mix of a Democratic Yes rate and a Republican Yes rate, with the unit's Democratic two-party governor share as the weight. The model is fitted across reporting units with at least 20 referendum votes, weighted by referendum votes, using the ward tables in Sections 4 and 6 [4][7][8]. Two fits bracket the estimates:

| Fit (2026) | Units | Democratic Yes rate | Republican Yes rate | Predicted district Yes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| All units | 24 | 0.76 | 0.07 | 60.3% (actual 60.1%) |
| Units fully inside MCPASD | 10 | 0.73 | 0.22 | 61.4% (recentered to 60.1%) |

The same fits for 2022 give a Democratic Yes rate of 0.79–0.82. The decline to 0.73–0.76 in 2026 is the defection described in Section 6. Every scenario below holds the 2026 rates fixed and changes only the electorate, except the last, which also lowers the Democratic Yes rate. Margins assume about 23,720 votes on the question, as in 2026. The caveats in Section 2 apply in full. These are ward aggregates with few units, the Republican rate is poorly identified, and results are directional, not forecasts.

### A.3 Scenarios

| Scenario | Electorate assumption | Estimated Yes | Estimated margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Actual 2026 (blue wave) | Democratic share 77.3% | 60.1% | +4,806 |
| Neutral year, composition only | Democratic share back to 2022's 76.6% | ~59.8% | ~+4,650 |
| Neutral year, turnout effect | 2022-like turnout; the extra 2026 voters absent | up to ~62.9% (upper bound) | up to ~+6,130 |
| Red-leaning midterm | Democratic share 4 points lower (~73%) | ~57.6–58.1% | ~+3,600 to +3,850 |
| Red-leaning midterm with sharper tax revolt | ~73% Democratic, and the Democratic Yes rate falls to 0.70 | ~53–56% | ~+1,500 to +2,800 |

The "neutral year" result is a range because of the ambiguity in A.1. If the 2026 slip came from returning voters changing their minds, removing the wave's extra voters changes almost nothing (about 59.8%). If it came from the extra voters themselves, a 2022-sized electorate would have produced something close to 2022's 62.9%. The true effect probably lies between, so a reasonable central guess is that the wave cost the referendum zero to two points. That fits Section 5's finding that high turnout in 2026 did not mean a more school-supportive electorate. It also qualifies the general pattern that larger, younger, presidential-year electorates help school measures [84]. Higher turnout helps when the added voters are parents and school-connected households, not when they are low-engagement voters drawn out by a nationalized race.

### A.4 Break-even points

Holding the other parameter at its 2026 value, the question falls below 50% if either:

- the district's Democratic two-party share drops to about **57–62%** (from 77%), roughly Waunakee's electorate (66.5%) minus several more points [16], or
- the Democratic Yes rate drops to about **0.60–0.63** (from 0.73–0.76), meaning roughly two in five Democratic-voting referendum voters choose No.

The first is implausible for MCPASD in any foreseeable midterm. The second is a continuation of the trend already measured: the Democratic Yes rate fell about five to six points from 2022 to 2026, and another two drops of that size would put the district at the edge.

### A.5 Implications

1. **Plan for a neutral or red-leaning 2030 midterm, but don't fear it most.** A hostile national climate costs perhaps two to three points. That hurts, but it does not decide the result.
2. **Treat Democratic defection as the main risk.** The model's most sensitive input is how Democratic-leaning homeowners vote, which is why Sections 6, 7 and 12 focus on the City of Middleton, the Madison wards and the Village of Cross Plains.
3. **Don't treat high turnout as a strategy.** Turnout programs should target school-connected households. Raising general turnout in a nationalized year brought in voters who skipped the question or voted No.
4. **Re-run this appendix after the Nov. 17 canvass** and, if possible, with voter-file turnout data that separates new voters from repeat voters. That would resolve the A.1 ambiguity.
