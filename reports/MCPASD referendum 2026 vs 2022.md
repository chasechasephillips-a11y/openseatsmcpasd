# Democratic softness shrinks Middleton's referendum cushion

The Middleton-Cross Plains Area School District's Nov. 3, 2026 operating referendum passed with **60.1% (14,263 Yes to 9,457 No, unofficial)**. That is 2.8 points below the **62.9% (14,596 to 8,608)** the Nov. 8, 2022 question won, even though the 2026 ask was almost twice as large on paper: **$118.5M over four years against $65.5M** ([Dane County 2026](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Precincts-Result/197/0040); [Dane County 2022](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Precincts-Result/145/0042)). Yes lost only 333 votes, but No gained 849. Almost all of the new No votes came from the district's most Democratic places: the City of Middleton, the Madison wards and the Village of Cross Plains. The Town of Middleton held at 58% Yes while its electorate grew 14%. The slip was not partisan polarization. Yes ran **13.7 points behind the Democratic governor vote in 2022 and 17.1 points behind in 2026**, the link between party and referendum vote weakened, and the arithmetic requires that **at least one in five Crowley voters voted No**. Roll-off on the back-of-ballot question rose to **7.2%**, mostly in the City and Town of Middleton, where it ran two to three times the rate on the statewide questions on the same ballots. Taxes framed the fight. The school mill rate fell 13% from 2021-22 to 2025-26, but the levy rose 27% on a 47% jump in property values. A City of Middleton revaluation landed in the middle of the campaign, and voters saw two different sets of tax-impact numbers. Against the state, the result is strong: November school pass rates fell from about 79% in 2022 to about 57% in 2026. But the remaining cushion rests on Democratic-leaning homeowners who are drifting away. The 2030 renewal will start at $34.6M. It needs four things: a persuasion program aimed at those voters, one tax number held to from start to finish, a fix for the back-of-ballot problem, and a plan for a new Democratic state government that could change the math.

## A bigger ask lost 2.8 points while the state lost 22

The two ballot questions were built the same way. Both asked for four years of **non-recurring** authority to exceed the revenue limit, with a purpose clause that is word-for-word identical: "ongoing educational and extracurricular programming, and compensation, and other operational expenses" ([2022 district presentation](https://clubrunner.blob.core.windows.net/00000006121/en-ca/files/homepage/m-cp-school-district-2022-referendum/Nov-2022-Referendum-M-CP-area-school-district-Presentation.pdf); [Dane County What's on the Ballot, Nov. 2026](https://elections.countyofdane.com/documents/pdf/What-s-on-the-Ballot-Nov-2026.pdf)). The difference is the shape of the money. In 2022 the authority ramped up from zero to $24.9M. In 2026 it started at that $24.9M plateau and stepped up from there.

| Result | 14,596 Yes / 8,608 No, **62.9%** (official) | 14,263 Yes / 9,457 No, **60.1%** (unofficial) |

The "renewal" label is mostly accurate. Four years at the existing $24.9M accounts for **$99.6M of the $118.5M (84%)**. Only about **$18.9M** is new money above the current level ($3.0M, $6.2M and $9.7M in years two through four). The district said it "would continue funding authorized under the district's 2022 operational referendum while adding annual adjustments to address rising costs" ([Channel 3000, Aug. 12, 2026](https://www.channel3000.com/news/middleton-cross-plains-school-board-approves-34-6m-referendum-for-nov-3-election/article_1db2aab8-7a6b-4944-aab3-394bc0a8ac26.html)). The board president, Bob Hesselbein, said the board weighed "about six different referendum scenarios" and "didn't want to ask for more because everyone's hurting" ([Civic Media](https://civicmedia.us/news/2026/09/21/middleton-referendum-discussion-highlights-tension-between-property-taxes-and-school-funding)). But the label also hides a 39% higher final-year ceiling. The Wisconsin Policy Forum ranked the measure the **seventh-largest operating referendum in state history** ([Cap Times, Oct. 20, 2026](https://captimes.com/news/education/two-dane-county-school-districts-ask-voters-to-raise-property-taxes/article_56b581ca-9603-48fd-ad2e-566c6748b5bc.html)). It was second on this November's ballot only to Green Bay's $158M ask, which squeaked through at about 51% in early returns ([WPR](https://www.wpr.org/news/wisconsin-school-districts-referendums-nov-3-election); [WBAY](https://www.wbay.com/2026/11/04/wisconsin-voters-decide-79-school-referendum-questions-including-four-northeast-wisconsin/)).

Judged against that climate, MCPASD held up well. Statewide, only **3 of 8 recurring operating questions** passed in November. Limited-term operating questions in larger cities and suburbs did better ([Civic Media, Nov. 4, 2026](https://civicmedia.us/news/2026/11/04/majority-of-school-referendums-pass-in-2026-general-election)). Next door, Waunakee's operating question passed with about 52.6% ([Cap Times, Nov. 4, 2026](https://captimes.com/news/elections/dane-county-voters-pass-three-school-funding-referendums/article_612b45b9-058a-4b7c-ae10-3712d49218ff.html)). MCPASD's 60% nearly matched Elmbrook's 61% capital question and Whitefish Bay's 63%, which FOX6 called the widest approval margin in southeast Wisconsin ([FOX6](https://www.fox6now.com/news/wisconsin-election-school-referendum-results)). The longer trend line is the part to watch. MCPASD's 2018 operating question won **69.2%** ([Channel 3000, 2018](https://www.channel3000.com/news/local-news/largest-school-referendum-in-the-state-passes-in-middleton/article_3d70481f-fba4-56ed-ad87-923a4783d0cf.html)). The district has now slid from 69% to 63% to 60% over three cycles, each a larger ask than the last. The 2026 figures stay unofficial until the canvass. Wisconsin county canvass boards must certify by **Nov. 17, 2026** ([Campaign Legal Center guide](https://campaignlegal.org/sites/default/files/2026-08/2026%20Wisconsin%20Certification%20Guide_Final.pdf)). A 4,806-vote margin will not change, though individual ward figures may shift by a few votes.

## No votes rose in Democratic strongholds while the Town of Middleton held

Yes share fell in **seven of eight municipalities**. The 849 net new No votes came almost entirely from four places: the City of Middleton (+261), the Madison wards (+260), the Village of Cross Plains (+203) and the Town of Middleton (+139). The rural towns together added *fewer* No votes than in 2022 (−14). Each place shows a different kind of loss. The **Village of Cross Plains, Wards 1–6** is the clearest case of lost persuasion. Yes was flat (−4 votes) while No rose by 164 on almost the same number of ballots (2,252 to 2,340), so the extra voters there broke almost entirely No. The village's core is now a near-even **51.8%**. The **City of Middleton** losses were concentrated in Wards 5–8/21 (67.1% to 62.5%, +132 No) and Wards 9–13/19/22/24 (+125 No), where growth around the new Ward 24 added mostly No votes. City Wards 14–18 behaved differently. Yes fell 177 votes while No *also* fell 33, so the unit lost votes to the blank line rather than to the other side. The **Town of Middleton** added 325 referendum votes (+8.8%), and the new voters split the same way as the existing ones. The MCPASD portion of the **Town of Cross Plains** flipped from a narrow Yes majority (50.6%) to a No majority (46.6%). The other rural towns lost 133 Yes votes in total, while their No count was flat. They did not get angrier. They got quieter on Yes.

The Madison wards need care. Individual split wards show large drops: Ward 102 fell 16.0 points, Ward 100 16.6 and Ward 109 11.7. But those wards are mostly outside MCPASD and cast only a few hundred district votes each. Madison also renumbered several wards between the elections: 113, 139, 140 and 141 disappeared, 120, 134 and 135 appeared, and Ward 118's ballots grew from 798 to 1,264. Combining the west-side whole wards to keep the geography constant gives a cleaner figure: **71.8% to 68.0% (−3.7 points)**. Wards changed in three other municipalities too. The Town of Middleton went from one reporting unit to four, the Village added Ward 7, and the City merged Wards 21 and 22 into neighbouring units and added Ward 24. All of these splits fall on assembly-district lines ([What's on the Ballot, 2026](https://elections.countyofdane.com/documents/pdf/What-s-on-the-Ballot-Nov-2026.pdf)), so the comparisons above are made at the unit or municipal level. The new Village Ward 7 voted **63.6% Yes**, well above the village core, on 107 votes.

## Roll-off doubled in Middleton but fell elsewhere

Turnout rose modestly, and the extra voters did not help. Referendum votes rose **2.2%**. Ballots cast in units fully inside the district rose about **8%**, and in the Town of Middleton they rose 14.1% ([Dane County ballots cast, 2022](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Precincts-Result/145/0002); [2026](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Precincts-Result/197/0002)). Dane County's 2026 raw results file counts under-votes directly. It shows **1,834 under-votes and 5 over-votes on 25,559 ballots** that carried the question, a roll-off rate of **7.2%** ([Dane County raw results](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Election-RawResults)). Dane County does not publish raw under-vote data for past elections, so 2022 roll-off can be estimated only in units wholly inside the district, as ballots cast minus Yes and No.

| Town of Middleton (all) | 3.6% | **8.1%** (7.2% direct count) | 2.5–3.1% | 0.6% |

Sources: [2026 governor](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Precincts-Result/197/0004), [2026 State Ref. 1](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Precincts-Result/197/0034), [2022 governor](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Precincts-Result/145/0004), and the MCPASD precinct tables cited above.

This is not a uniform "voters missed the back of the ballot" story, although NBC15 reported that the question "will appear on the back of the ballot" ([WMTV, Oct. 22, 2026](https://www.wmtv15news.com/2026/10/22/middleton-cross-plains-voters-weigh-school-referendum-district-faces-nearly-25m-potential-cuts/)). Roll-off *fell* in the Village and in Madison while it nearly doubled in the City and Town of Middleton. The **City of Middleton alone produced 1,090 of the district's 1,839 blank or spoiled ballots (59%)**. In those two Middleton municipalities the school question drew two to three times the roll-off of the three statewide constitutional questions printed on the same ballots. In the Village and Madison it ran about level with them. The pattern looks like a ballot-layout difference by municipality, such as which column or side the school question landed on. Nobody has yet checked the actual ballot styles, so this remains a hypothesis. Skipping was also *highest in the most Democratic places*. The weighted correlation between a unit's under-vote rate and its Republican governor share was **−0.47**, which points to conflicted Democrats leaving the line blank rather than Republicans ignoring it.

In the clean geography (units fully inside the district), the extra 2026 voters split badly for Yes. Of **1,492 net new ballots**, about 12% became Yes votes (+175), 54% became No votes (+801) and 35% skipped the question (+516). Roll-off did not threaten passage. Had the City and Town of Middleton held their 2022 roll-off rates, roughly **550 more votes** would have been cast, adding perhaps 100–150 to the Yes margin. In a closer race that is a margin worth fighting for, and it is one of the few problems a campaign can fix at almost no cost.

## Democrats, not Republicans, cost the referendum its points

Party still predicts where Yes is strong. Across 24 reporting units, Yes share tracked the Democratic governor share with a weighted correlation of **0.88 in 2022 and 0.79 in 2026**. The relationship got **weaker and flatter**, though: Yes rose 0.81 points per Democratic point in 2022 and only 0.69 in 2026. Among units wholly inside the district the slope fell from 0.68 to 0.51 (computed from the [2022](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Precincts-Result/145/0004) and [2026](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Precincts-Result/197/0004) governor and referendum tables). With so few units the change is directional rather than conclusive: 77% of bootstrap resamples showed a flatter 2026 slope. Yet it points the same way under every partisan measure tested. The referendum became *less* partisan because it lost ground in the most Democratic territory.

The gap between Yes and the Democratic vote widened. District-wide, Yes ran **13.7 points behind Evers in 2022 and 17.1 behind Crowley in 2026**. The cleanest year-over-year test is the attorney general race, a Kaul–Toney rematch in both years. On that measure the electorate became *more* Democratic (Kaul's two-party share in MCPASD rose from 74.3% to 77.5%), and the gap still widened from **−11.4 to −17.4** ([Dane AG 2022](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Precincts-Result/145/0005); [2026](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Precincts-Result/197/0005)).

Partisan shares are weighted by referendum votes. For split towns and Madison wards they describe the whole reporting unit, not just its MCPASD slice.

**The Town of Middleton stayed true.** Its Yes share held while its Democratic share edged up, and Wards 5–8 ran **6.7 points ahead** of what partisanship predicts: 59.5% Yes in a unit that went 66.6% Crowley. Madison Ward 118 (+7.2) and City of Middleton Wards 1–4 (+3.1) also beat their partisan baselines. The gap grew most in the Madison split wards, where it widened from −10.8 to −21.9, and in **City of Middleton Wards 5–8/21**. Those wards went 86.7% for Crowley but only 62.5% Yes, a −24.2 gap, the widest of any large unit wholly inside the district. The Town of Berry is the district's biggest outlier in both years, at **28.9% Yes in a unit that went 57.6% Crowley**. Berry is a split unit, however, so its MCPASD voters may lean more Republican than the town as a whole.

The Democratic wave raised turnout without raising Yes. In the clean geography, Crowley ran **1,265 votes ahead of Evers (+9.2%)** and Tiffany gained 266 votes on Michels. Yes rose only 175 votes while No rose 801, **three times the Republican gain**. Yes votes fell from 79.2% of the Democratic governor vote to 73.7%. In no unit with 20 or more votes did Yes beat the Democratic share, in either year, so the data require *zero* Republican crossover anywhere. Assume instead that every Republican voted No. Then at least **17.9% of Democratic-voting referendum participants voted No in 2022, and at least 22.3% in 2026**. A more realistic scenario has 15% of Republicans voting Yes, which puts Democratic No voters at about **27%**. An ecological (Goodman) fit gives the same direction: Democratic Yes fell from about 79% to about 73%, and Republican Yes may have ticked up from about 11% to about 22%. That second figure is not reliable with only ten clean units.

Other contests on the ballot show the same cross-pressure. Three Republican-sponsored constitutional amendments drew **28–39% Yes** in MCPASD's clean units, well above Tiffany's roughly 21.6%. One was the amendment limiting the partial veto, written in response to Evers's "400-year" school-funding veto. It took 32.5% Yes there and was ratified statewide ([Wikipedia, 2026 Wisconsin elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Wisconsin_elections); [Dane race 0034](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Precincts-Result/197/0034)). MCPASD's Yes-minus-Democrat gap was **the largest of any Dane County school question in 2022, and it grew in 2026**. Waunakee's operating question, by comparison, ran −13.9 behind its Democratic baseline ([Dane County 2026](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Election-Result/197)).

Statewide research shows the same compression. In 2022, local referendum support outran Evers's county share in **61 of 81** measures. Of the 20 where Evers ran ahead, 12 were in Dane or Milwaukee counties ([Cap Times, Nov. 24, 2022](https://captimes.com/news/education/wisconsin-school-referendum-results-dont-follow-partisanship/article_a5bd39dc-7265-5b77-af03-5703777a7123.html)). Polling, though, shows school-funding attitudes splitting roughly 80/20 by party ([Marquette Law School Poll, Feb. 2026](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/2026/02/25/mlsp87-release/)). The best way to square the two is that the statewide turn against referendums reached Democratic suburban homeowners through their tax bills, without deepening the party split. One caveat covers this whole section: these are ward aggregates, not individual votes, so the defection figures are bounds, not observations.

## School tax rates fell 13% while the levy rose 27%

The tax record that voters carried into 2026 is a paradox, and both sides can quote it truthfully. Department of Public Instruction (DPI) data show the district's equalized value (its total taxable property value, measured with tax-increment districts excluded, or "TIF-out") rising far faster than the levy. As a result the mill rate fell even as tax bills grew.

| School year | Levy | Equalized value (TIF-out) | Mill rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2021-22 | $79.6M | $8.00B | 9.95 |
| 2022-23 | $81.5M | $9.17B | 8.89 |
| 2023-24 | $84.1M | $10.00B | 8.41 |
| 2024-25 | $91.2M | $10.96B | 8.32 |
| 2025-26 | $101.2M | $11.73B | 8.62 |

Sources: DPI equalized mill rates for [2021-22](https://sfs.dpi.wi.gov/safr_ro/all_mill_rate.asp?year=2022), [2022-23](https://sfs.dpi.wi.gov/safr_ro/all_mill_rate.asp?year=2023), [2023-24](https://sfs.dpi.wi.gov/safr_ro/all_mill_rate.asp?year=2024), [2024-25](https://sfs.dpi.wi.gov/safr_ro/all_mill_rate.asp?year=2025), [2025-26](https://sfs.dpi.wi.gov/safr_ro/all_mill_rate.asp?year=2026). The 2026-27 figures were not yet posted on Nov. 5.

From 2021-22 to 2025-26, the levy rose **27%** while the rate fell **13%** because values jumped **47%**. The municipal rate sheets confirm the DPI rate ([Dane County Treasurer, 2025 mill rates](https://treasurer.danecounty.gov/documents/pdf/CurrentYear-DaneCountyMillRatesByDistrict-NOT-C-Madison.pdf)). Take a home worth $400,000 in 2021-22 whose value tracked the district average. By derived arithmetic, its gross school tax went from about **$3,980 to about $5,055**, a 27% rise. Homes that appreciated less than the district total, which includes new construction, saw smaller increases. Measured by rate, the 2022 promises held up better than projected. The district had projected per-$100K increases of $7, $53 and $39 for the first three years ([2022 presentation](https://clubrunner.blob.core.windows.net/00000006121/en-ca/files/homepage/m-cp-school-district-2022-referendum/Nov-2022-Referendum-M-CP-area-school-district-Presentation.pdf)). The actual rate moved **−$48, −$9 and +$30**. The referendum's own share of the rate grew almost exactly as projected in years two and three (+$53 and +$40). One figure is a gift to any future opponent. Levy growth from 2022-23 to 2025-26 (+$19.6M) almost equals that year's referendum authority ($19.435M). On a rough reading, the referendum accounts for nearly all of the levy growth since it passed.

The two campaigns were set against different state backdrops. The 2022 ask followed **two straight years of $0 per-pupil revenue-limit increases**, 4.7% inflation and $6.9M in one-time COVID money that the district warned would create "a significant fiscal cliff" ([2022 presentation](https://clubrunner.blob.core.windows.net/00000006121/en-ca/files/homepage/m-cp-school-district-2022-referendum/Nov-2022-Referendum-M-CP-area-school-district-Presentation.pdf); [Cap Times, Oct. 24, 2022](https://captimes.com/news/education/eight-dane-county-school-districts-have-referenda-on-november-ballot/article_ce8ceb2d-51d4-55cb-a08b-47b3c7665771.html)). By 2026, districts were getting **$325 per pupil a year**. Evers's partial veto extended that increase through 2425, and the state Supreme Court upheld the veto in April 2025 ([Wisconsin Examiner](https://wisconsinexaminer.com/2025/04/18/wi-supreme-court-upholds-gov-evers-partial-veto-extending-school-funding-increases-for-400-years/)). But the 2025-27 budget added no general aid, so the increase falls on local property taxes ([WSAA](https://wsaa.org/archives/19868); [DPI Act 15 summary](https://dpi.wi.gov/administrators/biweekly-mailing/summary-changes-2025-act-15-2025-27-biennial-budget)). That matters more in MCPASD than in most districts. Its leaders say it is **about 80% property-tax funded** and "one of the highest in Dane County for property values to students" ([Civic Media](https://civicmedia.us/news/2026/09/21/middleton-referendum-discussion-highlights-tension-between-property-taxes-and-school-funding); [WKOW, Aug. 12, 2026](https://www.wkow.com/news/middleton-cross-plains-school-district-asks-taxpayers-for-more-money-in-november-referendum/article_a1c6ba6d-441d-4075-bc4f-e46c4110d69e.html)). Special-education reimbursement was budgeted at 42% but came in at a 35% interim rate ([Wisconsin Examiner, Nov. 2025](https://wisconsinexaminer.com/2025/11/21/special-education-reimbursement-payments-to-schools-fall-far-below-estimate/)). The levy jumped 8.4% and then 11.0% in the two years *before* this vote. Voters were already watching their school taxes rise when the question arrived.

The 2026 tax figures themselves were muddled. In August the district's numbers implied **$44, $65, $64 and $64 per $100K**, which WKOW summed to "$237 over four years" ([WKOW, Aug. 12](https://www.wkow.com/news/middleton-cross-plains-school-district-asks-taxpayers-for-more-money-in-november-referendum/article_a1c6ba6d-441d-4075-bc4f-e46c4110d69e.html); [BadgerHomeowner](https://www.badgerhomeowner.com/school-referendums-dane-county/)). By September the district's explanatory page said **$0, $21, $40 and $59** ([Civic Media](https://civicmedia.us/news/2026/09/21/middleton-referendum-discussion-highlights-tension-between-property-taxes-and-school-funding)). Those are levels above today's bill: on a $500,000 home, about $295 a year by year four and $600 in total over four years ([Channel 3000, Sept. 28](https://www.channel3000.com/news/middleton-cross-plains-area-school-district-voters-deciding-on-118m-school-referendum-in-november/article_21c34ed2-934f-4903-9354-3e66b2bfc210.html)). NBC15 described the same figures as "$600 more annually by the fourth year," which is almost certainly a misreading ([WMTV, Oct. 22](https://www.wmtv15news.com/2026/10/22/middleton-cross-plains-voters-weigh-school-referendum-district-faces-nearly-25m-potential-cuts/)). The Cap Times printed "$120 per $100,000 … over the next four years" right next to Waunakee's "$210 per year" ([Cap Times, Oct. 20](https://captimes.com/news/education/two-dane-county-school-districts-ask-voters-to-raise-property-taxes/article_56b581ca-9603-48fd-ad2e-566c6748b5bc.html)). Nobody stated the opposite side of the "year one costs nothing" claim. Had the question failed, the $24.9M would have dropped out of the 2027-28 levy. By derived arithmetic at 2025 values, that is **about $212 per $100K, or roughly $1,060 on a $500,000 home**, as a tax *cut*. Revaluation added to the tension. The City of Middleton had been assessing at 78% of market value and revalued to 100% for 2026 ([City revaluation overview](https://www.cityofmiddleton.us/DocumentCenter/View/20472/Middleton-Revaluation-Overview)). Total assessed value rose about 32%, and the Board of Review met on **Sept. 22, weeks before the vote** ([City notice, secondhand](https://cityofmiddleton.us/CivicSend/ViewMessage/message/297551)). The Town of Middleton mailed its own revaluation notices in April ([Town newsletter](https://middletonwi.govoffice3.com/vertical/Sites/%7B97A50AAB-3824-4833-ACEA-EF2B9A14C856%7D/uploads/Spring_2026_website.pdf)). No reporting tied the revaluations to referendum sentiment. Still, the City is where the gap grew and the Town is where it held, and the City's notices landed at the worst possible moment.

## Reporters headlined $118.5 million while the campaign said renewal

Voters heard **three different sizes** for the same question. The district's August release led with the final-year "$34.6M" and talked about continuing the $24.9M ([Channel 3000, Aug. 12](https://www.channel3000.com/news/middleton-cross-plains-school-board-approves-34-6m-referendum-for-nov-3-election/article_1db2aab8-7a6b-4944-aab3-394bc0a8ac26.html)). Most later coverage led with the four-year total: "$118M," "$118.5 million boost," "seventh-largest in state history" ([Cap Times, Oct. 20](https://captimes.com/news/education/two-dane-county-school-districts-ask-voters-to-raise-property-taxes/article_56b581ca-9603-48fd-ad2e-566c6748b5bc.html); [Channel 3000, Nov. 3](https://www.channel3000.com/news/middleton-cross-plains-area-school-district-voters-poised-to-approve-118m-referendum/article_609b4a19-3042-41f0-a79b-71dcba49c850.html)). NBC15 framed it as a renewal against "nearly $25M in potential cuts" ([WMTV, Oct. 22](https://www.wmtv15news.com/2026/10/22/middleton-cross-plains-voters-weigh-school-referendum-district-faces-nearly-25m-potential-cuts/)). In 2022 the press had likewise used the total ("$65.5 million") while the district used its final-year "$24.9 million" ([Channel 3000, Nov. 9, 2022](https://www.channel3000.com/news/politics/elections/middleton-cross-plains-area-school-district-referendum-passes/article_1d33d875-2226-504c-a797-f9ea84c6890a.html)). The gap between the two framings was much larger in 2026.

The district and the independent Vote Yes campaign made one consistent argument. They called it a renewal with no new cost in year one. They said the state formula has fallen **$3,600 per student behind inflation since 2010**, about a $25.4M gap ([WMTV](https://www.wmtv15news.com/2026/10/22/middleton-cross-plains-voters-weigh-school-referendum-district-faces-nearly-25m-potential-cuts/)). They said failure meant a 20% cut that Hesselbein called "catastrophic" and that "could lead to the closure of an elementary school" ([Cap Times, Oct. 20](https://captimes.com/news/education/two-dane-county-school-districts-ask-voters-to-raise-property-taxes/article_56b581ca-9603-48fd-ad2e-566c6748b5bc.html)). The district also volunteered that it would cut **about $5 million over four years even if the measure passed**, a forward commitment that the 2022 pitch, which cited $6.3M in cuts already made, did not offer ([MCPASD](https://www.mcpasd.k12.wi.us/o/mcpasd/article/3232285)). Co-chair Kris Tawil described the cause as "a statewide epidemic … Until it's fixed at the state level, our only option is to do these funding renewal referendums" ([Cap Times](https://captimes.com/news/education/two-dane-county-school-districts-ask-voters-to-raise-property-taxes/article_56b581ca-9603-48fd-ad2e-566c6748b5bc.html)). She named the campaign's biggest hurdle plainly: "educating people on A, that it's going to be on the ballot and B, why we need it" ([WMTV](https://www.wmtv15news.com/2026/10/22/middleton-cross-plains-voters-weigh-school-referendum-district-faces-nearly-25m-potential-cuts/)).

There was **no organized No committee** in 2026 ([Civic Media](https://civicmedia.us/news/2026/09/21/middleton-referendum-discussion-highlights-tension-between-property-taxes-and-school-funding)), and the research found no evidence of one in 2022 either. Opposition came from individuals, and it was coherent. A parent said "we just can't keep adding taxes on top of taxes on top of taxes" and wanted the state surplus spent on schools ([WKOW, Aug. 12](https://www.wkow.com/news/middleton-cross-plains-school-district-asks-taxpayers-for-more-money-in-november-referendum/article_a1c6ba6d-441d-4075-bc4f-e46c4110d69e.html)). A realtor and parent objected to "the continuous ask" and wanted "a side-by-side comparison of budgets" for pass and fail ([Channel 3000, Sept. 28](https://www.channel3000.com/news/middleton-cross-plains-area-school-district-voters-deciding-on-118m-school-referendum-in-november/article_21c34ed2-934f-4903-9354-3e66b2bfc210.html)). A retiree asked, "Who has a $100,000 house in Middleton?" He went on: "There's room to cut." That exchange came at a Sept. 17 information session attended by **about 20 people** ([Civic Media](https://civicmedia.us/news/2026/09/21/middleton-referendum-discussion-highlights-tension-between-property-taxes-and-school-funding)). The polling backdrop was hostile. In February 2026, only **43%** of Wisconsin voters said they would back a school tax referendum, down from 52% in June 2025. **60%** preferred cutting property taxes to raising school spending, the highest share in 26 polls since 2013 and up from 42–46% in fall 2022 ([Marquette Law School Poll](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/2026/02/25/mlsp87-release/)).

The district's own surveys show how hard this electorate is to read. In 2022, 1,925 responses tested support by dollar level: **64% at $20M, 58% at $24.9M and 52% at $30M**. The $24.9M question then won 62.9% ([2022 presentation](https://clubrunner.blob.core.windows.net/00000006121/en-ca/files/homepage/m-cp-school-district-2022-referendum/Nov-2022-Referendum-M-CP-area-school-district-Presentation.pdf)). In 2026, the Donovan Group survey drew 1,818 responses, and **66%** said they would probably or definitely support a measure ([MCPASD board recap](https://www.mcpasd.k12.wi.us/o/mcpasd/article/2975849)). After weighting to likely voters, support reportedly fell to **about 47%** ([Citizen Portal, secondhand](https://citizenportal.ai/articles/8140470/wisconsin/school-districts/middleton-cross-plains-area-school-district/survey-finds-mixed-community-backing-for-november-operational-referendum-weighted-support-about-47)). The result, 60.1%, landed between the raw and weighted numbers. The weighting was too pessimistic about a November electorate, or the campaign moved voters, or both. Non-supporters in that survey cited "property tax fatigue," a "preference for structural or state-level solutions," and a desire for "greater fiscal discipline, and greater transparency." That is very nearly a list of what persuadable Democrats need to hear next time.

## What changed this time, and the questions to settle before 2030

Seven things separated 2026 from 2022. **The ask changed shape**, from a ramp built from zero to a plateau with escalators, so "renewal" was true in year one and arguable after that. **The climate turned**: November pass rates statewide fell 22 points and Marquette's referendum question went net-negative. **The campaign changed**: 2026 had an independent Yes committee doing visible fieldwork, which the research could not find in 2022. **Taxes were already rising**, because the $325 per-pupil increases with no new aid pushed the levy up 8–11% a year before the vote. In 2022, by contrast, the pitch had been that the state was giving nothing. **The ballot changed**: in 2022 the school question shared the ballot with a City of Middleton levy referendum that also passed comfortably ([WPF via WNA](https://wnanews.com/?p=52191)), while in 2026 it sat behind three tax- and veto-themed constitutional amendments on the back of the ballot. **Revaluation notices** went out in both Middletons. And **the partisan tide rose without lifting Yes**, which breaks the easy assumption that a big Democratic turnout carries a school question in Dane County.

The next decisions hinge on questions nobody has answered yet. These are the ones worth settling before the board picks a number.

| Question | Why it matters | How to answer it |
|---|---|---|
| Who are the 22–27% of Democratic voters who voted No, and what would move them? | They are the entire swing; Republicans did not grow the No vote | A post-election survey with ward identifiers and focus groups in City of Middleton Wds 5–8/21 and Village Wds 1–6 |
| Why did roll-off nearly double in the City and Town of Middleton but halve in the Village and Madison? | About 550 lost votes, possibly fixable by layout or instructions | Get 2022 and 2026 sample ballots from all eight clerks and map where the question sat in each ballot style |
| What will the 2027–29 and 2029–31 state budgets do now that Democrats hold the governorship and both chambers? | A revenue-limit or aid increase shrinks the 2030 ask; a token one kills the "state won't fix it" message while leaving the ask intact | Track the budget; model three state scenarios before the 2029 survey ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Wisconsin_gubernatorial_election); [Civic Media](https://civicmedia.us/news/2026/09/21/middleton-referendum-discussion-highlights-tension-between-property-taxes-and-school-funding)) |
| Does the ratified partial-veto amendment touch the $325-a-year increases already running to 2425? | That increase underpins the baseline the referendum sits on | A legal read from the Wisconsin Association of School Boards (WASB), DPI or the Legislative Fiscal Bureau |
| Renew as non-recurring again, or convert part to recurring? | Each non-recurring renewal starts at the last ceiling ($34.6M in 2030) and escalates; only 3 of 8 recurring operating questions passed in Nov. 2026 | Test both in the survey; model the levy path |
| What does a household's bill look like if it fails? | An organized No campaign will say "vote no and save about $1,000" | Have the district publish side-by-side pass and fail budgets and bills, as voters asked |
| Were the $5M in promised cuts delivered, and where? | It is the best answer to "room to cut" and "continuous ask" | An annual public cuts tracker |
| Why did the Town of Cross Plains slice flip to No, and why did the Village core fall to 51.8%? | The Village is now the closest large unit in the district | Local listening sessions; check for local grievances such as building, boundary or transportation issues |
| How should the next survey be weighted? | Raw 66%, weighted ~47%, actual 60.1% | Calibrate weights against 2026 ward results and turnout |
| Which election date? | Spring cuts passage odds about 23 points in Wisconsin data ([Kogan et al. 2018](https://joeornstein.github.io/pols-4641/readings/Kogan%20et%20al.%20-%202018%20-%20Election%20Timing,%20Electorate%20Composition,%20and%20Policy%20Outcomes.pdf)) | Default to Nov. 5, 2030; treat April 2030 only as a deliberate first try |
| Who will sit on the board that writes the 2030 question? | Two Area seats convert to open seats in April 2027 ([Open Seats MCPASD](../index.html)) | Recruit candidates who can speak to tax-weary homeowners |

The timing logic deserves emphasis. The 2026 authority runs through 2030-31, so the natural renewal date is **Nov. 5, 2030**, another midterm. In Wisconsin data midterms carry no significant penalty against presidential years, while spring electorates hold about **10 points more voters over 65** and measures there are about **23 points less likely to pass** ([Kogan, Lavertu & Peskowitz 2018](https://joeornstein.github.io/pols-4641/readings/Kogan%20et%20al.%20-%202018%20-%20Election%20Timing,%20Electorate%20Composition,%20and%20Policy%20Outcomes.pdf)). The Wisconsin Association of School Boards' Dan Rossmiller has noted that when a non-recurring referendum expires, "very typically the school district is coming to ask for a bigger amount in the subsequent ask" ([WPR, 2020](https://www.wpr.org/wisconsin-voters-approved-84-percent-school-referendums-election)). MCPASD is living that cycle, and voters have noticed it.

## Ten ideas the campaign has not yet tried

**Run a persuasion program, not only a turnout program.** The 2026 field plan worked lists of voters already rated likely Yes ([Referendum canvass volunteer plan](Referendum%20canvass%20volunteer%20plan.md)). That is the right list for turnout and the wrong one for the votes that slipped away. The losses came from Democratic-leaning homeowners in high-value city wards and the Village. A 2030 program should build a second universe of Democratic-voting, older, long-tenured homeowners in City of Middleton Wards 5–8/21 and 9–13/19/22/24 and Village Wards 1–6. It should reach them with a different script and different messengers: retirees, longtime neighbors and realtors. The scale argument is simple. The City of Middleton casts **47% of all referendum votes**. A three-point recovery there adds about **670 votes to the margin**, more than every rural town's referendum vote combined could plausibly move.

**Turn "fix it at the state" from the opposition's line into the campaign's cause.** Survey non-supporters asked for state-level solutions. Opponents asked why the district was not lobbying lawmakers. The board president's spouse, Senate Democratic Leader Dianne Hesselbein, has promised special-education and general-aid action "in January" ([Civic Media](https://civicmedia.us/news/2026/09/21/middleton-referendum-discussion-highlights-tension-between-property-taxes-and-school-funding)). A visible 2027–29 resident campaign aimed at the Capitol would give tax-weary Democrats a way to be pro-school and anti-levy at the same time. The campaign should pair it with a public ask of the board: if the state raises aid or revenue limits, pledge to levy less than the full referendum authority. That turns the "continuous ask" critique into a shared goal.

**Pre-empt the argument nobody made this time.** The 2026 Yes coalition never faced an organized opponent, and No still reached 40%. A 2030 opposition with a budget would lead with the **roughly $1,060 cut on a $500,000 home** that failure implies. The campaign should test its answer now. That means saying what $24.9M–$34.6M in cuts would do to class sizes, buildings and home values, and supporting the side-by-side pass-and-fail budget that skeptical voters asked for.

**Lock one tax number, on a real house, before the board votes.** Two sets of figures ($237 and $120 per $100K), a final-year headline and a four-year headline, plus a broadcast error, all reached voters in 2026. Next time, agree with the district on a single figure for an *actual median home at post-revaluation assessed value*. Hand reporters a one-page sheet on the day of the board vote, correct errors within 24 hours, and offer an online calculator that asks for a household's own assessed value. That answers "Who has a $100,000 house?" before anyone asks it. It also helps to remember that seniors who resist state spending are far more willing to fund their *own* district when housing values and local ties are at stake ([Balsdon & Brunner 2003](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2003-24.pdf)). The tax message to them should lead with home values and neighbors, not inflation charts.

**Treat the back of the ballot as an access problem, with every clerk, in writing, by August.** By the campaign's own internal (unverified) record, it asked six clerks for a neutral "check both sides" reminder and only the City of Madison agreed. Madison Wards 118–119 were among the places where roll-off *fell* (4.9% to 3.5%). That is suggestive, not proof, since the Village also fell without any known reminder. In 2030, ask **all eight** municipal clerks early. Request reminder language on the ballot instructions, absentee envelopes and poll-worker scripts, and put "flip your ballot" on every sign rider, hanger and post.

**Use the unclaimed turfs as a free experiment.** By the campaign's internal tracker, only 25 of 45 turfs were claimed, and roughly 849 targeted doors were never visited. Once Wisconsin Elections Commission (WEC) voter history updates, export the list and compare turnout in worked and unworked turfs. The comparison is not randomized, but it is the only local evidence the campaign will ever have on whether its doors moved votes. It should decide how 2030 volunteer hours get spent.

**Go where the newcomers live.** Apartment buildings were skipped in 2026, according to the campaign tracker. The growth areas, meanwhile, behaved very differently from one another. The Town of Middleton grew and held, the new Village Ward 7 voted 63.6% Yes, and growth around City Ward 24 broke toward No. A newcomer program would reach renters, who pay the levy through their rent, and new subdivisions with "your schools, your vote, it's on the back" materials. It would close the gap between a district whose enrollment is growing again ([MCPASD board recap](https://www.mcpasd.k12.wi.us/o/mcpasd/article/2975849)) and an electorate whose Yes vote is not.

**Publish an annual "what your referendum bought, and what we cut" report.** "The continuous ask" is the most effective line the opposition has. The best answer is four years of receipts, not four weeks of yard signs. Tie the report to the promised $5M in cuts, the class sizes the district says it protected, and the tax-rate record, which is truthfully better than the 2022 projections.

**Study the Town of Middleton before writing any 2030 script.** It is the one large community that stayed true to the partisan baseline while growing 14%. Whatever explains it, whether younger families in new subdivisions, school proximity or something else, is the best local model for what works. A dozen interviews there are worth more than another statewide poll.

**Keep the network warm through the April 2027 board races, and weigh a presidential-year option.** The conversion of two Area seats to open seats gives the Yes coalition a reason to stay organized in 2027 ([Open Seats MCPASD](../index.html)). The board those races produce will decide the size and structure of the 2030 question. One option is worth modelling for **Nov. 7, 2028**, the highest-turnout, most parent-heavy electorate available: converting part of the base to recurring authority to break the four-year cycle. It is a harder ask in this climate and invites a "stacking" critique, so it is speculative. It still deserves a spreadsheet before it is dismissed.

## Conclusion

The 2026 vote shows that MCPASD's referendum coalition is not a partisan coalition. It is a tax-trust coalition built on a Democratic base, and that base's loyalty now depends on believing the bill is fair, the district is frugal and the state is to blame. Each cycle since 2018 has asked for more and kept a little less (69%, then 63%, then 60%). This time the attrition happened without an organized opponent, inside a Democratic wave, in the county where Democrats are most reliable. The cushion is real, but it was earned against a non-opponent, and the 2030 question will start at $34.6M.

The new Democratic state government is the variable that cuts both ways. A meaningful aid or revenue-limit increase could shrink the 2030 ask and blunt "continuous ask" fatigue. A token one would weaken the campaign's central argument, that the state won't fix it, while leaving most of the ask in place. The campaign that wins in 2030 will treat the four years in between as the campaign. That means pressing the Capitol, posting the receipts, fixing the ballot and persuading Middleton's skeptical Democrats long before anyone prints a yard sign.
