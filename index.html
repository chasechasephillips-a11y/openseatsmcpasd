No mystery

Exactly how the night goes

Most people have never been to an Annual Meeting. It's far less intimidating than it sounds, and we're starting with pizza — here's the whole night, start to finish.

  • 6:30 PM · PIZZA TAILGATE
    Come early and eat. Pizza outside the Middleton High School Performing Arts Center, 2100 Bristol Street, free parking on site. Grab a slice, say hi, and walk in with people instead of alone — which is exactly why we start at 6:30. There's nothing you need to bring.
  • SEATING · WITH OR WITHOUT KIDS
    It's an auditorium, so there's room to spread out. Kids are welcome in the room — sit near an aisle if you think you'll need a lap or a hallway walk, and nobody will blink. This is a meeting about their schools.
  • SIGNING IN
    No photo ID required. You sign a short statement that you're an eligible elector — 18 or older, a US citizen, and living in the district for at least 28 days. That's the whole check. If you live here and you vote here, you're in.
  • 7:00 PM · MEETING OPENS
    First up is the district's annual budget review. The district estimates 10 to 15 minutes, and we're genuinely glad to sit through it — it's our budget too. You just sit. Nobody is called on.
  • THE MOTION
    The petition comes to the floor. Chase makes the motion, and it needs a second from someone in the room. If you'd be willing to be that person, text 336-207-1832 so we're not scanning the room for a raised hand.
  • DISCUSSION
    The floor opens. Anyone with concerns speaks first — on purpose. Chase speaks last, because meeting rules give her one turn and she'd rather answer what's actually raised than guess at it in advance. You are not expected to speak. Most people won't, and that's completely normal.
  • THE VOTE
    Show of hands or paper ballot, depending on how the meeting runs it. A simple majority of the electors present carries it. This is the moment. Raise your hand.
  • ~7:45 PM · HOME
    That's it. A rule set in 1963, finally put to the people it applies to — and you were in the room for it. Local press is expected to cover the meeting, so how full that room is becomes part of the story either way.

The 60-second case

Same board. Wider bench.

All 9 MCPASD board seats are locked to a geographic Area — lines drawn in 1963, when Middleton and Cross Plains merged. A qualified neighbor who lives three blocks outside the Area with an open seat simply cannot run. This petition keeps 5 seats anchored, so every Area keeps a guaranteed voice, and opens 4 to any district resident.

It isn't just who can run. It's how often.

Board terms are staggered, so only a few seats come up each spring. Because the 1963 map gave one part of the district four of the nine seats, your address doesn't just decide which seat you could run for — it decides which years you're allowed to run at all.

If you live in what was the
City of Middleton in 1963
You can run almost every year.

That area holds four of the nine seats, so there is nearly always one on the ballot — and in some years, two at once. If you're a parent there who decides this is the year you want to serve, the door is open.

If you live in roughly
half the district
You're barred two years out of every three.

Your area has one seat, and it comes up once every three years. In the other two, you are not simply unlikely to win — you are legally ineligible to file. Same district, same taxes, same schools, same kids.

The map behind that rule was drawn in 1963. Middleton had fewer than 5,000 people and most of the subdivisions we live in today hadn't been built yet. Do 1963's boundaries and 1963's population assumptions still make sense as the thing deciding who may run for school board — and in which years?

What it does

  • Keeps 5 geographic seats — one per Area, guaranteed voice for every neighborhood
  • Opens 4 seats to any district resident, regardless of address
  • Gives voters real choices — in April 2025 and April 2026, not one race was contested
  • Follows Wisconsin statute step by step — attorney-reviewed

What it doesn't do

  • Add or remove seats — the board stays at 9
  • Remove any current board member or shorten a term
  • Change who votes — all district electors still elect every seat
  • Happen overnight — seats convert at natural re-election, starting Spring 2027

Every other large Wisconsin district already did this — see the table

Verona, right next door, runs 3 area + 4 at-large — the exact hybrid proposed here. Elmbrook runs 4 area + 3 at-large. Madison, Kenosha, Appleton, Waukesha, Eau Claire, Sun Prairie and Stevens Point have all moved to at-large or hybrid models. MCPASD is the only large Wisconsin district where every single seat is still locked to an Area.

How the rollout works — nobody loses a seat

April 2027: two Area seats reaching natural re-election convert to open seats.
April 2028: a third converts.
April 2030: the fourth completes the transition — 5 area anchors + 4 open = 9 total.

No incumbent is removed, no term is shortened, and current members can run for either an anchor seat or an open seat. See the seat-by-seat handout →

What the school board actually does — and why the bench matters

The board hires, supports and evaluates the superintendent — the single biggest lever they pull. They approve the budget under state revenue caps, decide whether to put a referendum on the ballot, adopt policy, and represent families to the district. With growth, revenue-cap pressure and a likely referendum ahead, that's a lot to ask of nine volunteers. The deeper the pool of capable neighbors eligible to step up, the better our schools are governed.

This is about expanding the pool, not replacing the people in it. The neighbors serving today have earned our gratitude — and their path forward stays fully intact.

We're not changing who serves — we're widening who can. The next great trustee shouldn't be ruled out by which Area line their house sits inside.

Straight answers

Questions people actually ask

Can I vote if I don't have kids in the district?

Yes. Any MCPASD elector — an adult US citizen who has lived in the district for at least 28 days — can vote at the Annual Meeting. Having kids in the schools is not required.

What do I need to bring?

Nothing. There's no photo ID requirement at an Annual Meeting. You sign a short statement at the door confirming you're an eligible elector — 18 or older, a US citizen, and a resident of the district for at least the past 28 days. Then you sit down. That's it.

Will I have to speak or know anything?

No. You can sit quietly the entire time and simply raise your hand when the vote is called. That's the whole job, and it's a real one.

Can I vote by proxy, absentee, or online?

No — and this is the part that catches people. Wisconsin's Annual Meeting requires electors to be physically present. There is no absentee, proxy, or online option. Attendance is the vote.

How many people does it actually take?

A simple majority of the electors present. About 30 people attended in 2025. So it doesn't take hundreds — it takes enough committed neighbors to be the clear majority in the room. The other side can turn people out too, which is exactly why margin matters. Bring someone.

Do I have to run for anything?

No. None of the organizers are candidates either. This widens who's allowed on the ballot so more good neighbors could run if they ever wanted to.

Is this partisan, or aimed at a board member?

Neither. No party, no PAC, no candidate, and it's not aimed at anyone serving. Every current member keeps their full term and can run again. It's a structural fix to who's eligible — not a referendum on any person or policy.

Does where I live really change whether I can run?

Yes, and it changes when too. Seats are staggered across three-year terms, so only a handful come up each spring. The area that was the City of Middleton in 1963 holds four of the nine seats, so somebody living there has a seat to run for almost every year — sometimes two in the same election. The areas with a single seat get one shot every three years, which means residents of roughly half the district are legally ineligible to run for school board two years out of every three.

Middleton had fewer than 5,000 residents in 1963 and most of today's neighborhoods hadn't been built. The lines never moved. Opening four seats means a qualified neighbor can step up in the year they're actually ready, instead of waiting for their address's turn.

Will my Area lose representation?

No. Every Area keeps a permanent anchor seat — one per Area, guaranteed. The petition opens four additional seats district-wide; your neighborhood's anchor representation stays intact.

Will the board still reflect the diversity of our families?

It's a fair question and we won't hand-wave it. Research shows opening seats can reduce a group's representation mainly where that community is sizable and geographically concentrated. In a more dispersed district like ours, the Area lines do little to protect representation that way. The design also protects it directly: five seats stay anchored, and hybrids kept mostly to single-member seats represent about as well as all-district systems. The clearer effect of opening seats is simply more candidates and real choices. We'd welcome a full demographic review before anything is adopted.

Can I bring my kids?

Yes. Children are welcome in the room. This is a public meeting about their school district, and a wiggly kid in row six is not going to derail anything — bring snacks, headphones, a tablet, whatever gets everyone through 45 minutes. A fair number of us will be walking in with children.

If you'd genuinely rather have a sitter, neighbors have offered. And if a ride is the obstacle, we can cover that too — text Chase at 336-207-1832 or email chase.chasephillips@gmail.com. Don't let logistics be the reason we're one hand short.

Why couldn't this be done any other way?

Under Wis. Stat. § 120.02(2)(a), the only way to change apportionment in a common school district is a citizen petition followed by a vote at the district Annual Meeting. No board, superintendent or PAC can do it. The 1963 structure has held simply because nobody had organized a petition until now.
