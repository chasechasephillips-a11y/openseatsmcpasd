How should I vote on Nov. 3? On the school question: YES.

If you live in the Middleton–Cross Plains Area School District, there's a school funding question on your ballot. It renews money our schools already run on, which runs out after this school year. Here's the whole thing in plain English, with the real tax numbers.

The 30-second version

A renewal, not a new ask

  1. In 2022, 63% of voters approved four years of school operating funding. It runs out after this school year (June 2027).
  2. This question renews it for four more years. $24.9 million in 2027–28, the same as this year, then stepping up with inflation to $34.6 million by 2030–31.
  3. It pays for the everyday stuff: teachers and staff, small class sizes, safety, arts, athletics and activities. It doesn't pay for new buildings.
  4. If it fails, the district loses about a fifth of its operating budget starting in fall 2027.

Why do we have to vote on this at all?

Wisconsin caps how much each school district can collect from state aid and local property taxes combined. It's called the revenue limit, and it's been in place since 1993. The only way past it is a local vote.

The cap's yearly increases haven't kept up with inflation. District leaders put the gap at nearly $3,600 per student, or about $25.4 million for MCPASD. That's why more than 70 Wisconsin school districts have referendums on ballots this fall. We're not special. We're just next.

Property tax myths, busted

What you heard at the bus stop vs. what's true

Myth

"It's a new tax on top of the 2022 one."

Reality

It's a renewal. The 2022 referendum ends after this school year, and this one replaces it. It doesn't stack on top. Year one is $24.9 million, the same as this year, so year one adds $0 to your bill.

Source: MCPASD; Wisconsin Examiner, Sept. 21, 2026

Myth

"A renewal means my taxes won't go up at all."

Reality

We'd love to tell you that. It isn't true. After year one the amount steps up with inflation. The district estimates about $64 more per year per $100,000 of value by year four, compared with today. On a $450,000 home, that's about $288 a year, or $24 a month.

Source: MCPASD Board of Education announcement, Aug. 2026

Myth

"My assessment went up, so the schools are getting a windfall."

Reality

State law caps what the district can collect, no matter what homes are worth. When values rise, the same total gets spread over more value, so the rate per $1,000 goes down. Your share depends on how your home's value changed compared with everyone else's.

Source: Wisconsin DPI, revenue limits and equalized levy rates

Myth

"Our school taxes are already the highest around."

Reality

MCPASD's school tax rate for 2025–26 was $8.62 per $1,000 of property value. Sun Prairie's was $10.21 and Verona's was $10.66. Ours is among the lower rates in Dane County.

Source: Wisconsin DPI, FY 2025–26 equalized levy rates

Myth

"Vote no and the state will have to step up."

Reality

Nobody at the Capitol is waiting on our vote. This year's state deal on property taxes and school funding died in the Senate in May. Even when the state adds aid, the revenue limit means it mostly lowers property taxes rather than adding classroom dollars. A No vote here doesn't bring money from the state. It just means a ~20% cut here.

Source: Wisconsin Examiner, May 13 & Sept. 21, 2026 (citing the Legislative Fiscal Bureau)

Myth

"They should cut the fat first."

Reality

They are. The district plans about $5 million in cuts over the next four years even if this passes. Without it, the cut is about 20% of the operating budget, and that comes out of classrooms, not fat.

Source: MCPASD, as reported by local news, Sept. 2026

The honest trade-off

What a No vote saves, and what it costs

We're not going to pretend a No vote saves you nothing. It does. Here's both sides of the ledger.

If it fails, you save

Roughly $2 for every $1,000 of home value in 2027–28, at today's values. That's about $900 a year on a $450,000 home, and more in later years.

Our math: $24.9M ÷ about $11.7B of district property value (DPI, 2025–26).

If it fails, the schools lose

About 20% of the district's operating budget, starting fall 2027. District leaders say that means larger class sizes, cuts to programs and activities, and possible school consolidation.

You get to decide whether that trade is worth it. We don't think it's close.

Questions neighbors ask

FAQ

Didn't we just vote on this?

Yes, in 2022, and 63% said yes. That referendum was four years long by design. It runs out after this school year, so it's back on the ballot.

Is this for new buildings?

No. It's an operating referendum, for day-to-day costs like staff, programs, safety and upkeep. It doesn't borrow money or build anything new.

Why not make it permanent?

It's deliberately temporary. It ends after 2030–31, and then voters get to weigh in again. That keeps the district accountable to us.

I don't have kids in school. Why should I vote yes?

Strong schools are a big reason people want to live here, and that demand is part of what holds up home values. They're also where the people who'll staff our clinics, run our businesses, and coach our Little League are growing up right now.

I'm on a fixed income. Is there any help?

Possibly. Wisconsin's Homestead Credit helps lower-income homeowners and renters with property taxes, and the state's school property tax credit can take up to $300 off your state income tax. Details and eligibility are at revenue.wi.gov (search "Homestead Credit").

Is the school district running this site?

No. School districts can share facts but can't tell you how to vote. This site is run by local residents and volunteers. Every number here comes from the district, the state, or news reporting, and the sources are listed below.

Where does "$64 per $100,000" come from?

It's the district's own estimate, based on current projections, for year four compared with today. The real number will depend on property values, state aid and enrollment. Want the district's full explanation? Watch its referendum video.
