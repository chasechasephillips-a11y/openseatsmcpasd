Speaking last.

You get one turn, after everyone else. That is the best seat in the room if you use it right, and a trap if you try to answer everything. This is built to be held in one hand at a podium.

Tue Sept 22 · Middleton High School Performing Arts Center, 2100 Bristol St · pizza 6:30 · meeting 7:00 · budget review first (10–15 min) · your motion, a second, then discussion.

1 · Before you stand up

While others speak, you are not rehearsing. You are taking notes. Three columns on a blank page:

  1. Name + concern. Literally write down who said what. Using someone's name back to them is the single most disarming thing you can do in a room like this.
  2. True / not true. Mark which concerns are legitimate. Some will be. Those are the ones you concede, and conceding them is what makes the rest of you credible.
  3. Did it move the room? Watch faces, not speakers. You only need to answer what actually landed. Two or three things, maximum.
The trap: trying to rebut every point raised. It reads as defensive, it burns your one turn, and it makes small objections look bigger by treating them as equals. Answer what moved the room. Let the rest die on its own.

2 · The shape of your three minutes

Keep it under three minutes. You've made the case for four months; tonight you're closing, not presenting.

  1. Thank the room, and mean it. Including the people who spoke against. They showed up to a school district annual meeting on a Tuesday. That's the behavior you're trying to create more of.
  2. Concede the real thing. One sentence. "[Name] is right that…" It costs you nothing and buys you everything.
  3. Answer the two that landed. Short. Specific. No throat-clearing.
  4. Correct any factual error, once, gently. Only if it's material. "Just to clarify one thing…"
  5. Close on your throughline. Not on the rebuttal. The last thing they hear before voting should be yours, not theirs.

3 · Three throughlines — pick by room

Read the room during discussion and choose. Don't try to use more than one.

A. If the room is warm and mostly persuaded

The invitation This was never a complaint about the people serving. It's an invitation to the ones who aren't. Every one of us knows somebody who'd be good at this and can't run — not because voters rejected them, but because of a line on a map drawn before most of our houses existed. Tonight we can just… let them try.

B. If the room is skeptical or the opposition landed

The narrow ask I want to be precise about what you're voting on, because it's smaller than it sounds. Nine seats stays nine seats. Every area keeps a guaranteed representative. No one on this board loses anything, and every current member can run again. Voters still elect every single seat. The only thing that changes is whether your address disqualifies you from filing. That's it. That's the whole vote.

C. If "why change it" is the dominant mood

The 1963 question Nobody in this room chose this system. It was set in 1963, when Middleton had fewer than five thousand people and most of our neighborhoods were farmland. It has never once been put to the people who live under it. We're not the generation that made this rule. We're just the first one that's been asked. Whatever you decide tonight, at least it'll finally be decided by us.

4 · Likely objections, and what to say

Spoken language, not written. Say them out loud once tonight so they sound like you.

"Why change something that isn't broken?"
Concede: it's a fair instinct.

I'd ask what "working" means. In the last several springs we've had almost no contested races — the number of candidates matched the number of seats. That's not a sign of a healthy system, it's a sign of a small pool. If the only people who can run are the ones who happen to live in the right area in the right year, we're not choosing from our best. We're choosing from whoever's eligible.

"This is aimed at someone on the board." / "This is political."

No. And I want to be direct about it: no member loses a seat, no term gets shortened, and every one of them can run again — for an anchor seat or an open one. There's no party here, no PAC, no money, and none of us organizing this are candidates. If I wanted to beat somebody, this would be a spectacularly inefficient way to do it. It takes until 2030 to fully phase in.

"My area will lose its voice." / "Rural areas get drowned out by the city."
Concede: this is the most legitimate concern raised tonight. Say so.

That's exactly why five seats stay anchored. Every area keeps a permanent, guaranteed representative — that never goes away. What's actually true right now is the reverse of the worry: the areas with a single seat can only run once every three years, while the area that was the City of Middleton in 1963 holds four of nine and can run almost every year. Opening four seats district-wide gives the smaller areas a path in the years they currently have none.

"At-large seats favor people with money and name recognition."
Concede: there's real research behind this. Don't wave it off.

It's a documented effect and I won't pretend otherwise. Two things. One, it's why we kept five seats anchored instead of going fully at-large — a hybrid that stays mostly single-member performs about like a district system. Two, the alternative isn't a level playing field, it's a field where most people can't step on at all. And we'd welcome the district doing a full review before anything is adopted. I'd rather get this right than just win.

"Why not just recruit better candidates instead of changing the rules?"

We've tried. I can tell you about specific neighbors — capable, civic-minded people who wanted to serve — and the conversation ended at "you live three blocks the wrong way." You can't recruit your way around an eligibility rule. Recruiting is the work either way; this just means the recruiting isn't confined to one part of the map in any given year.

"Only a few hundred people signed. That's not a mandate."

It's not meant to be a mandate — it's the threshold the statute sets to get the question to this room. The mandate is what happens in the next ten minutes. That's the design, and it's why I'd rather lose this vote than have it decided anywhere but here.

"This feels rushed." / "Why now?"

Sixty-three years is a strange thing to call rushed. But I hear the real question, which is whether people had a chance to look at it. It's been public since spring, the full proposal and FAQ have been online the whole time, and neighbors collected signatures in person, door by door, all summer.

"Who's paying for this?"

Parents. Printing and a domain name, out of our own pockets. There's no PAC, no outside organization, nothing for sale, and nobody's collecting donations. Happy to show anyone the receipts, though I'll warn you it's mostly pizza.

5 · Facts to keep straight

Board size9 seats, unchanged. 5 area anchors + 4 open.
Phase-in2027 (two convert), 2028 (a third), 2030 (the fourth). Only at natural re-election.
StatuteWis. Stat. § 120.02(2)(a). Citizen petition + Annual Meeting vote is the only route.
Peer districtsVerona: 3 area + 4 at-large. Elmbrook: 4 area + 3 at-large. Madison, Kenosha, Appleton, Waukesha, Eau Claire, Sun Prairie, Stevens Point: at-large or hybrid.
The asymmetryOld City of Middleton area holds 4 of 9 seats — a seat nearly every year, sometimes two. Single-seat areas: once every three years.
1963 populationSay "fewer than five thousand." Don't give a precise number.
⚠️ Verify before you cite: our own materials disagree on the recent election numbers. One page says "four candidates for four seats in 2025, three for three in 2026"; another says "all three open seats in April 2025 had a single candidate." If someone in that room has the actual clerk's records and you cite the wrong version, you lose the room on a detail that doesn't matter. Safe phrasing: "in recent years we've had almost no contested races." Only use specific counts if you've confirmed them today.

6 · Do not

  • Do not characterize anyone's motives. Not one word about why someone opposes it.
  • Do not say "actually" or "with respect." Both read as a countdown to an insult.
  • Do not relitigate the petition process, signature counts, or anything procedural. Dead weight.
  • Do not answer a question you don't know. "I don't know — I'll find out" is a strong move, not a weak one.
  • Do not run long. The room's goodwill has a half-life and it started at 7:00.

7 · The last line

Have one sentence loaded and end on it deliberately. Then stop talking and sit down — the silence after a clean ending does more work than another paragraph.

If you only remember one thing We're not changing who serves. We're widening who can.
