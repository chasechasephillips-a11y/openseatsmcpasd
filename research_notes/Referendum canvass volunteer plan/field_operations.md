## Q1. Pace benchmarks: doors per hour for knock-and-talk vs. lit drop/door hanger, walkable suburban vs. large-lot/rural turf, and contacts per hour

### Takeaway
Industry and practitioner sources agree that knock-and-talk runs about **15–20 doors/hour** in ideal conditions. They advise planning for **10–15 doors/hour** once travel, briefing and breaks are counted. Suburban driveways slow this to **15–18**, and rural or large-lot turf to **8–12**. A lit drop with no conversation is commonly described as covering **3–4× as many homes** in the same time. No reputable source gives a hard lit-drop rate, so roughly 40–70 doors/hour is an inference. Expect only about **1 conversation per ~6 doors per pass**, which is roughly **3–6 contacts/hour** for a volunteer knocking at a typical pace.

### Cited Findings
**Knock-and-talk pace**
- Expect 15–20 doors/hour when canvassing. Plan for 10–15 doors/hour once travel, training and breaks are included. The figure counts raw knocks, not conversations ("You may knock 30 doors but speak with only 15 people") — [Qomon, "How Many Doors per Hour in Canvassing?"](https://qomon.com/resources/blog/article/canvassing-doors-per-hour) **[industry]**
- Pace by density: apartment buildings and row houses 25+ doors/hour; suburban neighborhoods with driveways 15–18; rural areas 8–12 — [Qomon](https://qomon.com/resources/blog/article/canvassing-doors-per-hour) **[industry]**
- About 2–4 minutes per door on average, including walking, knocking and notes. Qomon's calculator assumes 3 minutes per door for traditional canvassing and 15 for deep canvassing. A 25–30-door list takes 45–90 minutes — [Qomon, "How Long Does Canvassing Take?"](https://qomon.com/resources/blog/article/canvassing-duration); [Qomon canvassing calculator](https://qomon.com/ecosystem/canvassing-calculator) **[industry]**
- Qomon pages contradict each other on ideal shift length: one says the standard shift is 2–3 hours, another says 4–5 hours is optimal, and a third says most campaigns use 3-hour blocks with about 2 hours of actual knocking — [Qomon](https://qomon.com/resources/blog/article/canvassing-duration) **[industry; internally inconsistent]**
- A canvasser can average 18–23 doors an hour — [Canvassing King, "The Benchmarks & Numbers You Should Know" (2015)](https://canvassking.com/blog/2015/04/04/the-benchmarks-numbers-you-should-know/) **[industry; older]**
- One to three minutes per door, which lets a canvasser visit about 20 doors per hour — [Local Victory, door-to-door campaign guide](https://www.localvictory.com/grassroots/door-to-door-campaign-guide.html) **[industry/practitioner]**
- The average canvassing **pair** knocks about **40–45 doors per shift** of roughly 2–2.5 hours — [Indivisible, MiniVAN Mobile Canvassing Guide](https://indivisible.org/resource/minivan/) **[practitioner]**. *Uncertain: the extract does not say whether the pair walks together. If it does, that is about 16–22 doors/hour per pair, or about 8–11 per person-hour.*
- A Michigan campaign tells volunteers to expect at least 25 doors in a couple of hours, and sometimes 50+ over 2–3 hours — [Abdul for Senate, self-directed canvass shifts](https://go.abdulforsenate.com/self-directed-canvass-shifts) **[practitioner]**

**Lit drop / door-hanger pace**
- Lit drops are faster because volunteers don't stop to talk. Volunteers "can hit 3-4 times as many homes in the same amount of time" as knocking — [Local Victory](https://www.localvictory.com/grassroots/door-to-door-campaign-guide.html) **[industry/practitioner; roughly 11 years old, rule of thumb]**
- In a statewide Michigan experiment, door hangers were cost-effective partly because hanging literature on a doorknob takes very little time and volunteers can move from door to door very quickly — [Nickerson, Friedrichs & King (2006), *Political Research Quarterly*](https://sites.temple.edu/nickerson/files/2017/07/Nickerson_Friedrichs_King.PRQ2005.pdf) **[peer-reviewed]**. No per-hour figure was retrieved.
- Tarrant County (TX) Democrats' GOTV lit-drop program loads about 76 doors per list. Volunteers drop the literature, mark the door complete in the app and move on. No per-hour rate is given — [Tarrant County Democrats, GOTV Lit Drop Program](https://tarrantdemocrats.org/gotv-lit-drop-program/) **[practitioner]**
- Two people can cover about 100 homes in a relatively short 10-block walk by splitting the two sides of the street. No time window is stated — [SC Women Lead, "Guide to Canvassing"](https://scwomenlead.net/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Guide-to-Canvassing.pdf) **[practitioner]**

**Contacts (conversations) per hour**
- Green & Gerber: multiple attempts are often needed to get contact rates above 25%. Canvassers may reach only **one voter out of every six doors on each pass** through a walk list, especially with outdated lists or secured buildings — [Green & Gerber, *Get Out the Vote* (book PDF excerpt)](https://groups.io/g/psychologyonline/attachment/219/0/Get%20out%20the%20vote%20%20how%20to%20increase%20voter%20turnout.pdf) **[academic book]**
- The 2004 edition of *Get Out the Vote* assumed canvassers reach **12 people an hour** at $16/hour, giving about $19 per additional vote — [Chronicle of Philanthropy (2004)](https://www.philanthropy.com/news/cost-of-door-to-door-canvassing-is-19-per-vote-research-finds/). A later write-up quotes the authors as assuming **6 voters contacted per hour** at $16/hour and **1 vote per 14 contacts**, or about $29 per vote — [rc3.org quoting Farhad Manjoo (2008)](https://rc3.org/2008/10/28/text-messaging-as-a-marketing-tool/) **[secondary reports of an academic book; the 12 vs. 6 contacts/hour assumption differs by edition — verify against the current edition]**

**Yield per door: why pace is not the only metric**
- Door hangers (partisan, Michigan 2002, 13 sites pooled): **+1.6 percentage points** turnout (SE 0.7), or about **12 new votes per 1,000 flyers attempted** — [Nickerson, Friedrichs & King](https://sites.temple.edu/nickerson/files/2017/07/Nickerson_Friedrichs_King.PRQ2005.pdf) **[peer-reviewed]**. A related Harvard working paper reports about 1.3% for households where a hanger was delivered — [HKS working paper](https://appext.hks.harvard.edu/publications/getFile.aspx?Id=120) **[academic/other]**
- Nonpartisan leafleting (Gerber & Green 2000): no effect among registered Republicans or Democrats, but about a 7% turnout gain among voters unaffiliated with a major party — [ISPS summary, "The Effect of a Nonpartisan Get-Out-the-Vote Drive: An Experimental Study of Leafletting"](https://isps.yale.edu/node/19712) **[peer-reviewed; unclear from the extract whether this is 7 percentage points or 7% relative — verify]**
- A meta-analysis of leaflets and mail found partisan messages had a statistically insignificant **−0.2 pp** effect on turnout (Green, McGrath & Aronow 2013), as summarized in search extracts citing the meta-analysis — [Nickerson et al. PDF / search extract](https://sites.temple.edu/nickerson/files/2017/07/Nickerson_Friedrichs_King.PRQ2005.pdf) **[peer-reviewed meta-analysis; secondhand]**
- Door-to-door canvassing (71-study meta-analysis, Green et al. 2013): **+2.54 pp** average turnout among treated households, vs. **+1.94** for volunteer phone calls — [Minaudier (2021), citing Green et al. 2013](http://www.clementminaudier.com/Minaudier_2021_Motivations_beliefs_and_returns_to_canvassing.pdf) **[academic working paper citing peer-reviewed meta-analysis]**
- A UK party experiment (Liberal Democrats, 2017) found leaflets alone raised turnout **4.3 pp**, and canvass visits added only about **0.6 pp** on top — [Political Science Research and Methods, "Is it worth door-knocking?"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/political-science-research-and-methods/article/is-it-worth-doorknocking-evidence-from-a-united-kingdombased-get-out-the-vote-gotv-field-experiment-on-the-effect-of-party-leaflets-and-canvass-visits-on-voter-turnout/757F1AF7D16F43A444BDDE55181471B5) **[peer-reviewed; non-US, so generalization is uncertain]**

### Inferences
- **Planning rates for this turf** (my synthesis of the ranges above, not a sourced figure):
  - Walkable single-family Middleton/Cross Plains blocks: about 12–18 doors/hour knocking per canvasser, or about 40–70 doors/hour for lit-drop-only.
  - Downtown apartments: faster per door if inside access exists, near zero if buildings are locked.
  - Large-lot Town of Middleton subdivisions requiring driving: about 8–12 doors/hour knocking, and perhaps 20–35/hour lit drop. The second figure is my guess, scaled from the 3–4× multiplier but discounted for drive time.
- In a 2-hour shift with about 15 minutes of check-in and travel, one volunteer realistically completes about 20–35 knock doors in walkable turf, or about 75–120 lit-drop doors.
- With about 13 volunteers × 2 hours per weekend (about 26 volunteer-hours), that is roughly 300–450 knocked doors (about 75–110 conversations at 25% contact) or roughly 1,000–1,800 lit-dropped doors per weekend. These are inferences built on the industry ranges.
- A per-door comparison favors knocking where conversations happen. But doubling-to-quadrupling coverage with hangers can produce comparable total yield in mobilization terms (about 12 votes per 1,000 hangers vs. 1 vote per about 14 contacts). Neither figure was measured for a school-referendum persuasion message.

### Gaps
- No NGP VAN help-center, re:power/Wellstone, Progressive Majority or DNC manual excerpt with a lit-drop doors/hour figure was retrieved. Full-text fetches were blocked, and search extracts did not include one.
- No peer-reviewed source reports door-hanger doors/hour.
- No source distinguished knock pace on Wisconsin-style suburban lots specifically.
- The Gerber & Green contacts/hour assumption (6 vs. 12) needs verification against the current (4th) edition of *Get Out the Vote*.

## Q2. Contact (answer) rates in canvassing experiments and by day of week/time of day; best canvassing windows

### Takeaway
Classic US canvassing experiments reached only **about 14–45% of targeted voters**, averaging **around 25–30%**, even with repeat visits.

The only large time-of-day dataset found is commercial, not political (about 237,000 sales knocks). In it, answer rates **rise through the day, from 31.5% at 9 a.m. to 46.2% at 8 p.m.**. Weekday evenings (4–8 p.m.) slightly beat weekend midday, and Saturday was the weakest day.

Practitioner guidance converges on **weekday ~4–8 p.m. and weekend ~10 a.m.–early evening**. In late-October Wisconsin, though, weekday evening windows are cut short by darkness (sunset about 6:05 p.m. Oct 22, about 4:50 p.m. CST Nov 1).

### Cited Findings
**Experimental contact rates**
- **Six-city study** (Green, Gerber & Nickerson 2003, *Journal of Politics*; 2001 local elections): canvassers reached **14–45%** of treatment households depending on site. The shortfall came mostly from empty homes, bad addresses and refusals. Average ITT effect **+2.1 pp**; among those reached, **+7.1 pp** — [J-PAL, "Getting Out the Vote in Local Elections in the United States"](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/getting-out-vote-local-elections-united-states); [ISPS publication page](https://isps.yale.edu/node/19921) **[peer-reviewed / J-PAL summary]**
- Site contact rates in the paper's table: **Bridgeport 28.1%, Columbus 14.3%, Detroit 30.9%, Minneapolis 18.5%, Raleigh 44.6%**. The St. Paul row was cut off in the extract. Site "effects on voting" (likely among those contacted — verify) were 14.4, 9.7, 7.8, 10.1 and 0.2 pp respectively — [Green, Gerber & Nickerson (2003) PDF](https://sites.temple.edu/nickerson/files/2017/07/Green_Gerber_Nickerson.JOP_.2003.pdf) **[peer-reviewed]**
- **New Haven 1998** (Gerber & Green 2000, *APSR* 94(3):653–663; about 30,000 registered voters): canvassing raised turnout substantially, mail slightly, and phone calls not at all — [ISPS](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps00-001) **[peer-reviewed]**
  - Reported contact figures **conflict across versions** of the analysis: 37.4% (PNAS 1999 version); 18% contacted directly plus 11% indirectly through another household member, out of 8,721 assigned to treatment; and "only one fourth of those assigned a treatment actually received it" (a later reanalysis) — [PNAS (1999)](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.96.19.10939); [Gerber & Green APSR PDF](https://jwolf-ra.wdfiles.com/local--files/field-experiments/Canvassing%20-%20Gerber_Green_2000.pdf) **[peer-reviewed; figures uncertain — verify]**
  - Turnout was about 59% among treatment-group members who were contacted vs. 40% among those not contacted. This is a comparison of who answers, not a causal effect. Control turnout was about 44.6% — [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/pdf/10.1073/pnas.96.19.10939) **[peer-reviewed]**
  - A correction (Gerber & Green 2005) states that repairing data-processing errors leaves the substantive New Haven findings unchanged — [APSR correction](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/abs/correction-to-gerber-and-green-2000-replication-of-disputed-findings-and-reply-to-imai-2005/5FD2A691AC3ADF31291806B02E43FAFF) **[peer-reviewed]**
- **Michelson 2003** (*Political Behavior* 25(3); municipal election in rural Central California, Dos Palos): Latino canvassers were more effective at getting potential voters, especially Latinos, to **open the door**. Message content had no detectable effect on turnout. The exact contact rate was not retrieved — [Springer abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1025167607369) **[peer-reviewed]**
- A UK party GOTV experiment reported a canvass contact rate of **28%** — [PSRM](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/political-science-research-and-methods/article/is-it-worth-doorknocking-evidence-from-a-united-kingdombased-get-out-the-vote-gotv-field-experiment-on-the-effect-of-party-leaflets-and-canvass-visits-on-voter-turnout/757F1AF7D16F43A444BDDE55181471B5) **[peer-reviewed, non-US]**
- "Multiple attempts are often necessary to achieve contact rates of over 25 percent" — [Green & Gerber, *Get Out the Vote*](https://groups.io/g/psychologyonline/attachment/219/0/Get%20out%20the%20vote%20%20how%20to%20increase%20voter%20turnout.pdf) **[academic book]**
- A volunteer canvassing guide warns to expect "about a 10 percent contact rate" — [SC Women Lead, Guide to Canvassing](https://scwomenlead.net/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Guide-to-Canvassing.pdf) **[practitioner; well below experimental figures, possibly a per-pass or per-door figure]**

**Day-of-week / time-of-day**
- **Scoutdata, about 237,000 real knocks** (commercial door-to-door, not political):
  - Overall answer rate **37.0%**.
  - **Monday highest (38.1%)**, **Saturday lowest (34.7%)**. Sunday had a small sample and is directional only.
  - **4–8 p.m. window 37.6%** vs. **10 a.m.–3 p.m. 36.3%**.
  - By hour, answer rates climb from **31.5% in the 9 a.m. hour to 46.2% in the 8 p.m. hour**.
  - [Scoutdata, "Door Knocking Statistics: 237k Real Knocks"](https://scoutdata.com/guides/door-knocking-statistics) **[industry, commercial-sales data — "answer" is not the same as reaching a specific targeted voter]**
- Vendor guides recommend weekday evenings about 4–7 p.m. and weekend windows of 10 a.m.–1 p.m. or 3–6 p.m. Some claim Tue–Thu beat Mon/Fri. Expected answer rates of 30–50% are generally given without underlying data. They also note that evening windows "fall apart in winter" as daylight shrinks — [Walklists.com, "Best Time to Canvass"](https://walklists.com/blog/best-time-to-canvass); [CanvassLite](https://canvasslite.com/blog/best-time-to-knock-doors.html); [SPOTIO](https://spotio.com/blog/know-knock-maximize-canvassing-contact-rate/); [Knockbase](https://www.knockbase.com/blog/what-data-tells-us-about-the-best-times-for-door-to-door-sales) **[industry]**
- A Michigan US Senate campaign sets its canvass windows at **Mon–Fri 4–8:30 p.m.; Sat/Sun 10 a.m.–8:30 p.m.** — [Abdul for Senate, Clare canvass shifts](https://go.abdulforsenate.com/clare-canvass-shifts-any-time-9am-9pm) **[practitioner]**
- People answer most often during weekday dinner time, Saturday mornings and Sunday afternoons/evenings. Other guidance notes that dinner times vary, so any 5–8 p.m. knock risks interrupting a meal — [search extract summarizing canvassing guides incl. SPOTIO](https://spotio.com/?p=611) **[industry]**
- Some areas are best canvassed at particular times and "only experience will tell you". Canvass housing for older residents in daylight, because residents may be reluctant to open doors in the dark — [ALDC (UK Liberal Democrat councillors' association), "Voter ID: who, where and when"](https://www.aldc.org/2010/02/voter-id-who-where-and-when/) **[practitioner, non-US]**

### Inferences
- Plan on about **25–35% of targeted doors** producing a conversation on a single pass in Middleton suburbs. Saturday/Sunday afternoon shifts are probably similar to or slightly below weekday evenings, by the Scoutdata pattern. A second pass on a different day or time is the main way to raise contact above about 30%.
- For Thu/Fri shifts Oct 22–23 and Oct 29–30, the useful knock window is roughly **4:00–6:30 p.m.** (sunset about 6:05 p.m. Oct 22 and about 5:53 p.m. Oct 30; civil dusk about 6:20–6:33 p.m.). After dusk, contact rates may hold for younger households but drop for older residents.
- On weekends, **late morning through late afternoon (about 10:30 a.m.–5:00 p.m.)** fits both answer-rate patterns and daylight. On **Sun Nov 1**, clocks fall back and sunset is about 4:50 p.m. CST, so afternoon shifts should end by about 4:30 p.m.
- Lit-drop/door-hanger shifts don't depend on anyone answering. They can be scheduled in lower-answer windows (weekday daytime, Saturday morning) and in early darkness with lights. Hanging at a dark porch is less intrusive than knocking.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed analysis of political canvass contact rates by day/time was found. The six-city and New Haven papers' timing details (days/hours worked) were not retrieved.
- The St. Paul contact rate and the Michelson 2003 contact rate were not retrieved.
- No NGP VAN, re:power or Ecanvasser/CallHub page with time-window guidance was surfaced, despite targeted searches.

## Q3. Timing relative to Election Day and early voting: decay, primacy vs. recency (Panagopoulos 2011), contact during the early-voting window

### Takeaway
Most experimental evidence favors **contact close to when the voter decides or votes**.
- Nickerson (2007) found phone calls in the **final week** most effective and earlier calls largely ineffective.
- Kalla & Broockman (2018) found that persuasion from contact **long before Election Day decays**.
- Panagopoulos (2011) tested calls 4 weeks, 2 weeks and 3 days out in a low-salience municipal race. He found **small effects and no significant difference by timing**, so early contact "can also be effective". There are hints that high-propensity voters respond to earlier contact and low-propensity voters to later contact.

Wisconsin in-person absentee voting runs **Tue Oct 20 – Sun Nov 1, 2026**, so the planned Oct 22 – Nov 1 shifts fall **entirely inside the early-voting window** and in the last 12 days before the Nov 3 Election Day.

### Cited Findings
- **Panagopoulos (2011), "Timing Is Everything? Primacy and Recency Effects in Voter Mobilization Campaigns"**, *Political Behavior* 33(1):79–93 — [ISPS](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps10-044); [ResearchGate PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/225458872_Timing_Is_Everything_Primacy_and_Recency_Effects_in_Voter_Mobilization_Campaigns); [Adam Brown (BYU) summary](https://adambrown.info/p/notes/panagopoulos_timing_is_everything_primacy_and_recency_effects_in_voter_mobilization_campaigns) **[peer-reviewed; academic summary]**
  - Design: nonpartisan GOTV **phone calls** from commercial phone banks in the **2005 Rochester, NY municipal election**, delivered 4 weeks, 2 weeks or 3 days before Election Day.
  - **Conflict:** another extract describes the arms as the week of the election and 1, 2 and 3 weeks prior — verify.
  - Hypotheses: "recency" (recent reminders are top-of-mind) vs. "primacy" (early reminders raise awareness of a low-salience election).
  - Findings (Brown summary): the treatment groups together turned out only about **0.9 pp** above control, within the margin of error. There was **no significant difference across timing groups**, with the 4-week group matching the 3-day group.
  - High- and low-propensity voters responded differently. Secondary sources report earlier calls more effective for high-propensity voters and later calls for low-propensity voters — **uncertain; exact figures not retrieved**.
  - The author argues early calls "can also be effective". This challenges Nickerson (2007), whose methods he considered left its recency conclusion tentative.
- **Nickerson (2007), "Quality Is Job One: Professional and Volunteer Voter Mobilization Calls"**, *AJPS* 51(2):269–282: calls made in the **final week** were most effective, and calls before the last week were largely ineffective — [secondary descriptions in search extracts, incl. Panagopoulos PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Costas-Panagopoulos/publication/225458872_Timing_Is_Everything_Primacy_and_Recency_Effects_in_Voter_Mobilization_Campaigns/links/0046351eee89d2d217000000/Timing-Is-Everything-Primacy-and-Recency-Effects-in-Voter-Mobilization-Campaigns.pdf) **[peer-reviewed, described secondhand]**
- **Kalla & Broockman (2018), "The Minimal Persuasive Effects of Campaign Contact in General Elections: Evidence from 49 Field Experiments"**, *APSR* 112(1):148–166 — [APSR](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/minimal-persuasive-effects-of-campaign-contact-in-general-elections-evidence-from-49-field-experiments/753665A313C4AB433DBF7110299B7433); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3042867) **[peer-reviewed]**
  - The average persuasive effect of contact on candidate choice in **general elections is zero**.
  - Persuasion appears in only two rare circumstances. One is "when campaigns contact voters long before election day and measure effects immediately — **although this early persuasion decays**".
  - As Election Day approaches, the effects of early contact decay and the immediate effects of later contact "almost always go to zero" — [eScholarship PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt103775sx/qt103775sx.pdf?t=qamtj9).
- Gerber & Green's handbook chapter: strong early phone results "sparked a series of experiments designed to sort out whether the strong effects reflect timing, the use of repeated calls, or the conversational style of the scripts" — [Gerber & Green, "Field Experiments on Voter Mobilization: An Overview" (J-PAL-hosted PDF)](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/Gerber%20Green%20Handbook.pdf) **[academic handbook chapter]**
- A Tufts CIRCLE research compilation lists cost per additional vote of about **$20 for contact in the last 3 weeks vs. $105 in earlier periods** — [CIRCLE, Young Voter Mobilization Tactics](https://circle.tufts.edu/sites/default/files/2020-02/young_voters_mobilization_tactics.pdf) **[academic/other; extract was fragmentary — the tactic (likely phone) and population (youth) are uncertain]**
- New Haven canvassing was conducted "shortly before" the November 1998 election — [ISPS](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps00-001) **[peer-reviewed]**
- GOTV practice includes "active tracking of eligible voters who have already voted", plus literature drops early on Election Day or the evening before — [Wikipedia, "Get out the vote"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Get_out_the_vote) **[reference]**. Door-to-door GOTV is typically concentrated in the final 72 hours, aimed at confirming voting plans with identified supporters — [CallHub, GOTV Canvassing playbook](https://callhub.io/blog/canvassing/gotv-canvassing/) **[industry]**
- **Wisconsin calendar (Nov 3, 2026 general election)** — [866ourvote.org Wisconsin](https://866ourvote.org/state/wisconsin/); [FOX6, Wisconsin voter deadlines Nov 2026](https://www.fox6now.com/news/wisconsin-election-voter-deadlines-november-2026); [Vote411 Wisconsin](https://production.vote411.org/wisconsin) **[reference]**
  - In-person absentee (early) voting: **Tue Oct 20 – Sun Nov 1, 2026**. Municipal clerks set their own hours within the window (e.g., Sparta weekdays 8 a.m.–4 p.m.; Town of Lebanon only two sessions) — [Sparta clerk notice](https://www.spartawisconsin.org/media/13531); [Town of Lebanon](https://townoflebanon.wi.gov/election-information/).
  - In-person registration at the clerk's office through **Fri Oct 30, 5 p.m.**; same-day registration at the polls.
  - Absentee ballots must be received by **8 p.m. Nov 3**.
- No study was found that directly tests canvassing timing within an early-voting window, or the effect of contacting people who have already voted — [search result summary; no direct study surfaced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Get_out_the_vote) **[gap noted]**

### Inferences
- Because early voting opens Oct 20, some targeted voters will cast ballots **before** a volunteer reaches them. Turf should prioritize, in this order:
  1. Areas with lower expected early-vote uptake, and voters whose decision is still ahead.
  2. On the final weekend (Oct 29 – Nov 1): Election-Day reminders and logistics such as polling place, hours and same-day registration. These matter most for the Nov 3 voters who remain.
- If the campaign can get absentee-returned lists from the Wisconsin Elections Commission or the clerk, removing already-voted households avoids wasted doors. Availability for 2026 was **not verified** (see Gaps).
- The recency evidence (Nickerson; Kalla & Broockman decay) supports putting **knock-and-talk persuasion closer to each voter's likely voting date**. Panagopoulos suggests early-window contact is not wasted, especially for habitual (high-propensity) voters, who are disproportionately the early voters.
- A referendum is lower-salience than a top-of-ticket race. But Nov 2026 is a statewide midterm, so referendum awareness may be the binding constraint more than turnout. Door hangers that **state the date and the referendum's existence** plausibly serve the "primacy" function early in the window. This is inference, not tested.

### Gaps
- Whether the Wisconsin Elections Commission publishes daily absentee-ballot return files (voter-level) for Nov 2026, and how fast, was not researched here.
- Exact Panagopoulos (2011) effect sizes by timing arm and by propensity were not retrieved, and the timing-arm description conflicts across sources.
- From memory, *unverified here*: Kalla & Broockman report that persuasion can be detectable in **primaries and ballot-measure** contexts where party cues are absent. This would be directly relevant to a school referendum, but it could not be confirmed from search extracts.
- No canvassing-specific (as opposed to phone) experiment on the decay curve of mobilization effects within the final 2–4 weeks was retrieved.
- Middleton and Cross Plains clerks' in-person absentee hours were not retrieved.

## Q4. Turf design and route efficiency: doors per turf for a 2-hour shift, density, loops vs. out-and-back, pairs vs. solo, splitting street sides, driving turfs, apartments/locked buildings, cul-de-sacs

### Takeaway
NGP VAN's own turf-cutting best practices give **30–50 doors per packet**, scaled to script, volunteer and density, with **no more than about a block between doors** and no natural barriers inside a turf. Indivisible's MiniVAN guide reports **about 40–45 doors per pair per 2–2.5-hour shift**. Practitioners range from **about 40 doors for a driving turf to 100+ for a dense walking turf**, and GOTV "pocket walks" are smaller (**15–25 doors**). Splitting a street's two sides between partners is a recognized way to double coverage. Secured apartment buildings require management permission or should be skipped.

### Cited Findings
- **Doors per packet:** a rough guide of **30–50 doors per packet**; the right number depends on the script, volunteers and turf density — [NGP VAN, "Organizing Best Practices: Turf Cutting" (PDF)](https://www.ngpvan.com/wp-content/uploads/Turf-Cutting-OBP.pdf) **[industry/practitioner — vendor for Democratic campaigns]**

### Inferences
**Recommended packet sizes for 2-hour shifts** (my synthesis):
- Knock-and-talk, walkable suburban: **35–50 doors per volunteer** (or 50–80 per pair splitting sides), consistent with NGP VAN's 30–50.
- Knock-and-talk, driving large-lot turf: **20–35 doors**.
- Door-hanger-only walkable turf: **100–150 doors**, consistent with the 3–4× multiplier and the 76-door lit lists plus margin.
- Door-hanger-only driving turf: **50–80 doors**.
- Cut slightly over expected pace so fast volunteers don't run out. List an optional "bonus" street.

**Route shape**
- Loops are preferable to out-and-back. Turfs should **start and end near where the volunteer parked**, which matters most for driving turfs.
- Cul-de-sacs are best walked as a single in-and-out by one person while the partner continues on the through street, then the two rejoin.
- No source above addresses loops or cul-de-sacs explicitly. This is standard practice inferred from NGP VAN's "easy to navigate" principle.

**Pairs vs. solo**
- Pairs walking together are safer and better for new volunteers, but halve per-person door output (Indivisible's 40–45 per pair).
- Pairs **splitting opposite sides of the same street**, keeping each other in sight, keep most of the safety benefit at nearly solo pace (SC Women Lead's 100 homes per pair). This is the recommended default for experienced volunteers in daylight.
- After dark, pairs should knock together or switch to door-hanger-only.

**Apartments**
- Downtown Middleton apartments with locked entries should be treated as **"no-access" in the packet**. Options:
  - Seek manager permission in advance.
  - Leave materials only where management allows, e.g., a lobby literature table. **Never put them in the cluster mailboxes** (see Q6).
  - Reach residents by other modes (text, mail).

- No evidence was found comparing contact rates or productivity of pairs vs. solo canvassers.
- No evidence was found on loops vs. out-and-back or cul-de-sac handling.
- No Wisconsin-specific law was found on canvasser access to multi-unit buildings. It appears to be governed by private-property rules, but this is unverified.

## Q5. Canvasser effects: local/neighbor canvassers (Sinclair, McConnell & Michelson 2013), canvasser training/quality, scripted vs. conversational

### Takeaway
Evidence modestly favors **local, shared-identity or socially connected canvassers**:
- Sinclair, McConnell & Michelson (2013) found canvassers from the voter's own neighborhood more effective (2006 general election).
- The MoveOn 2004 neighbor-to-neighbor program produced about a **9 pp** turnout effect among contacted voters.
- Michelson's work shows co-ethnic canvassers get more doors opened.

On scripts, the evidence base is mostly phone-based. Nickerson's "Quality Is Job One" and the Gerber/Green handbook point to **conversational, unhurried delivery and canvasser quality**, not script wording. Message content often shows no detectable effect (Michelson 2003). No experiment was found that directly randomizes scripted vs. conversational door canvassing.

### Cited Findings
- **Sinclair, McConnell & Michelson (2013), "Local Canvassing: The Efficacy of Grassroots Voter Mobilization"**, *Political Communication* 30(1):42–57 — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10584609.2012.737413); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/263455682_Local_Canvassing_The_Efficacy_of_Grassroots_Voter_Mobilization) **[peer-reviewed]**
  - Compares face-to-face GOTV visits by canvassers living in the voter's own neighborhood vs. canvassers from other neighborhoods.
  - Data: a randomized campaign by a local community outreach group in the **2006 general election** (reported as Los Angeles).
  - Findings support a link between social influence and turnout: **local canvassers more effective**.
  - Exact effect sizes were not retrieved. A Substack write-up says voters who talked with local canvassers were "between 4 and 11 percent more likely to vote" — [The Connector (Substack)](https://theconnector.substack.com/p/when-the-doors-you-knock-belong-to) **[secondary; unverified]**
- Later work cites this study as showing canvassers are more effective with voters who live in the **same zip code** — [Ho et al., "The Effectiveness of a Neighbor-to-Neighbor GOTV Program: Evidence from the 2017 Virginia State Elections" (Stanford)](https://dho.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/The_Effectiveness_of_a_Neighbor_to_Neighbor_Get_Out_the_Vote_Program___Evidence_from_the_2017_Virginia_State_Elections.pdf) **[academic/other]**. Another paper says "voters trust more canvassers that are similar to them when they can identify them" — [Minaudier (2021)](http://www.clementminaudier.com/Minaudier_2021_Motivations_beliefs_and_returns_to_canvassing.pdf) **[academic working paper]**
- **Middleton & Green (2008), *Quarterly Journal of Political Science***: MoveOn's 2004 neighbor-to-neighbor volunteer canvass. Regression-discontinuity design using precinct boundaries (one side of a street canvassed, the other not); **46,277 voters in 13 swing states**. Contact with MoveOn volunteers raised turnout by about **9 pp**, even in a high-salience battleground election — [ISPS](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps08-005); [Now Publishers](https://nowpublishers.com/article/Details/QJPS-7019) **[peer-reviewed]**. 2007 coverage of the working paper reported about **7%** — [American Prospect](https://prospect.org/2007/07/06/moveon-moved-voters/) **[secondary; discrepancy unexplained]**
- **Michelson (2003)**: Latino canvassers were more effective at getting Latino voters to **open their door**, and message content had no effect on turnout — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1025167607369) **[peer-reviewed]**
- **Michelson (Fresno, 2006 *Social Science Quarterly*)**: young Latino voters targeted by Latino canvassers were **more likely to be contacted**. Once contacted, Latinos reached by non-Latino canvassers were just as likely to vote, so the shared-identity effect works mainly through **contact rate** — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/socsci/v87y2006i5p1188-1206.html) **[peer-reviewed]**
- **Canvasser quality/scripts**
  - Nickerson (2007) "Quality Is Job One" (*AJPS* 51(2)) is the standard citation that call **quality and delivery** (volunteer and professional) drive effects. It is a phone study — [cited in Panagopoulos (2011) PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Costas-Panagopoulos/publication/225458872_Timing_Is_Everything_Primacy_and_Recency_Effects_in_Voter_Mobilization_Campaigns/links/0046351eee89d2d217000000/Timing-Is-Everything-Primacy-and-Recency-Effects-in-Voter-Mobilization-Campaigns.pdf) **[peer-reviewed, secondhand]**
  - Gerber & Green note experiments probing whether effects stem from "timing, the use of repeated calls, or the conversational style of the scripts" — [Gerber & Green handbook chapter](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/Gerber%20Green%20Handbook.pdf) **[academic]**
  - A theoretical model: returns to canvassing are higher when more canvassers are intrinsically motivated and willing to go "off-script" — [Minaudier (2021)](http://www.clementminaudier.com/Minaudier_2021_Motivations_beliefs_and_returns_to_canvassing.pdf) **[academic working paper — theory, not a direct test]**
  - In the UK experiment, canvassers were instructed to deliver the script "but not to stick rigidly to it, and to be as friendly as they liked" — [PSRM](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/political-science-research-and-methods/article/is-it-worth-doorknocking-evidence-from-a-united-kingdombased-get-out-the-vote-gotv-field-experiment-on-the-effect-of-party-leaflets-and-canvass-visits-on-voter-turnout/757F1AF7D16F43A444BDDE55181471B5) **[peer-reviewed, non-US]**
  - Vendor claims that adaptive (non-rigid) scripting raises persuasion "35–50%" have **no identifiable underlying study** — [Ecanvasser, "The Perfect Doorstep Interaction"](https://www.ecanvasser.com/blog/the-perfect-doorstep-interaction); [CallHub GOTV playbook](https://callhub.io/blog/canvassing/gotv-canvassing/) **[industry; treat as unsupported]**
- **Texting after a canvass** (Virginia 2025): post-canvass texting produced modest turnout gains, concentrated among women, and did not improve canvass contact itself — [Center for Campaign Innovation](https://campaigninnovation.org/research/testing-text-messaging-as-a-complement-to-door-to-door-canvassing) **[industry/think-tank research]**

### Inferences
- **Assign volunteers to their own neighborhoods** (or immediately adjacent ones) wherever possible. Have them open with "I live on ___ Street / my kids go to ___ school". For a school referendum, the neighbor/parent identity is likely the strongest available credibility cue. This follows from Sinclair et al. and Middleton & Green, though neither tested referenda.
- The shared-identity benefit appears mainly through **more doors opened** (Michelson). That is an additional reason to put local volunteers on knock-and-talk turfs and newer or non-local volunteers on door-hanger turfs.
- Training should prioritize a short, **natural, conversational ask** (a few key facts, the date, a voting-plan question) over memorizing a script. A brief practice role-play is worth more than script wording. This is inferred from the phone-quality literature and the null message-content findings.

### Gaps
- Exact Sinclair, McConnell & Michelson (2013) effect sizes, sample and location were not verified (paywalled; fetch blocked).
- No door-canvassing experiment directly randomizing training intensity or scripted vs. conversational delivery was found.
- No evidence was found specific to referendum or school-funding canvassing.

## Q6. Practical rules: 18 U.S.C. § 1725 (mailboxes), "No Soliciting" signs and political canvassing (Watchtower v. Stratton 2002), safety after dark, daylight and weather in southern Wisconsin late October

### Takeaway
- **Never put anything in, or presumably on, a mailbox.** 18 U.S.C. § 1725 bars depositing unstamped mailable matter in letterboxes, and this was upheld in *USPS v. Council of Greenburgh Civic Ass'ns* (1981). Doors, knobs and porches are the lawful alternative.
- Under *Watchtower v. Stratton* (2002), municipalities can't require permits for door-to-door political advocacy. The Court pointed to residents' **"No Solicitation" signs** and their "unquestioned right to refuse" as the proper protection. Honoring such signs is legally prudent and good practice, even though political canvassing is generally not "soliciting" under most ordinances.
- **Daylight:** sunset in Madison is about **6:05 p.m. CDT on Thu Oct 22**, about **5:51 p.m. on Sat Oct 31**, and about **4:50 p.m. CST on Sun Nov 1** after DST ends at 2 a.m.
- **Weather:** late-October afternoons typically run in the **50s °F**, falling into the **40s/30s after sunset**, with roughly a **40% chance of measurable precipitation** on a given day.
- Safety guidance favors **daylight, pairs after dark, staying on sidewalks, and not entering homes**.

### Cited Findings
**18 U.S.C. § 1725 (mailboxes)**
- The statute bars depositing unstamped "mailable matter" in USPS-approved mailboxes. It is read to cover flyers, brochures, business cards and notices, and applies to political campaigns. A recipient's verbal permission does not override it. Doors are not covered: "hang something on the knob, place it on the porch" — [LegallyExplained](https://legallyexplained.com/is-it-illegal-to-put-flyers-in-mailboxes/); [DoorHangersWork, "Is It Legal to Put Flyers on Doors?"](https://www.doorhangerswork.com/?p=25098) **[secondary/commercial — verify against the statute text: [Cornell LII, 18 U.S.C. § 1725](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/1725)]**
- The Supreme Court upheld § 1725 in *U.S. Postal Service v. Council of Greenburgh Civic Associations*, 453 U.S. 114 (1981), holding that a letterbox is not a public forum — [LegallyExplained (secondary)](https://legallyexplained.com/is-it-illegal-to-put-a-flyer-in-a-mailbox/) **[legal; secondary description]**
- **Penalty figures conflict**: "up to $5,000 per occurrence" vs. "a fine equal to the postage that would have been due". Neither could be verified against the statute here — [LegallyExplained](https://legallyexplained.com/is-it-illegal-to-put-flyers-in-mailboxes/); [LegalOverview](https://legaloverview.com/?p=4119) **[secondary — uncertain]**
- A US canvassing guide: never leave literature in mailboxes — [SC Women Lead safety guide](https://scwomenlead.net/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Strategies-for-Safety-While-Canvassing.pdf) **[practitioner]**

**Watchtower v. Stratton and "No Soliciting" signs**
- *Watchtower Bible & Tract Society of N.Y. v. Village of Stratton*, **536 U.S. 150 (2002)**, decided **June 17, 2002, 8–1** (Stevens, J.; Rehnquist, C.J., dissenting) — [Cornell LII syllabus](https://law.cornell.edu/supct/html/00-1737.ZS.html); [Cornell LII opinion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/536/150); [FIRE](https://www.fire.org/supreme-court/watchtower-bible-and-tract-society-new-york-inc-v-village-stratton/opinions) **[legal — primary]**
  - Holding: a village ordinance making it a misdemeanor to engage in door-to-door advocacy without first registering with the mayor and obtaining a permit **violates the First Amendment** as applied to religious proselytizing, **anonymous political speech** and the distribution of handbills.
  - The Court reasoned that an unchallenged section of the ordinance authorizing residents to post **"No Solicitation" signs**, "coupled with their **unquestioned right to refuse to engage in conversation with unwelcome visitors**", provides ample protection for unwilling listeners. It also found no evidence of a special crime problem tied to door-to-door canvassing.
- *Martin v. City of Struthers* (1943) struck down an ordinance making it a crime to knock on residents' doors or ring doorbells to distribute literature. It did not address "No Soliciting" signs — [FIRE case page](https://www.fire.org/supreme-court/martin-v-city-struthers/cases) **[legal — primary-source summary]**
- NGP VAN's position: because political canvassing is generally not considered "soliciting", canvassers are "not legally required to avoid 'No Solicitors' signs in most cities". Local ordinances vary — [NGP VAN blog, "Is political canvassing soliciting?"](https://ngpvan.com/blog/is-political-canvassing-soliciting) **[industry; not a legal authority]**
- **Local:** Dane County's solicitor ordinance exempts "candidates for political office" from permit rules — [Dane County Ord. ch. (PDF)](https://build-plandev.danecounty.gov/documents/pdf/ordinances/ord061.pdf) **[legal — county; applies to unincorporated areas/towns as applicable]**. An aggregator claims Dane County cities, including Middleton, exempt political canvassing, but it mostly describes Madison's MGO §9.06 and cites no Middleton code — [cityrulelookup.com](https://cityrulelookup.com/soliciting/solicitor-permits/middleton-wi) **[aggregator — unreliable]**

**Safety after dark**
- Canvass in daylight where possible: voters often dislike being disturbed after dark. In the evening, carry only essentials and keep an incident log — [Local Government Association (UK), "Seven principles for safer canvassing"](https://www.local.gov.uk/publications/seven-principles-safer-canvassing-guide-councillors-and-candidates) **[practitioner, non-US]**

### Inferences
**Door-hanger placement rule:** on the door (knob or handle, or wedged in the storm-door frame), or securely on the porch. **Never in, on, or taped to the mailbox or its post.** The "on/attached to" part is a conservative reading; see Gaps.

**"No Soliciting" signs:**
- Train volunteers to **skip doors with "No Soliciting" or "No Trespassing" signs** for knock-and-talk.
- For door hangers, the conservative and courteous rule is also to skip them. The legal risk is low for political speech, but the reputational cost to a school referendum of ignoring signs could be high.
- Leave immediately if asked.
