## 1. Best estimates of face-to-face canvassing effects, and how they vary with baseline turnout and salience

### Takeaway
A face-to-face GOTV conversation at the door is the most reliable mode the experiments have found. US meta-analyses put the precision-weighted effect at about **2.5 percentage points per person actually contacted** (CACE). Older Green & Gerber summaries, which lean on low-salience elections, put it near **one extra vote per 14–16 contacts** (about 6–7 pp per contact). Effects are smaller in Europe (about 0.8 pp). The meta-analysis finds no strong link between baseline turnout and effect size. However, *who* responds changes with salience: in high-turnout elections like a governor's race, the gains come mostly from **low-propensity** voters.

### Cited Findings
**Pooled and meta-analytic estimates**
- Green, McGrath & Aronow (2013, *J. of Elections, Public Opinion & Parties* 23(1):27–48; DOI 10.1080/17457289.2012.728223) review the field-experiment literature. Their replication archive covers **294 field experiments with validated turnout** as the outcome. — [ISPS Yale listing](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps13-020)
- Townsley (2018) summarizes Green, McGrath & Aronow's precision-weighted estimates in his Table 1. Face-to-face canvassing is **"around 2.5 percentage points."** Canvassing and phone figures are **CACE (per contact)**, while leaflet and direct-mail figures are **ITT**. Less personal tactics, such as commercial phone calls and leaflets, have smaller effects. **[secondhand: Townsley's summary of GMA 2013, not GMA's own table]** — [Townsley 2018, PSRM](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/political-science-research-and-methods/article/is-it-worth-doorknocking-evidence-from-a-united-kingdombased-get-out-the-vote-gotv-field-experiment-on-the-effect-of-party-leaflets-and-canvass-visits-on-voter-turnout/757F1AF7D16F43A444BDDE55181471B5)
- Bhatti, Dahlgaard, Hansen & Hansen ("Is Door-to-Door Canvassing Effective in Europe? Evidence from a Meta-study across Six European Countries," *BJPolS* 49(1):279–290, published online 2016, print 2019):
  - Pooled European effect is **substantially smaller than the US** effect.
  - Two new Danish experiments found **no effect**.
  - **No strong relationship between turnout level and effect size**, either in Europe or the US, so baseline turnout does not explain the gap.
  - The authors stress "considerable uncertainty" in the European estimates.
  — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/bjposi/v49y2019i01p279-290_00.html); [VIVE summary](https://www.vive.dk/en/publications/is-door-to-door-canvassing-effective-in-europe-ov992lvn/)
- As cited by Townsley (2018), the Bhatti et al. precision-weighted canvassing effect is **0.78 pp in Europe vs 2.54 pp in the US**. **[secondhand]** — [Townsley 2018](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/political-science-research-and-methods/article/is-it-worth-doorknocking-evidence-from-a-united-kingdombased-get-out-the-vote-gotv-field-experiment-on-the-effect-of-party-leaflets-and-canvass-visits-on-voter-turnout/757F1AF7D16F43A444BDDE55181471B5)
- A review of about 200 experiments concludes that commonly used interventions move turnout only "in the low single digits." — [SSIR, "Getting out the vote is tougher than you think"](https://ssir.org/articles/entry/getting_out_the_vote_is_tougher_than_you_think)

**Green & Gerber book summaries (earlier editions)**
- Green & Gerber (*Get Out the Vote*, 2004 edition, as reported in a news story): door-to-door canvassing yields **one additional vote for every 14 people contacted** on average. — [Chronicle of Philanthropy](https://www.philanthropy.com/news/cost-of-door-to-door-canvassing-is-19-per-vote-research-finds/)
- 4th edition (Brookings, 2019): the book calls volunteer canvassing the "gold standard" mobilization tactic and includes **Appendix A: Meta-Analysis of Door-to-Door Canvassing Experiments**. I could not retrieve the appendix's pooled number. — [Perlego listing](https://www.perlego.com/book/5143575/get-out-the-vote-how-to-increase-voter-turnout-pdf); [Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/Get_Out_the_Vote.html?id=LKGaYyZqZbEC)

**Original experiments**
- Gerber & Green 1999 (PNAS, "Does canvassing increase voter turnout? A field experiment"), New Haven 1998:
  - A contact raised the probability of voting by about **6.3 percentage points**, so **about 16 contacts per additional voter**.
  - At 10 contacts/hour and $10/hour, that works out to about $16 per vote.
  — [Gerber & Green PNAS PDF (UVM mirror)](https://www.uvm.edu/~dguber/POLS234/articles/gerber.pdf)
- Gerber & Green 2000 (APSR), New Haven, about 30,000 registered voters:
  - Turnout rose "substantially" with personal canvassing, "slightly" with direct mail, and not at all with (commercial) phone calls.
  - Face-to-face canvassing raised turnout from about **44% (control) to 53% among those canvassed**.
  - The New Haven phone result was later disputed (implementation errors).
  — [APSR abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/abs/effects-of-canvassing-telephone-calls-and-direct-mail-on-voter-turnout-a-field-experiment/E7C645533F3B750E599BAFE710B477BF); [GOTV book PDF mirror](https://groups.io/g/psychologyonline/attachment/219/0/Get%20out%20the%20vote%20%20how%20to%20increase%20voter%20turnout.pdf)
- Youth-vote canvassing (Pew-commissioned, near public universities) raised turnout by an average of **8.5 pp**. A partisan canvass with a **7.6 pp ITT** effect cost **$10.40 per additional vote**. — [CIRCLE, "Young Voter Mobilization Tactics"](https://circle.tufts.edu/sites/default/files/2020-02/young_voters_mobilization_tactics.pdf)
- Analyst Institute, 2017 Virginia cycle: a "typical GOTV canvass" raised turnout by **0.3** (percent or pp; unclear) in that high-activity environment. **[secondhand, reported on advocacy blogs; not verified against the AI memo]** — [Blue Virginia](https://bluevirginia.us/2018/06/the-mighty-pen-prevails-in-the-digital-age-handwritten-voter-contact-is-a-powerful-secret-weapon/)

**Heterogeneity by salience and voter propensity**
- Arceneaux & Nickerson (2009, AJPS 53(1):1–16, "Who Is Mobilized to Vote? A Re-Analysis of 11 Field Experiments"):
  - Model: only voters near an indifference threshold get mobilized, and that threshold depends on general interest in the election.
  - Implication: **low-propensity voters can be effectively mobilized only in high-turnout elections**, while **high-propensity voters are easier to mobilize in low-turnout elections**.
  - Some CVs title the paper "Seven Randomized Field Experiments"; the published title says 11.
  — [Wiley abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-5907.2008.00354.x); [Temple PDF](https://sites.temple.edu/nickerson/files/2017/07/Arceneaux_Nickerson.AJPS_.2009.pdf)
- Later commentary warns that the canonical canvassing evidence "stems from relatively low-salience elections and may not readily generalize to high-salience elections." — [search summary of citing work; see GOTV book mirror](https://groups.io/g/psychologyonline/attachment/219/0/Get%20out%20the%20vote%20%20how%20to%20increase%20voter%20turnout.pdf) **[secondhand]**
- Rogers, Green, Ternovski & Young (2017, *Electoral Studies* 46:87–100) tested social pressure in the high-salience **2012 Wisconsin gubernatorial recall**:
  - Control-group turnout was about **65.4%**.
  - Effects were "smaller but still sizable," concentrated among **low-propensity voters**.
  - This is mail, not canvassing, but it is the closest Wisconsin evidence on how mobilization scales when baseline turnout is high.
  — [Harvard PDF](https://scholar.harvard.edu/files/todd_rogers/files/rogers_et_al._social_pressure_and_voting.pdf); [HKS page](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/social-pressure-and-voting-field-experiment-conducted-high-salience-election)

### Inferences
- For planning, use a range of **about 1 extra vote per 40 face-to-face conversations** (2.5 pp CACE, the modern meta-analytic figure) up to **about 1 per 14–16** (older low-salience estimates). A Nov 2026 Wisconsin midterm with an open governor's race is high-salience. Middleton-area baseline turnout among registered voters is likely high, so the planning figure should sit toward the **conservative end** (about 1 vote per 30–50 conversations) for the electorate as a whole.
- Arceneaux & Nickerson's result implies that in a high-turnout November election, volunteer conversations pay off most with **low- or mid-propensity, supportive households** (for example, younger parents, renters, recent movers). Reliable voters will turn out for the governor's race regardless.
- Bhatti et al.'s null link between baseline turnout and effect size is a caution against assuming the effect collapses to zero in high-turnout settings. The gubernatorial-recall mail experiment (65% control turnout) still found sizable effects.

## 2. What door-hanger, leaflet, and lit-drop experiments find, including knocking plus leaving literature

### Takeaway
Literature left at the door without a conversation has, in US experiments, produced effects ranging from **zero to about 1 pp** (ITT, per household or voter targeted). The older Green & Gerber benchmark is about **one vote per 200 people leafleted**. The best-known partisan door-hanger test (Michigan Democrats, 2002 governor's race) found **+1.2 pp**. The GMA 2013 pooled partisan leaflets plus mail estimate is about **−0.2 pp (not significant)**. One UK experiment found a much larger leaflet effect (+4.3 pp) and **no significant added effect from a canvass visit on top of the leaflet**.

### Cited Findings
**US leaflet and door-hanger experiments**
- **Nonpartisan leafleting**, Gerber & Green (2000, JOP 62:846–857, "The Effect of a Nonpartisan Get-Out-the-Vote Drive: An Experimental Study of Leafletting"; DOI 10.1111/0022-3816.00035). Hamden, CT, 1998 general, **2,021 registered voters on 50 streets**:
  - Treatment was an 8"×11" card with a nonpartisan encouragement to vote, left at the door.
  - No effect on registered Democrats or Republicans.
  - Turnout of **unaffiliated** voters rose by "approximately 7%." Sources disagree on whether that is relative or percentage points.
  - J-PAL reports turnout **10 pp higher** among unaffiliated voters who voted in 1996, against a **63%** control rate.
  — [JOP abstract](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1111/0022-3816.00035); [J-PAL evaluation summary](https://povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/effect-nonpartisan-get-out-vote-drive-experimental-study-leafletting); [ISPS](https://isps.yale.edu/node/19712)
- **Partisan door hangers, Michigan 2002 governor's race**:
  - Design: a statewide experiment with the Michigan Democratic Party across **14 state house districts**, testing door hangers, volunteer phone calls, and face-to-face visits.
  - Results: door hangers raised turnout **1.2 pp**; volunteer phone calls **3.2 pp** (Nickerson 2005, *Annals*). The working paper concludes that "all three GOTV strategies possess **similar cost-effectiveness**."
  - Citation: the journal version is King, "Partisan Mobilization Campaigns in the Field: Results from a Statewide Turnout Experiment in Michigan," *Political Research Quarterly* 59 (March 2006): 85–98. The working paper is Nickerson, Friedrichs & King (2004), "Mobilizing the Party Faithful."
  — [IDEAS, Nickerson 2005 Annals](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/anname/v601y2005i1p10-27.html); [IDEAS, HKS working paper](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ecl/harjfk/rwp04-018.html); [HKS publication page](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/partisan-mobilization-campaigns-field-results-statewide-turnout-experiment-michigan)
- The same Michigan 2002 Democratic leaflet test cost about **$14 per vote, excluding overhead**. — [CIRCLE compilation](https://circle.tufts.edu/sites/default/files/2020-02/young_voters_mobilization_tactics.pdf)

**Pooled benchmarks**
- Green & Gerber (2004 edition): distributing leaflets yields **one extra vote for every 200 people** (about 0.5 pp), at about **$43 per vote** under the book's assumptions. — [Chronicle of Philanthropy](https://www.philanthropy.com/news/cost-of-door-to-door-canvassing-is-19-per-vote-research-finds/)
- Green, McGrath & Aronow (2013), as summarized by Townsley: for leaflets and direct mail, **partisan messages have a statistically insignificant −0.213 pp** effect on turnout (ITT). **[secondhand]** — [Townsley 2018](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/political-science-research-and-methods/article/is-it-worth-doorknocking-evidence-from-a-united-kingdombased-get-out-the-vote-gotv-field-experiment-on-the-effect-of-party-leaflets-and-canvass-visits-on-voter-turnout/757F1AF7D16F43A444BDDE55181471B5)
- Green, Gerber & Nickerson (2003) note that "other experimental evidence seems to show that **leaflets alone have minimal effects** on turnout." — [Temple PDF](https://sites.temple.edu/nickerson/files/2017/07/Green_Gerber_Nickerson.JOP_.2003.pdf) **[secondhand via search snippet]**

**Combinations: knocking plus leaving literature (non-US evidence)**
- **Townsley (2018, PSRM, DOI 10.1017/psrm.2018.39)**, UK 2017, Liberal Democrats, **6,525 voters**:
  - Party leaflets raised turnout **4.3 pp**.
  - A canvass visit *after* the leaflet added **0.6 pp, not statistically significant**.
  - The author urges caution: the contact rate was low, uncertainty is high, and there was **no canvass-only arm**.
  - Replication data: Harvard Dataverse DOI 10.7910/DVN/VEZ66S.
  — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/political-science-research-and-methods/article/is-it-worth-doorknocking-evidence-from-a-united-kingdombased-get-out-the-vote-gotv-field-experiment-on-the-effect-of-party-leaflets-and-canvass-visits-on-voter-turnout/757F1AF7D16F43A444BDDE55181471B5); [Dataverse](https://dataverse.harvard.edu/dataset.xhtml?persistentId=doi%3A10.7910%2FDVN%2FVEZ66S); [Mark Pack summary](https://www.markpack.org.uk/151349/leaflets-canvassing-field-experiment-gotv/)
- Secondary literature cites **Foos & John (2018)** and Townsley (2018) together: a canvass visit following a leaflet "does not produce a substantial increase in turnout compared to the effect of the leaflet alone." — [search summary of citing work (Cambridge/ResearchGate "Canvassing")](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/384355069_Canvassing) **[secondhand]**

### Inferences
- In US data, a door hanger alone is a **low-yield but very cheap** contact: roughly 0–1.2 pp ITT, centered near 0.5 pp. One door hanger is worth perhaps **1/5 to 1/10 of a conversation** in turnout terms, but takes a fraction of the time.
- The Michigan finding that door hangers, volunteer calls, and visits had **similar cost-effectiveness** matters directly here. With unpaid volunteer time as the binding constraint, a fast hang-only route can match or beat knock-everything on votes per volunteer-hour, especially in suburban turf where contact rates are low.
- **Knock, and leave a hanger when nobody answers** captures the canvass effect at answered doors plus the leaflet effect at the rest, for seconds of extra time per door. The UK evidence suggests little gain from stacking a canvass on top of a leaflet already delivered. In other words, a household that has already been reached probably does not need both.
- The Hamden result (effects only among unaffiliated voters) and the GMA partisan null suggest that **nonpartisan, civic-framed** literature may do better than a "Vote YES" partisan-style flyer for pure turnout. But a referendum campaign wants supporters to vote *yes*, so see Q4 on persuasion.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve GMA 2013's pooled estimate for **nonpartisan** leaflets, or the number of leaflet studies pooled.
- I found no US randomized experiment comparing knock-and-leave-hanger against hang-only against knock-only in the same study. The combination evidence is UK-only (Townsley; Foos & John).
- I found no experiment testing a door hanger left at not-home doors as a canvass follow-up ("Sorry we missed you"), in either partisan or ballot-measure settings.

## 3. Do message features printed on literature change effects?

### Takeaway
Some message features have strong evidence, but **almost none of it comes from door hangers**. It comes from mail, phone, and canvass scripts:
- **Social pressure** (showing people their own or neighbors' voting records) is the strongest single message feature: +4.8 to +8.1 pp by mail in a low-salience primary, smaller in high-salience elections, with backlash risk.
- **Thank-you/gratitude** mail: +2 to +3 pp.
- **Plan-making**: +4.1 pp per *phone* contact, +9.1 pp in single-voter households; largely unreplicated.
- **"High turnout" descriptive norms**: these beat "low turnout" framing, but the evidence is mostly on stated intention.
- **Handwritten notes**: small effects (about 0.2–1 pp in big elections, up to 3.4 pp in low-salience specials). Partly preprinted cards perform about as well as fully handwritten ones.
- **Polling-place information**: modest, imprecise gains.

### Cited Findings
**Social pressure**
- **Gerber, Green & Larimer (2008, APSR)**, Michigan Aug 2006 primary, mailers: the "Neighbors" mailer, which disclosed household and neighbors' past turnout, raised turnout **8.1 pp**, "about as effective as in-person canvassing." The "Self" mailer raised it **4.8 pp**. — [J-PAL evaluation](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/social-pressure-and-voter-turnout-united-states); [Rogers et al. 2017 PDF (citing GGL)](https://scholar.harvard.edu/files/todd_rogers/files/rogers_et_al._social_pressure_and_voting.pdf); [ISPS PDF](https://isps.yale.edu/sites/default/files/publication/2012/12/ISPS08-001.pdf)
  - The commonly reported Civic Duty (+1.8 pp) and Hawthorne (+2.5 pp) arms, against **29.7%** control turnout, are **[unverified this session]**. See the [Adam Brown summary](https://adambrown.info/p/notes/gerber_green_and_larimer_social_pressure_and_voter_turnout).
- **Rogers et al. (2017)**, 2012 Wisconsin recall: effects were smaller but sizable in a high-salience election, concentrated among low-propensity voters. The paper also documents **backlash** that the Neighbors mailers provoked. — [HKS](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/social-pressure-and-voting-field-experiment-conducted-high-salience-election)
- Mann (2010), "Is There Backlash to Social Pressure? A Large-scale Field Experiment on Voter Mobilization," *Political Behavior*. Exact numbers not retrieved. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11109-010-9124-y)
- A Colorado social-pressure mailer in 2019 drew public backlash ("really missed the mark"). — [Colorado Sun](https://coloradosun.com/2019/11/13/social-pressure-mailers-politics-coloradans-for-prosperity/)
- **Progressive Turnout Project / TMC 2022 postcard RCT**, about 5.3M voters in six swing states: **social-pressure messaging outperformed alternative messages**. — [PTP 2022 Postcard Experiment PDF](https://turnoutpac.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/2022-Postcards-RCT-1.pdf)

**Descriptive norms ("high turnout" framing)**
- **Gerber & Rogers (2009, JOP)**, "Descriptive Social Norms and Motivation to Vote: Everybody's Voting and So Should You":
  - In two GOTV field experiments (phone scripts, California and New Jersey), **high-expected-turnout messages beat low-expected-turnout messages**.
  - Effects appear only among **infrequent/occasional voters**.
  - The outcome was **self-reported vote intention, not validated turnout**. One summary says the high-turnout script group was about **7%** more likely to report a 100% probability of voting. **[secondhand]**
  — [ISPS](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps09-33); [JOP](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1017/S0022381608090117); [Decision Lab summary](https://thedecisionlab.com/intervention/how-social-norms-increased-voting-intentions-by-7)
- Gerber & Rogers (2009) and Nickerson & White (2009) give competing answers on descriptive-norm effects on actual turnout. A follow-up with validated turnout (Hawthorne, CA) exists; numbers not retrieved. — [Springer, "Social Pressure, Descriptive Norms, and Voter Mobilization"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11109-013-9234-4)

**Plan-making / implementation intentions**
- **Nickerson & Rogers (2010, *Psychological Science*)**, "Do You Have a Voting Plan?", 2008 presidential cycle, phone calls, **N = 287,228** (a BPS summary cites 155,669, a discrepancy):
  - A plan-making script raised turnout **4.1 pp among those contacted**.
  - In **single-eligible-voter households**, the effect was **+9.1 pp**. Multiple-voter households were unaffected.
  - A standard encouragement call and a self-prediction script had no significant impact.
  - A later study notes the result is **largely unreplicated**.
  — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797609359326); [Rogers publication page](https://scholar.harvard.edu/todd_rogers/publications/do-you-have-voting-plan-implementation-intentions-voter-turnout-and-organic); [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-increase-voter-turnout)

**Gratitude / "thank you for voting"**
- **Panagopoulos (2011, JOP)**, "Thank You for Voting: Gratitude Expression and Voter Mobilization":
  - Three randomized field experiments. Prior voters who received a thank-you postcard voted **2–3 pp** more than controls; in Georgia, **2.4–3.1 pp**.
  - Standard GOTV reminders fell "somewhere in the middle."
  - The effect was robust across subgroups, including low- and high-propensity voters, and the author distinguishes it from social pressure.
  — [ISPS](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps11-017); [Fordham news](https://news.fordham.edu/?p=32194)

**Handwritten / personalized notes**
- **Vote Forward handwritten letters** (organization's own RCTs; the 2020 study was peer-reviewed by the Analyst Institute):

  | Election | Estimated turnout effect |
  |---|---|
  | 2017 Alabama special | **3.4 pp** |
  | 2018 Ohio special | **1.4 pp** (just short of significance) |
  | 2019 Virginia | **1.9** and **1.1 pp** (two campaigns) |
  | 2020 general | **0.8 pp** (range 0.5–0.8) |
  | 2024 | about **0.2 pp** among low-propensity voters |
  | 2025 | **0.3–0.5 pp** (the group attributes the decline to saturation of control groups) |

  Overall, **22 of 29** tests showed positive effects. — [2017–18 experiments](https://votefwd.org/blog/2017-and-2018-experiments); [Virginia 2019](https://votefwd.org/blog/virginia-2019); [2020 General](https://votefwd.org/blog/2020-general-election); [2024 results](https://votefwd.org/blog/vote-forwards-2024-results-show-that-to-win-elections-its-time-to-rethink-voter-outreach-based-on-research-amp-transparency); [2025 results](https://votefwd.org/blog/2025-results); [Campaigns & Elections](https://campaignsandelections.com/industry-news/nonprofit-group-letter-writing-campaign-boosted-voter-turnout-in-2020/)
- **Handwritten vs printed** (Blue Wave Analytics, six postcard formats): **partly preprinted cards with a handwritten closing and signature performed as well as fully handwritten ones**, and volunteers can produce more of them per hour. — [Blue Wave Analytics PDF](https://actionnetwork.org/user_files/user_files/000/037/479/original/Blue_Wave_Analytics_Postcard_Best_Practices_Data_and_Analysis.pdf)

**Polling-place and logistics information**
- Youth-vote experiments (2000): turnout was about **3 pp higher** when polling-place information was provided, but the estimate was "not statistically robust." — [Green & Gerber, "Getting Out the Youth Vote" (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/209410165_Getting_Out_the_Youth_Vote_Results_from_Randomized_Field_Experiments)
- Israel, **16,005 voters**: GOTV effectiveness depended on distance to the polling place; effects were largest for voters about **400–800 m** away. — [Waismel-Manor, Canetti, Sarid & Nickerson, SSRN](https://www.ssrn.com/abstract=2301595)

**General**
- An early Green & Gerber summary concludes that "message variations make little difference" relative to the mode of contact. **[secondhand; the snippet's source paper was not pinned down]** — [Gerber & Green 2000 APSR (PDF mirror)](https://jwolf-ra.wdfiles.com/local--files/field-experiments/Canvassing%20-%20Gerber_Green_2000.pdf)

### Inferences
- **On a door hanger**, the evidence-backed additions are low-risk and cheap:
  - A short handwritten line plus first-name signature ("Sorry we missed you. —Jen, your neighbor on Elm St."). Partial handwriting appears to work about as well as full handwriting.
  - A "most of your neighbors vote / join them" **high-turnout norm** line, not "turnout is low."
  - A **plan-making prompt** with checkboxes: When will you vote? Early in-person Oct 20–Nov 1 or Election Day Nov 3; what time; how will you get there.
  - **Where/when** information: clerk early-voting location and hours, and the polling place.
  - Gratitude framing ("Thank you for being a voter").

  None of these features has been tested *on door hangers*. Expect them to add a fraction of a point at most.
- **Avoid true social-pressure content** that shows a household's or neighbors' voting history. It is the strongest message in mail tests, but backlash is documented (Wisconsin recall; Mann 2010; Colorado 2019). A school-district-adjacent volunteer group in a small community is especially exposed to reputational cost. The referendum also needs yes votes, not just turnout, and annoyed recipients may vote no.
- Single-voter households responded most to plan-making (Nickerson & Rogers), so a plan-making prompt may be most useful at apartments or single-occupant addresses.

### Gaps
- I found **no randomized test of message content on door hangers** specifically (social norm, plan-making, gratitude, or handwritten note on a hanger).
- I found no experiment testing a "we'll thank you for voting" (prospective gratitude) message. Panagopoulos tested thanks for *past* voting.
- I found no study isolating an **early-voting-specific** reminder or early-voting information on literature.
- A reported Resistbot 2019 experiment (reminder with polling location and hours, about +1.8 pp, replicating smaller 2018 results) surfaced in search, but I could not tie it to a verifiable source page.
- I could not retrieve the validated-turnout results of Gerber & Rogers-style descriptive-norm tests (the Hawthorne, CA follow-up), or plan-making results delivered by mail or print.

## 4. Evidence for local, ballot-measure, and school-referendum elections; persuasion vs mobilization

### Takeaway
- **Turnout**: face-to-face canvassing works across local elections, and one precinct-randomized canvass on a **local ballot measure** raised turnout from 29.1% to 33.5%.
- **Persuasion**: ballot measures are the main exception to the "minimal persuasive effects" finding for campaign contact. Without a party label, voters can be moved, so message content matters more for a referendum than for a candidate race.
- **No randomized GOTV experiment on a US school tax or bond referendum** turned up in my searches.
- **Wisconsin context**: school referendums pass at higher rates in higher-turnout November elections than in spring elections (2024: 77.5% vs 60.2%). Low-turnout special elections skew older (Kogan, Lavertu & Peskowitz).

### Cited Findings
**Local and ballot-measure experiments**
- **Local elections**: Green, Gerber & Nickerson (2003, JOP), six door-to-door experiments in 2001 local elections. Face-to-face mobilization "was effective in stimulating voter turnout across a wide spectrum of local elections." — [Temple PDF](https://sites.temple.edu/nickerson/files/2017/07/Green_Gerber_Nickerson.JOP_.2003.pdf)
- **Ballot-measure canvass**: Arceneaux (2005), a precinct-randomized canvassing experiment on a local **bus-service ballot measure**:
  - **14 treatment and 14 control precincts**.
  - Turnout was **29.1% in control precincts vs 33.5% in treatment precincts** (precinct-level ITT, about +4.4 pp).
  - I could not confirm the city from my sources; it is commonly described as Kansas City.
  — [Gerber & Green Handbook chapter, J-PAL PDF](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/Gerber%20Green%20Handbook.pdf) **[via search snippet]**
- **Persuasion**: Kalla & Broockman (2018, APSR), "The Minimal Persuasive Effects of Campaign Contact in General Elections: Evidence from 49 Field Experiments":
  - A meta-analysis of 40 experiments plus 9 new ones estimates an **average persuasive effect of zero in general elections** (candidate races).
  - Exception: campaign contact **can persuade in primaries and ballot-measure elections**, where voters lack a party cue.
  — [APSR](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/abs/minimal-persuasive-effects-of-campaign-contact-in-general-elections-evidence-from-49-field-experiments/753665A313C4AB433DBF7110299B7433); [eScholarship PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt103775sx/qt103775sx.pdf); [SSN key findings](https://scholars.org/sites/scholars/files/ssn-key-findings-kalla-and-broockman-on-what-campaigns-need-to-know-about-the-difficulty-of-persuading.pdf)
- **Persuasion**: Rogers & Middleton (2015), "Are Ballot Initiative Outcomes Influenced by the Campaigns of Independent Groups? A Precinct-Randomized Field Experiment Showing That They Are" (*Political Behavior*). Precinct-randomized mailed voter guides on ballot initiatives; the title reports a positive effect on outcomes. **Effect sizes not retrieved.** — [HKS PDF](https://appext.hks.harvard.edu/publications/getFile.aspx?Id=943)
- **Candidate canvassing** (not a ballot measure): a candidate-canvassing experiment found a large **29 pp persuasion effect among unaffiliated voters** but no meaningful turnout effect. This shows door contact can move *choices* even when it does not move turnout. — [Castillo et al., "What Persuades Voters?" (GMU working paper)](https://www3.gmu.edu/schools/chss/economics/icesworkingpapers.gmu.edu/pdf/1031.pdf?gmuw-rd=sm&gmuw-rdm=ht)

**School tax elections**
- Kogan, Lavertu & Peskowitz (2018, AJPS), "Election Timing, Electorate Composition, and Policy Outcomes: Evidence from School Districts":
  - Data: more than **10,000 school tax referenda** linked to voter micro-targeting data.
  - Election timing changes who votes, **mainly by age**: older residents are the most overrepresented group in low-turnout special elections.
  - Shifts in partisanship, ideology, and interest-group strength are modest.
  — [OSU Glenn College](https://glenn.osu.edu/research-and-impact/election-timing-electorate-composition-and-policy-outcomes-evidence-school)
- In a typical school board election, most voters do not have school-age children (related work by the same group). — [EdWorkingPaper 23-841](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai23-841_0.pdf)

**Wisconsin referendum context**
- **2024**:
  - Fall passage was **77.5%** vs **60.2%** in spring.
  - Full-year rate was **70.1%**, continuing a decline from the **89.7%** peak in 2018.
  - Spring 2024: **62 of 103** passed, the lowest share in a midterm or presidential year since 2010.
  - The Wisconsin Policy Forum notes that passage rates "typically increase amid the higher voter turnout of presidential and midterm election years."
  — [WPF, "Wisconsin sets more referenda records"](https://wispolicyforum.org/research/wisconsin-sets-more-referenda-records-to-fund-schools/); [WPF, "School referenda passage rates remain low"](https://wispolicyforum.org/research/school-referenda-passage-rates-remain-low/); [PBS Wisconsin](https://pbswisconsin.org/news-item/wisconsin-voters-approve-a-record-number-of-school-referendums-and-new-funding-levels-in-2024/)
- **2020**: about 87% passed in April; **43 of 51 (84.3%)** passed in November. — [WPR](https://wpr.org/wisconsin-voters-approved-84-percent-school-referendums-election)
- **April 7, 2026 spring election**:
  - **46 of 75** school questions passed (about 61%).
  - Nonrecurring operating requests: **32 of 56** passed. Recurring: **5 of 6**. Debt: **9 of 12**.
  - **11** questions were approved by **83 or fewer votes**.
  - Wisconsin Watch puts the 61% below the **70% average for 2020–2025** and above **2025's 56%**.
  — [Wisconsin Examiner](https://wisconsinexaminer.com/2026/04/09/wisconsin-schools-struggling-under-funding-system-consider-next-steps-after-referendum-results/); [WPR](https://www.wpr.org/politics/referendums/wisconsin-vote-75-school-referendums); [Wisconsin Watch](https://wisconsinwatch.org/2026/04/wisconsin-voters-school-district-referendum-funding-support-property-tax-election-2026/); [Spectrum News](https://spectrumnews1.com/wi/milwaukee/news/2026/04/08/wisconsin-school-referendum-results-spring-election-2026)
- In 2024 February and April ballots, **66.0%** of referendum questions sought property-tax increases for **operations**, driven by inflation outpacing revenue-limit increases. — [WPF via search summary](https://wispolicyforum.org/research/school-referenda-passage-rates-remain-low/) **[secondhand]**

### Inferences
- **Mobilization is not neutral in a referendum.** GOTV raises turnout among whoever it reaches. Untargeted door-hanging in mixed neighborhoods mobilizes some "no" voters too. The two levers are **targeting** (likely supporters: parent households, younger voters, past yes-precincts) and **message** (school-quality, renewal, "no new tax rate increase" framing, if accurate).
- The ballot-measure exception to minimal persuasion means **content on the hanger matters more here than in a candidate race**. Clear, factual statements of what the renewal is (for example, an operating renewal vs a new increase) could move undecided voters or reduce no-votes. Turnout effects alone may be smaller in a high-turnout governor's race.
- Fall elections already bring a broader, younger electorate than April or special elections (Kogan et al.; WPF). Much of the "turnout boost" that helps school measures comes from the November date itself. The marginal volunteer contact is then most valuable for **(a) low-propensity supporters**, and **(b) making sure supporters who vote for governor also complete the referendum question** at the end of the ballot (roll-off; see Gaps).
- Wisconsin operating referendums have been decided by very thin margins (11 of 75 in April 2026 by 83 or fewer votes). Even 0.5–1 vote per volunteer shift, multiplied by dozens of volunteers, could be decisive in a close MCPASD race.

### Gaps
- I found **no randomized GOTV or persuasion experiment specific to a US school tax, levy, bond, or operating referendum**, and no published campaign evaluation measuring door-hanger effects in one.
- No data turned up on **ballot roll-off** (voters skipping down-ballot referendum questions) in Wisconsin November elections, or on whether literature reduces it.
- I could not retrieve effect sizes for Rogers & Middleton (2015), or Arceneaux's (2005/2007) persuasion findings on the ballot measure.
- I could not get Wisconsin November 2022 (midterm) school referendum passage rates, the closest analog to Nov 2026.
- This brief does not cover MCPASD-specific referendum details (amount, recurring vs nonrecurring, prior results).

## 5. Approximate cost per vote across modes (Green & Gerber and others), applied to a 2-hour volunteer

### Takeaway
Green & Gerber's 2004 cost table, under its stated assumptions, puts door-to-door canvassing at **about $19 per vote**, volunteer phone banks at **$35**, leafleting at **$43**, direct mail at **$67**, and commercial calls at **$45–$200**. A later edition's example gives about **$31 per vote** for canvassing. For unpaid volunteers, the useful metric is **votes per volunteer-hour**. On that basis, knocking and hanging land in the same rough range (about 0.2–0.8 votes per hour each). **Knocking, and leaving a hanger wherever no one answers**, likely beats either pure strategy.

### Cited Findings
- **Green & Gerber 2004 cost table**:

  | Mode | Cost per vote |
  |---|---|
  | Door-to-door canvassing | **$19** |
  | Volunteer phone bank | **$35** |
  | Leafleting | **$43** |
  | Commercial live calls with coaching | **$45** |
  | Direct mail | **$67** |
  | Commercial live calls without coaching | **$200** |

  Canvassing assumptions: **1 vote per 14 contacts**, **12 contacts/hour**, **$16/hour** wage. Leafleting assumption: **1 vote per 200 people**. — [Chronicle of Philanthropy](https://www.philanthropy.com/news/cost-of-door-to-door-canvassing-is-19-per-vote-research-finds/); [GOTV book PDF mirror](https://groups.io/g/psychologyonline/attachment/219/0/Get%20out%20the%20vote%20%20how%20to%20increase%20voter%20turnout.pdf)
- A later edition's worked example: a canvass that "successfully contact[s] every fourth person" generates votes at about **$31 per vote**. Edition not confirmed. — [GOTV book PDF mirror](https://groups.io/g/psychologyonline/attachment/219/0/Get%20out%20the%20vote%20%20how%20to%20increase%20voter%20turnout.pdf) **[secondhand via search summary]**
- **Gerber & Green 1999**: about **6.3 pp per contact**, at 10 contacts/hour and $10/hour, gives about **$16 per vote**. — [PNAS PDF](https://www.uvm.edu/~dguber/POLS234/articles/gerber.pdf)
- **CIRCLE compilation**:
  - Partisan canvass: **$10.40 per vote** (7.6 pp ITT).
  - Young voters reached by canvass: **$11–$14** per new voter.
  - Michigan 2002 Democratic leaflet drop: **$14 per vote, excluding overhead**.
  — [CIRCLE PDF](https://circle.tufts.edu/sites/default/files/2020-02/young_voters_mobilization_tactics.pdf)
- **Michigan 2002 partisan experiment**: door hangers, volunteer calls, and visits had **"similar cost-effectiveness."** — [IDEAS, HKS working paper](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ecl/harjfk/rwp04-018.html)
- **Social-pressure mail** has been summarized at about **$1.93 per vote**, vs about $20 for canvassing. **[secondhand, from a search summary; the original source was not pinned down]** — [Adam Brown summary of GGL 2008](https://adambrown.info/p/notes/gerber_green_and_larimer_social_pressure_and_voter_turnout)
- **Caveat from Green & Gerber**: cost figures come from particular campaigns and may not transfer. — [Star Tribune commentary](https://www.startribune.com/political-engagement-the-high-cost-low-efficacy-of-going-door-to-door/567814952) **[secondhand]**

### Inferences
These are illustrative calculations for a 2-hour volunteer. **Throughput and contact-rate inputs are my assumptions, not sourced; replace them with local data** (for example, from the campaign's first canvass shift).

**Option A: knock every door.**
- Inputs: suppose 2 hours yields 40–50 doors knocked, with 20–30% answering. That is about 8–15 conversations, below Green & Gerber's 10–12 contacts/hour paid-canvasser assumption.
- At 2.5 pp per conversation (meta-analysis), that is about **0.2–0.4 votes**. At 1/14 (older low-salience), about **0.6–1.1 votes**.
- Adding hangers at the 70–80% no-answer doors (about 30–40 doors, roughly 50–70 registered voters at about 1.7 voters per household, also assumed) at about 0.5 pp per voter adds **about 0.25–0.35 votes**, for seconds per door.
- **Total for knock plus hang at not-homes: about 0.45–1.4 votes per 2-hour shift.**

**Option B: hang only.**
- Inputs: suppose 100–150 doors in 2 hours, about 170–250 registered voters.
- At about 0.5 pp per voter (Green & Gerber 1/200), about **0.85–1.25 votes**. At the Michigan 1.2 pp, about 2–3 votes. At the GMA partisan-leaflet ~0 pp, about **0**.
- Very wide uncertainty.

**Option C: split.**
- Knock (and hang at not-homes) in **high-priority turf**: likely-supporter, low- or mid-propensity households, apartments and single-voter households (where plan-making had its largest effect), and newer residents. Hang-only in lower-priority, high-propensity turf.
- This allocates scarce conversations where Arceneaux & Nickerson suggest they matter in a high-turnout election. It also gets literature, carrying information that can reduce no-votes or roll-off, onto every door.

**Conclusion**
- Taking the evidence at face value, **pure "knock everything" is unlikely to beat "knock plus hang at not-homes."**
- Pure hang-only versus knocking is roughly a wash in expected votes per hour. Hang-only has a higher ceiling but a credible zero floor; knocking has stronger evidence and adds persuasion and information value at the doors where someone answers.
- **The hybrid (C), with priority-turf knocking and hangers left at every unanswered door, is the most defensible plan.**

**Timing**
- Early in-person voting runs **Oct 20–Nov 1**. Literature with early-voting dates and locations delivered from about Oct 18–25 lets voters act on the plan-making prompt.
- Delivery closer to Election Day would better match how most GOTV tests were timed. I found no direct evidence on optimal timing.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm the 4th/5th-edition cost-per-vote table, its updated assumptions (wages, contacts per hour), or whether the book now reports volunteer-hours-per-vote. The $19/$43 figures are from the **2004** edition.
- I found **no sourced data on volunteer door-hanging throughput (doors/hour) or suburban contact rates**. Both inputs to the votes-per-hour comparison above are assumptions.
- I found no evidence on how GOTV timing interacts with early in-person absentee voting windows (whether contacts before or during the Oct 20–Nov 1 window are more effective).
