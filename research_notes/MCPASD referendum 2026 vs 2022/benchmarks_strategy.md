## 1. How did Nov. 3, 2026 statewide and Dane County school referendums compare with Nov. 2022 and April 2026?

### Takeaway
Nov. 2026 was a weak November for Wisconsin school referendums. About 45 of 79 questions passed (about 57%). That is below April 2026 (61.3%) and far below Nov. 2022 (about 79%). MCPASD's 60.1% puts it among the stronger results. Its yes share fell only about 2.8 points from 2022, while the statewide pass rate fell about 22 points. MCPASD's $118.5M, four-year nonrecurring ask was the second-largest operating request in the state, behind Green Bay's $158M. Green Bay passed narrowly, at about 51% in early returns.

### Inferences
- **MCPASD outperformed the statewide environment.** The statewide November pass rate fell about 22 points, from 79% to 57%. MCPASD's yes share fell 2.8 points. Its 60.1% beat Waunakee (52.6%) and Green Bay (about 51%), and was close to Elmbrook (61%) and Whitefish Bay (63%), the best Milwaukee-area results.
- **The decline is mostly new No votes, not lost Yes votes.** Total votes on the question rose by 522. No rose by 851 and Yes fell by 329. Some 2022 yes voters may have switched. More likely, the 2026 electorate added more no-leaning voters than yes-leaning ones, and some yes-leaning voters skipped the back-of-ballot question.
- **The "renewal" framing was only partly accurate.** Year 1 is a renewal at $24.9M. Years 2–4 escalate to $34.6M, about 39% above the 2022 ceiling. Total authority is about 1.8x 2022's, because 2022 phased in from $7.3M. A softer margin is consistent with a bigger real ask during statewide tax fatigue.
- **November 2026 ran below April 2026 statewide**, the reverse of the usual pattern (fall 2024 77.5% vs. spring 60.2%). Possible reasons: the November field was heavy with retries (16) and large asks; the governor's race may have drawn more conservative or tax-averse voters; or both. Untested.

## 2. What does research say drives school referendum success, especially in Wisconsin?

### Takeaway
The best Wisconsin-specific evidence (Kogan, Lavertu & Peskowitz 2018) finds no significant penalty for November midterms relative to presidential elections. Wisconsin's April spring elections cut the probability of passage by about 23 points. Spring electorates are about 10 points older (share 65+) and have about 4 points fewer voters with children at home. Other drivers with support are tax fatigue (the Marquette poll turned net-negative in 2026), size and escalation of the ask, renewal vs. new money, and back-of-ballot roll-off. Roll-off has anecdotal Wisconsin evidence but no systematic study found.

### Cited Findings
**Election timing and electorate composition (peer-reviewed, Wisconsin data included)**
- Kogan, Lavertu & Peskowitz, "Election Timing, Electorate Composition, and Policy Outcomes: Evidence from School Districts," *AJPS* 62(3): 637–651 (2018). The study matches more than 10,000 school tax and bond measures in California, Ohio, Texas and Wisconsin since 2000 to Catalist voter-file data. Wisconsin referenda data came from DPI, about 2000–2016, covering 78% of Wisconsin districts. — [paper PDF](https://joeornstein.github.io/pols-4641/readings/Kogan%20et%20al.%20-%202018%20-%20Election%20Timing,%20Electorate%20Composition,%20and%20Policy%20Outcomes.pdf); [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/amposc/v62y2018i3p637-651.html)
  - **Wisconsin turnout** (share of voting-age population) in presidential elections is **63.8%**. It is **−9.15 pts in midterms** and **−30.12 pts in "general statewide" (April spring) elections**. (Table 2)
  - **Wisconsin share of voters 65+** in presidential elections is **36.09%**. It is **+1.59 pts in midterms** (not significant) and **+10.01 pts in spring** (p < .001). (Table 5)
  - **Wisconsin share of voters with a child in the household** in presidential elections is **31.66%**. It is **−0.77 pts in midterms** (not significant) and **−3.99 pts in spring** (p < .001). (Table 5)
  - **Wisconsin Democratic share** in presidential elections is **50.73%**. It is **−3.14 pts in midterms** and −2.26 pts in spring. (Table 3)
  - **Wisconsin passage probability:** the baseline is **0.78** (bond measures in presidential elections, no recent failure). **Midterm −0.07** (not significant). **Spring "general statewide" −0.23** (p < .001). Primary −0.08. Special −0.18 (p < .01). (Table 6)
  - The authors conclude that "lower-turnout elections are consistently associated with less voter support and lower probability of passage in California, Ohio, and Wisconsin." The results are "consistent with seniors' being pivotal in low-turnout elections." They give the "gray peril" logic: seniors "rarely have school-age children" and "many are also on a fixed income and are thus particularly sensitive to tax increases."
  - Teachers are a larger share of the electorate in Wisconsin (4.24% in presidential years, +1.44 pts in spring) than in other states. Even so, the authors find age effects "far more consequential" than education-employee turnout.
- Parents with children are about **20–35%** of the electorate in the average school board election across 11 states. — [Ohio State News (Kogan)](https://news.osu.edu/why-parents-should-have-a-bigger-say-in-how-schools-are-run/) [secondhand via prior notes]
- Older voters without children oppose higher *state* school spending but are much more willing to support spending in their *own* district. Housing-value capitalization and intergenerational altruism both appear to matter. — [Balsdon & Brunner (2003), UConn working paper](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2003-24.pdf) [via prior notes]

**Wisconsin passage-rate patterns (WPF)**
- Passage rates "hovered around 50 percent for most of the late 2000s and early 2010s," then rose, peaking at about 90% in 2018. WPF: "Over the past several years, approval rates have trended downward." — [WPR, April 2024, citing WPF](https://wpr.org/news/wisconsin-voters-approve-just-60-percent-of-school-referendums)
- In 2024, fall passage was about 77.5% vs. spring 60.2%. The WPF says passage rates "typically increase amid the higher voter turnout of presidential and midterm election years." — [WPF](https://wispolicyforum.org/research/wisconsin-sets-more-referenda-records-to-fund-schools/) [via prior notes]; [WPF via WNA](https://wnanews.com/?p=64407)

**Tax fatigue and public opinion (2025–26)**
- Marquette Law School Poll, March 2026: asked whether they would vote for or against a school referendum, **51% said no and 48% yes**, vs. **46% no and 52% yes in June 2025**. — [WPR preview, Nov. 2026](https://www.wpr.org/news/wisconsin-school-districts-referendums-nov-3-election)
- Marquette professor Gabriel Velez: "the more people feel education is worsening, the easier it is for them to reject a referendum." — [WPR, April 2026](https://www.wpr.org/politics/referendums/wisconsin-vote-75-school-referendums)
- Opponents' argument: districts "receive more than $18,000 per pupil — slightly above national averages — so the problem is how that money is being spent." — [WPR preview](https://www.wpr.org/news/wisconsin-school-districts-referendums-nov-3-election)

**Renewals vs. new money; recurring vs. nonrecurring**
- WASB's Dan Rossmiller: conventional wisdom is that in tough economic times voters prefer limit increases that expire. When a nonrecurring referendum expires, districts are "back in the situation of having to go back to the voters ... very typically the school district is coming to ask for a bigger amount in the subsequent ask." Recurring operating referenda did well in Nov. 2020 (9 of 10 passed). — [WPR (Nov. 2020)](https://www.wpr.org/wisconsin-voters-approved-84-percent-school-referendums-election)
- A replacement referendum that fails lets taxes fall, which "would make it slightly more difficult to win over voters if the district tries again ... since it would mean a property tax increase" (Wausau example). — [WPR (Nov. 2020)](https://www.wpr.org/wisconsin-voters-approved-84-percent-school-referendums-election)
- Green Bay framed its larger renewal as "tax rate neutral." WBAY noted that "does not guarantee that each individual tax bill would stay the same." — [WBAY](https://www.wbay.com/2026/11/04/wisconsin-voters-decide-79-school-referendum-questions-including-four-northeast-wisconsin/)

**Size of ask (MCPASD's own survey evidence)**
- The 2022 MCPASD pre-referendum survey showed support falling with the size of the ask: **$20M: 64%; $24.9M: 58%; $30M: 52%**. The $24.9M question then passed with 62.9%. — [MCPASD Nov. 2022 presentation](https://clubrunner.blob.core.windows.net/00000006121/en-ca/files/homepage/m-cp-school-district-2022-referendum/Nov-2022-Referendum-M-CP-area-school-district-Presentation.pdf)
- The 2026 Donovan Group "Operational Needs Survey" (May 6–25, 2026) found **66%** would "probably support/definitely support" a November 2026 referendum. Non-supporters cited "property tax fatigue," a "preference for structural or state-level solutions," "spending reductions, greater fiscal discipline, and greater transparency." — [MCPASD Board recap, June 2026](https://www.mcpasd.k12.wi.us/o/mcpasd/article/2975849)

**Ballot position and roll-off**
- The Herman-Neosho-Rubicon (Dodge County) April 2024 referendum was the last question on the ballot and was on the back in two of the district's largest communities, "so voters may not have seen it." This is offered as a possible explanation for its failure. — [WPR, April 2024](https://wpr.org/news/wisconsin-voters-approve-just-60-percent-of-school-referendums) [back-of-ballot detail via search summary]
- No systematic Wisconsin study of back-of-ballot roll-off on school questions, and no WEC guidance on "check both sides" reminders, was found.

**State budget timing**
- The 2025–27 state budget "kept general school aid flat at $5.58 billion for the third consecutive year, providing zero inflationary increase." Meanwhile, "declining enrollment has hit nearly every district in the state." — [WPR preview](https://www.wpr.org/news/wisconsin-school-districts-referendums-nov-3-election)
- In February 2026, school districts, parents and advocacy groups sued the Legislature alleging systematic underfunding. — [WPF April 2026 report](https://wispolicyforum.org/research/k-12-referenda-add-to-revenue-gaps/) [secondhand]
- WPF warns of "a growing imbalance between districts based on voters' willingness and financial capacity to approve local school district referenda." — [WPF via WNA](https://wnanews.com/?p=64407)

**Partisan lean**
- Spring 2026: despite "a landslide victory for the liberal-leaning candidate in the state Supreme Court race," many school referendums were close. Partisan tides did not carry referendums. — [WPR, April 2026](https://www.wpr.org/politics/referendums/wisconsin-vote-75-school-referendums)
- Nov. 2022: in 61 of 81 school referendums, the yes vote exceeded Evers's vote. — [UpNorthNews](https://upnorthnewswi.com/news/election-results-prove-democrat-or-republican-voters-want-more-funding-for-public-schools/) [secondhand]

### Inferences
- **For 2030, November is strongly preferred over April.** The Wisconsin coefficients imply roughly a 23-point lower chance of passage in spring. Spring electorates have about 10 points more seniors and about 4 points fewer parents. MCPASD's 60% cushion is large, but a bigger escalating ask in a spring electorate is a real risk. An April 2030 attempt would only make sense as a deliberate first try that leaves November 2030 as the fallback, before authority lapses after 2030-31.
- **Escalating renewals erode the "renewal" message.** Rossmiller's "bigger amount in the subsequent ask" describes MCPASD's 2022→2026 path ($24.9M ceiling → $34.6M ceiling). A 2030 renewal starting at $34.6M with further step-ups would face the same erosion. A recurring question (a permanent base increase) is the structural alternative. It ends the four-year cycle but is a harder ask in tax-averse years.
- **Tax-fatigue messaging matters more than turnout alone.** The Marquette shift (52–46 yes in 2025 to 48–51 no in 2026) and the district's own survey themes (fiscal discipline, transparency, state-level fix) match the No side's Nextdoor arguments recorded below.

### Gaps

From background knowledge, these exist and are relevant; do not cite specifics without checking.

## 4. What's next: when MCPASD must return to voters, the recurring option, and upcoming civic dates

### Takeaway
The 2026 authority covers 2027-28 through 2030-31, so new authority must be in place for 2031-32. The natural renewal date is the **Nov. 5, 2030** general election, a midterm like 2022 and 2026. **April 2, 2030** is an earlier option that leaves November as a retry. The research above favors November. Next on the civic calendar is the **April 6, 2027** spring election. Per the Open Seats site, **two existing Area seats convert to open, any-resident seats** that cycle. These are conversions, not new seats; the board stays at nine.

### Cited Findings
- The 2026 authority covers four school years: 2027-28 ($24.9M), 2028-29 ($27.9M), 2029-30 ($31.1M) and 2030-31 ($34.6M). — [WKOW](https://www.wkow.com/news/middleton-cross-plains-school-district-referendum-passes/article_a78a2ddc-8a60-40f1-a2e3-96bff2443aa3.html)
- Wisconsin law generally limits districts to two revenue-limit referendums per calendar year, held on regularly scheduled spring or fall election days (with one exception). The next scheduled date after Nov. 2026 is April 6, 2027. — [WebSearch summary of Wisconsin referendum rules](https://dpi.wi.gov/sfs/reporting/safr/referenda-info) [secondhand; verify against Wis. Stat. § 121.91(3)]
- **Open Seats phase-in:** "APRIL 2027: Two Area seats reaching re-election convert to open seats." "APRIL 2028: A third converts." "APRIL 2030: The fourth completes the transition — 5 Area anchors + 4 open = 9 seats." — [Open Seats MCPASD site (local repo `index.html`)](file:///home/user/openseatsmcpasd/index.html)
- Citizen Portal's summary: the change shifts the nine-member board to five geographic and four at-large seats, "phased in at normal reelection points beginning in spring 2027"; vote 60 yes, 46 no. — [Citizen Portal](https://citizenportal.ai/articles/10156469/Wisconsin/School-Districts/Middleton-Cross-Plains-Area-School-District/Voters-approve-change-to-school-board-apportionment-after-divided-public-comment) [secondhand]
- Existing board structure: nine members with three-year terms, "three new seats are open for election each year." Members are "selected from specific geographic areas, but represent the District as a whole. During elections, residents vote for candidates in all areas." Voting was already district-wide; the change concerns who may run. — [MCPASD Board Candidate Information](https://www.mcpasd.k12.wi.us/article/918993)
- **Calendar (computed):**
  - Spring elections (first Tuesday in April): **April 6, 2027; April 4, 2028; April 3, 2029; April 2, 2030**.
  - Nov. general elections: **Nov. 7, 2028 (presidential); Nov. 5, 2030 (midterm/gubernatorial)**.
  - Computed by date arithmetic.
- Precedent for recurring operating referenda in Dane County: MMSD's 2020 operating question was recurring and passed with over 75%. — [WPR (Nov. 2020)](https://www.wpr.org/wisconsin-voters-approved-84-percent-school-referendums-election)

### Inferences
- **Option A: Nov. 5, 2030 nonrecurring renewal** (status quo path). Same midterm electorate type as 2022 and 2026, which Kogan et al. show is not significantly worse than presidential in Wisconsin. Risks: the ask likely starts at or above $34.6M with further step-ups; renewal fatigue builds; and a failure leaves no regular election before the 2031-32 school year begins (only an April 2031 retry, after budgets are set).
- **Option B: April 2, 2030 first attempt, Nov. 2030 fallback.** This buys a retry, but the spring electorate is older with fewer parents (−0.23 passage probability in Wisconsin). A spring failure could also feed a "they keep asking" narrative.
- **Option C: Nov. 7, 2028 presidential-year recurring question.** This uses the highest-turnout, most parent-heavy electorate to convert part of the base to recurring and end the four-year cycle. It would layer onto existing authority, which invites the "stacking" critique the board president rebutted in 2026 ("It's not stacking, it's just renewing"). It is speculative and needs board-level financial modeling.
- **State budget timing:** the 2027–29 and 2029–31 state budgets (enacted around mid-2027 and mid-2029) will be known before any 2030 vote. A meaningful revenue-limit or general-aid increase could shrink the 2030 ask. Continued flat aid makes the escalation larger.
- **April 2027 board race:** two open seats give the yes coalition (parents, Retired Educators, WEAC lists) a reason to keep its list, volunteer network and MiniVAN turfs active. Board composition will shape the 2030 question's size and structure. Nomination papers for an April 2027 school board race are typically due early January 2027 (first Tuesday = Jan. 5, 2027); verify with the district clerk.

## Yes campaign 2026 field facts (internal, from the campaign tracker; not independently verified)

### Takeaway
The Yes Committee ran a volunteer field and earned-media campaign: WEAC-list canvass turfs in MiniVAN, about 1,400 door hangers, about 460 yard signs, business signs, video testimonials and four earned-media hits. All items: Source: internal Yes-campaign tracker, as provided by the coordinator (not independently verified).

### Inferences
- **Field coverage gap:**
  - Only 25 of 45 turfs were claimed, and 8 were completed (plus 1 half done). That means roughly 18–20% of turfs were fully worked.
  - About 849 of about 2,102 targeted doors (about 40%) were in turfs no one claimed.
  - Apartments, likely home to many newer, younger and renter residents in a growing district, were skipped.
  - A 2030 campaign has clear headroom from earlier recruitment, earlier hanger printing and an apartment strategy.

The district's baseline support is strong. Field work likely mattered at the margin, but its effect cannot be separated without ward-level turnout and canvass-contact data.
- **The No side was disorganized yet No votes still rose by about 850.** That suggests the drivers were ambient: tax fatigue, size of ask, electorate composition. An organized opposition was not the cause.
