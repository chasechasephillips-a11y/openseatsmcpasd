# District trust, record, finances and reputation as levers on school operating referendums (Wisconsin and MCPASD, Nov. 3, 2026)

Scope note: these notes cover district-level and institutional factors. Voter demographics, partisanship, turnout and canvassing are covered in other notes. MCPASD's 2026 result (**60.1%, 14,263 Yes to 9,457 No**) is **unofficial** until the Dane County canvass, which must be certified by Nov. 17, 2026. Facts marked **[secondhand]** come from summaries, such as Citizen Portal meeting digests or earlier project notes, not from primary documents I could open. Private individuals quoted in news coverage are described by role, not by name.

## 1. Trust and satisfaction: do trust in the board and administration, perceived efficiency, communication and controversies predict support?

### Takeaway
Satisfaction with schools and willingness to fund them used to move together, and national and Wisconsin data show both slipping. Telling voters what schools already spend consistently cuts support for more money, and in Wisconsin the willingness to vote Yes on referendums in general turned negative in 2026. In MCPASD's own survey, the No side's concerns were about trust and efficiency ("fiscal discipline," "transparency," "room to cut"), not dissatisfaction with school quality. The district had no recent scandal, superintendent turnover or labor dispute that surfaced online.

### Cited Findings
**National polling (trust, grades, spending information)**
- In the 2025 PDK Poll (released Aug. 19, 2025), **43%** gave their community's schools an A or B, down from 53% in 2013. Only **13%** gave the nation's schools an A or B, down from 26% in 2004. A majority said teachers are underpaid. The poll surveyed 1,005 adults, ±3.9 points. — [EdWeek, Aug. 2025](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/what-americans-really-think-of-public-schools/2025/08); [PDK 2025 press release](https://pdkintl.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/2025-PDK-Poll-Press-Release-Embargoed.pdf); [CSBA blog summary](https://blog.csba.org/pdk-poll-25/)
- In Gallup's June 2025 confidence-in-institutions survey, **44% of Democrats** but only **18% of Republicans** expressed high confidence in public schools. I did not retrieve the all-adult figure. — [Deseret News, July 22, 2025](https://www.deseret.com/politics/2025/07/22/do-americans-trust-u-s-institutions-military-police-science-technology); [Gallup 2025 topline PDF](https://news.gallup.com/file/poll/692660/2025_07_17_Confidence%20in%20Institutions%20Topline%20and%20Tabs.pdf)
- **Information experiment (Education Next 2016, an older but long-running design).** Among respondents *not* told their district's per-pupil spending, **61%** wanted spending increased, 34% kept the same and 5% cut. Among those told the actual figure, **45%** wanted it increased, 45% kept the same and 10% cut. Respondents guessed local per-pupil spending at **$7,020** against an actual **$12,369**. — [The 74, Aug. 23, 2016](https://www.the74million.org/article/parents-like-tests-but-dont-know-how-much-school-costs-4-takeaways-from-the-new-ednext-poll)
- Education Next headlines that when the public is told current teacher pay, "only 36 percent support raising teacher salaries." The survey year is not visible in the retrieved snippet. In 2022, **72%** of those *not* told salaries said they should increase. — [Education Next EdStat](https://www.educationnext.org/edstat-public-told-much-teachers-currently-earn-36-percent-support-raising-teacher-salaries/); [EdSource summary](https://edsource.org/terms/education-spending)
- EdChoice's 2025 polling found Americans and school parents guessed per-pupil spending at about **$6,000** and **$5,000**, against an actual **$15,591** (FY22). When shown their state's figure, only **38%** said it was "too low." — [EdChoice, 2025](https://www.edchoice.org/establishing-a-baseline-how-do-school-parents-feel-in-2025/)

**Wisconsin polling**
- Marquette Law School Poll: when asked how they would vote on a school referendum, Wisconsin voters split **48% Yes / 51% No in March 2026**, against **52% Yes / 46% No in June 2025**. — [WPR preview, Nov. 2026](https://www.wpr.org/news/wisconsin-school-districts-referendums-nov-3-election) [secondhand, via earlier project notes]; [Marquette Law School Poll, Feb. 2026 release](https://law.marquette.edu/poll/2026/02/25/mlsp87-release/)

- Marquette's August poll found **58%** preferred property-tax cuts over more K-12 funding, as cited by Channel 3000. — [earlier project notes citing Channel 3000, Sept. 28 and Nov. 3, 2026] [secondhand]
- Marquette professor Gabriel Velez: "the more people feel education is worsening, the easier it is for them to reject a referendum." — [WPR, April 2026](https://www.wpr.org/politics/referendums/wisconsin-vote-75-school-referendums)

This is the firm's own claim, not independently verified. — [School Perceptions, BlogId=141](https://www.schoolperceptions.com/BlogPost.php?BlogId=141)

**Staff as an institutional constituency**
- Hartney and Kogan (June 2026) linked Ohio school employees to voter files. Adults became significantly more likely to vote in low-turnout school board elections after they started working in traditional public schools, though not in charter schools, and the effect extended to their household members. Still, voters with a direct employment stake are "a surprisingly modest share of the electorate." — [Hartney & Kogan, "The Electoral Power of the Teacher," PEPG 26-09, 2026](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/Taubman/PEPG/research/PEPG26_09.pdf)

### Inferences
- MCPASD's No vote (about 40%) formed without an organized campaign or a scandal. It reads as baseline tax fatigue and efficiency skepticism, matching the survey's own non-supporter themes, rather than a trust collapse. The 2026 statewide shift in Marquette's referendum item (+6 to −3 net) is a plausible headwind that MCPASD shared with every district.
- The pattern fits the Education Next and EdChoice information experiments: "room to cut" and "show me where the money goes" are the questions that flip informed, less-attached voters. That makes concrete budget transparency, such as side-by-side pass and fail budgets and a public cuts tracker, a plausible Yes lever. I found no causal test of it at the district level.
- The 2020 COVID-era recall attempt is the only public trust shock found. It failed, and both targeted incumbents were re-elected in 2022, months before the 2022 referendum won 62.9%. That suggests the COVID controversy did not durably erode support, though it cannot be ruled out as a minor factor for some voters.
- A high-performing, stable district gets less "trust dividend" than it used to, if DeMeuse's observation holds. Satisfaction no longer converts to Yes votes as reliably when cost-of-living pressure is high.

### Gaps
- No post-election survey of MCPASD voters links trust or satisfaction measures to vote choice. The full Donovan Group report (question wording, trust items, subgroup tables, weighting) was not available. The 47% weighted figure is secondhand.
- I found no Wisconsin peer-reviewed study that measures trust in a board or administration and links it to referendum outcomes. The School Perceptions trust claims are practitioner case studies.
- I could not retrieve the 2025 or 2026 Education Next informed-versus-uninformed spending figures, or Gallup's all-adult 2025 public-schools confidence figure.
- I searched for MCPASD curriculum, equity-policy, gender-identity-policy and labor disputes from 2023 to 2026 and found none in online coverage. Middleton Times-Tribune and State Journal archives are largely paywalled, so smaller controversies may have been missed.

## 2. School quality signals: do test scores, report card ratings or rankings raise or lower support?

### Takeaway
The best causal evidence (Ohio, NCLB era) shows that **negative** official labels cut levy passage. Failing AYP lowered the odds of passing a levy by about 10% in relative terms, even where students were making above-average gains. Evidence that **positive** ratings raise support is thinner. MCPASD went into 2026 with the state's top rating for the first time ("Significantly Exceeds Expectations," five stars, 84.4), yet its margin fell. That is consistent with quality signals mattering mainly as a downside risk, and with the "you're already great, why more?" counter-argument.

### Cited Findings
- **Kogan, Lavertu & Peskowitz (AJPS, 2016; posted 2015).** The study covered all Ohio school tax levies during NCLB. A district's AYP status "significantly affected" its ability to raise local tax revenue, and districts that failed AYP were **10% less likely** to pass a levy (a relative figure). About **70%** of Ohio districts labeled failing were average or above average in learning gains, but they still lost support. The burden fell hardest on districts serving many poor and minority students. — [AJPS blog summary, May 15, 2015](https://ajps.org/2015/05/15/federal-education-reforms-backfired-undermining-public-support-for-some-schools/); [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/amposc/v60y2016i2p418-435.html)
- **Kogan, Lavertu & Peskowitz (JPAM, 2016).** The authors found "little evidence" that poor performance on widely disseminated Ohio state and federal indicators affects school board turnover, incumbent vote share or superintendent tenure. They concluded that releasing performance information applies "little, if any, electoral pressure" on officials. — [IDEAS abstract, JPAM 35(3)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/jpamgt/v35y2016i3p639-661.html)
- Taken together, these suggest that voters punish districts through **tax votes** more than through **board elections** when given a bad label (inference from the two abstracts above).
- **Figlio & Kenny (J. Public Economics, 2009; pre-2015).** Florida school letter grades had "substantial effects" on schools' ability to raise private contributions. Donations to schools serving poor or minority families were more sensitive to grades. — [Northwestern IPR working paper](https://www.ipr.northwestern.edu/documents/working-papers/2009/IPR-WP-09-08.pdf); [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/pubeco/v93y2009i9-10p1069-1077.html)
- **Figlio & Lucas (AER, 2004; pre-2015).** Florida house prices responded to school letter grades beyond the underlying test-score information, so official labels carry their own signal. — [AER article page](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/0002828041464489)
- **Wisconsin outcome effects (the case *for* operating money).** Baron (AEJ: Economic Policy, 2022) used close Wisconsin revenue-limit referendums in a dynamic regression discontinuity design. Passing an **operational** referendum had "substantial positive effects on test scores, dropout rates, and postsecondary enrollment." Baron put the effects at about a **9% reduction in dropouts** and a **10% increase** in completers enrolling in postsecondary education. **Capital** referendums had little impact. — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aejpol/v14y2022i1p1-39.html); [AEA Research Highlights](https://www.aeaweb.org/research/school-spending-student-outcomes-wisconsin)
- **Seven-state replication.** Abott, Kogan, Lavertu & Peskowitz (J. Public Economics, 2020) used tax elections in seven states. They estimated that **$1,000 more per pupil** in operational spending raised test scores by about **0.15 SD** and graduation rates by about **9 percentage points**. — [Academic newsletter listing](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/275317) (figures from search snippet)
- **MCPASD's rating.** On the 2024-25 DPI report cards, MCPASD earned "Significantly Exceeds Expectations" for the first time, with an overall score of **84.4**, moving from a "longstanding four-star rating" to five stars. Six schools were rated Significantly Exceeds (Park, West Middleton, Glacier Creek Middle, Middleton High, Elm Lawn, Pope Farm), four Exceeds (Sunset Ridge, Northside, Kromrey, Clark Street) and one Meets (Sauk Trail). — [Madison365, Jan. 2, 2026](https://madison365.com/middleton-cross-plains-area-school-district-reports-highest-ever-dpi-report-card-scores/); [MCPASD article](https://www.mcpasd.k12.wi.us/article/2542352) (school list as summarized in search results)
- A DPI consultant quoted on the report-card redesign said of an earlier gap-closure measure: "In theory, this was a good measure for understanding gap closure, but in practice, in reality, it had a ton of problems." — [Madison365, Jan. 2, 2026](https://madison365.com/middleton-cross-plains-area-school-district-reports-highest-ever-dpi-report-card-scores/)
- NBC15 noted in campaign coverage that "the district has earned Wisconsin's highest overall school accountability rating." The Yes committee's line was "great schools don't happen by accident." — [WMTV/NBC15, Oct. 22, 2026](https://www.wmtv15news.com/2026/10/22/middleton-cross-plains-voters-weigh-school-referendum-district-faces-nearly-25m-potential-cuts/)
- After the vote, Superintendent Monogue tied passage to "low and competitive class sizes," a quality input rather than test scores. — [Cap Times, Nov. 4, 2026](https://captimes.com/news/elections/dane-county-voters-pass-three-school-funding-referendums/article_612b45b9-058a-4b7c-ae10-3712d49218ff.html)

### Inferences
- For MCPASD, the top rating mostly protected against a No argument ("failing schools don't deserve more") rather than adding Yes votes. Support slid 69% → 63% → 60% from 2018 to 2026 while ratings rose. The quality lever looks asymmetric: a downgrade is a strong No lever, while an upgrade is a weak Yes lever unless tied to a clear "this is what the money buys" story.
- Baron's Wisconsin results are the strongest evidence-based Yes message available. Operating money, specifically, improves outcomes in Wisconsin districts. I found no evidence that campaigns used it or that it persuades voters.

### Gaps
- I could not find a "Hasan/Kogan/Lavertu/Peskowitz" study on Ohio levies and test scores. The closest matches are the Kogan-Lavertu-Peskowitz papers above and Abott et al. (2020). The cited author combination may be a mis-citation.
- I found no study testing whether a positive rating (such as a move to five stars) increases referendum support, or any Wisconsin study linking DPI report card stars to referendum outcomes.
- Effect sizes for the 2016 AJPS paper in vote-share points were not retrieved, because the full text was blocked (403).

## 3. Enrollment trends, building capacity and growth: does rising or falling enrollment change support? Open enrollment.

### Takeaway
In Wisconsin, declining enrollment drives referendums mechanically: revenue limits are per-pupil, so falling membership cuts allowable revenue. I found no study showing that enrollment trends directly change voter support. MCPASD's story flipped from growth (2018 capacity referendum) to post-pandemic decline (2022) to a mixed picture by 2026, with elementary decline, secondary growth and a September 2026 enrollment bump. Open enrollment fills seats and brings revenue.

### Cited Findings
- The Wisconsin Policy Forum lists declining enrollment as a driver of referendums: it "reduces revenue under the caps." Statewide public school enrollment has declined since 2014, worsened by COVID-19. — [Wisconsin Policy Forum, April 2024](https://wispolicyforum.org/research/school-referenda-passage-rates-remain-low/); [BizTimes on WPF, April 17, 2026](https://biztimes.com/report-school-funding-gaps-widen-as-referendum-approval-rates-slip/)
- WPR's 2026 preview said "declining enrollment has hit nearly every district in the state." — [WPR, Nov. 2026](https://www.wpr.org/news/wisconsin-school-districts-referendums-nov-3-election) [secondhand, via earlier project notes]
- Eau Claire's superintendent cited "declining enrollment" alongside inflation and state underfunding as reasons districts seek referendums. — [WEAU, Nov. 4, 2026](https://www.weau.com/2026/11/04/voters-reject-34-wisconsin-school-referendums-approve-45/)
- **MCPASD 2018 to 2022.** The 2018 capital referendum addressed "building capacity issues related to growing enrollment," and the district had projected "steady enrollment increases through 2030" before the pandemic. Enrollment changes and their revenue effects were **+228** (2018-19), **+174** (2019-20), **+50** (2020-21), **−43** (2021-22, −$503,096) and **−94** est. (2022-23, −$1,100,280). The incoming kindergarten class was "110 students smaller than our 2022 graduating class," and the district blamed housing: "Price-points in our community are too high for many families with young children." — [MCPASD Nov. 2022 referendum presentation (PDF)](https://clubrunner.blob.core.windows.net/00000006121/en-ca/files/homepage/m-cp-school-district-2022-referendum/Nov-2022-Referendum-M-CP-area-school-district-Presentation.pdf) [via earlier project notes]
- **MCPASD 2025 to 2026.** The district describes itself as serving "approximately 7,100 students," 4K–12. — [MCPASD About Our District](https://www.mcpasd.k12.wi.us/page/about-our-district/). A DPI-sourced table lists 7,167. — (search-result summary; see [Madison365](https://madison365.com/middleton-cross-plains-area-school-district-reports-highest-ever-dpi-report-card-scores/))
- An April 2025 staff projection showed elementary decline and secondary growth, a net drop of about **99** students, with open-enrollment applications possibly adding up to **181**. — [Citizen Portal](https://citizenportal.ai/articles/6259238/wisconsin/school-districts/middleton-cross-plains-area-school-district/enrollment-projections-district-expects-elementary-decline-and-secondary-growth-glacier-creek-surge-noted) [secondhand]
- For 2026-27, the district set open-enrollment capacity at "up to a maximum of **324** new seats." Staff recommended only 25 freshman seats because the incoming 8th-grade class is smaller than the graduating class. — [Citizen Portal](https://citizenportal.ai/articles/7271563/wisconsin/school-districts/middleton-cross-plains-area-school-district/district-outlines-open-enrollment-policy-changes-and-projects-up-to-324-potential-new-seats) [secondhand]
- In September 2026, the district reported "nearly 200 new enrollments since June" and planned a projection update with its long-time demographer. — [Citizen Portal](https://citizenportal.ai/articles/9992654/Wisconsin/School-Districts/Middleton-Cross-Plains-Area-School-District/District-reports-nearly-200-new-enrollments-since-June-third-Friday-count-Sept-18) [secondhand]
- Third-party school sites report West Middleton Elementary falling from 491 (2018) to 334 (2025) and district 4K from 354 to 286. These figures are low reliability, unverified against DPI. — (search-result summary of third-party directories)
- **Enrollment and the electorate.** Earlier project estimates put households with a child enrolled in MCPASD at roughly **21–24%** of households. Enrollment is about 7,100–7,250, including nonresident open-enrollment students, against about 7,900 resident 5–17-year-olds. — [earlier project notes, "MCPASD referendum 2026 vs 2022"; estimate, not a reported figure]
- The district's $25.4M "funding gap" is stated as "$3,600 per student" based on 2025–26 enrollment, which ties the size of the ask directly to membership. — [WMTV/NBC15, Oct. 22, 2026](https://www.wmtv15news.com/2026/10/22/middleton-cross-plains-voters-weigh-school-referendum-district-faces-nearly-25m-potential-cuts/)
- Hesselbein warned that failure could close an elementary school, with larger class sizes elsewhere. — [Cap Times, Oct. 20, 2026](https://captimes.com/news/education/two-dane-county-school-districts-ask-voters-to-raise-property-taxes/article_56b581ca-9603-48fd-ad2e-566c6748b5bc.html)

### Inferences
- Shrinking elementary enrollment makes an elementary-closure threat credible to voters. It also hands opponents an efficiency argument ("fewer kids, why more money?").
- Open enrollment cuts both ways. Accepting nonresident students brings revenue and fills capacity, but some residents see it as subsidizing outsiders. I found no MCPASD-specific reporting of this argument in 2026.
- The September 2026 enrollment uptick came too late to change the campaign's framing. It may matter more for the 2030 renewal.

### Gaps
- DPI WISEdash third-Friday enrollment for 2021-22 through 2025-26, and MCPASD's open-enrollment in and out counts, were not retrieved. The net open-enrollment balance (whether MCPASD is a net receiver) is unknown.
- I found no peer-reviewed study estimating the effect of enrollment growth or decline on referendum vote share, in Wisconsin or nationally.
- The Wisconsin open-enrollment per-pupil transfer amount for 2025-26 and 2026-27 was not retrieved.

## 4. Fiscal structure: Wisconsin revenue limits, state aid, the $325 adjustment and "400-year" veto, ESSER, fund balance and staff pay

### Takeaway
Wisconsin's revenue limits have not kept pace with inflation since 2009. Two years of $0 per-pupil increases (2021-23), the end of about $2.4B in ESSER aid, and a $325-a-year adjustment funded largely by local levy rather than state aid have made operating referendums routine. Passage rates have fallen with repetition: about 90% in 2018, 70% in 2024, about 60% in spring 2026 and 57% in November 2026. Property-rich MCPASD gets only about 20% of its revenue from the state, so nearly every dollar of the "state funding gap" lands on the local levy. That makes the "it's the state's fault" message both true and a reason some voters prefer a state fix.

### Cited Findings
**State structure**
- Per-pupil revenue-limit increases were decoupled from inflation in 2009, so they no longer rise automatically. ## 5. Visible district actions before a vote: announced cuts, threatened cuts, layoffs, closures. Do specific threats persuade?

### Takeaway
I found no rigorous study showing that threatened cuts ("Washington Monument" tactics) increase passage. The evidence is case-based. MCPASD paired a large, specific failure consequence (about 20% cuts, possibly an elementary closure) with a pledge to cut $5M anyway and to avoid layoffs. Neighboring Waunakee published no reduction list and won by a smaller margin with a permanent ask. That contrast is confounded by structure and partisanship. Skeptical MCPASD voters asked for pass-and-fail budgets, not scarier threats.

## 6. Property value capitalization: does passing operating referendums raise home values, and does that message persuade?

### Takeaway
Strong evidence shows that school spending capitalizes into home prices: California capital bonds (about 6% within three years) and national operating spending (elasticity about 0.95, driven by teacher salaries, which is directly relevant to operating referendums). I found **no** Baron study of Wisconsin operating referendums and housing prices. I also found no evidence that the home-value argument persuades homeowners. The MCPASD campaign does not appear to have used it prominently.

### Cited Findings
- **Cellini, Ferreira & Rothstein (QJE, 2010; pre-2015).** In California districts where bond referendums narrowly passed or failed, passage raised home prices by about **3 percentage points right away and roughly double within three years**. The implied willingness to pay is **$1.50 or more per $1** of facility spending, suggesting districts underinvest. — [IDEAS record, QJE 125(1)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v125y2010i1p215-261..html); [NBER w14516](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14516); [CEPS working paper (PDF)](https://swh.princeton.edu/~ceps/workingpapers/180rothstein.pdf); [Penn working paper (PDF)](https://economics.sas.upenn.edu/sites/default/files/filevault/event_papers/Empirical101620080.pdf)
- **Bayer, Blair & Whaley (NBER w28255, 2020, rev. 2021).** Using school-finance reforms and 25 years of national house-price indexes, a **1%** rise in school spending raises house prices about **0.95%**. The effect comes mainly from **teacher salary** spending, which the authors read as causal evidence that U.S. teacher spending is "inefficiently low." An earlier version reported 0.86. — [NBER w28255](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28255); [NBER Digest, March 2021](https://www.nber.org/digest/202103/house-prices-and-local-spending-public-school-teachers)
- **Multistate bonds (IZA DP 16713).** Across 29 states, bonds raise test scores and house prices on average, but the effects differ by project. HVAC and roof upgrades raise scores but not prices, while athletic facilities raise prices but not scores. — [IZA DP 16713 (PDF)](https://docs.iza.org/dp16713.pdf)
- **Baron (2022)** studied Wisconsin student outcomes, not housing. Its design is modeled on the Cellini et al. housing approach. — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aejpol/v14y2022i1p1-39.html); [AEA conference listing](https://aeaweb.org/conference/2021/preliminary/paper/BTn2DeYn)
- **MCPASD context.** At the Sept. 17 session, a realtor and parent was among the skeptics asking for allocation detail. Being in the housing business did not translate into automatic support. — [Civic Media / Wisconsin Examiner, Sept. 21, 2026](https://civicmedia.us/news/2026/09/21/middleton-referendum-discussion-highlights-tension-between-property-taxes-and-school-funding). In districts where home values had jumped (TIF-out equalized value +47% from 2021-22 to 2025-26), the home-value message competes with assessment-driven tax anxiety. — [earlier project notes, DPI data] [secondhand]

### Inferences
- The capitalization evidence supports a "protects your home's value" Yes message, especially for operating money spent on teachers (Bayer et al.). For older homeowners not planning to sell, though, a capital gain does not offset a higher tax bill. That limits the message's reach among the fixed-income voters raised at MCPASD sessions.
- In a high-value district like MCPASD, where houses already sell at a premium, the more credible message may be loss avoidance ("a failed referendum and an elementary closure lower demand") rather than gain. That is an untested inference.

### Gaps
- I found no Wisconsin-specific estimate of how operating referendum passage affects home prices. The request's reference to "Baron on Wisconsin operating referendums" and housing could not be confirmed.
- I found no experimental or survey evidence that home-value messaging changes votes. I also found no record of whether MCPASD's district or Yes committee materials used a home-value argument in 2026.

### Inferences
- MCPASD's institutional assets (top rating, stable leadership, no scandal, no opposition, a self-restrained ask) probably help explain why it ran about **13 points** above its likely-voter-weighted survey (about 47%, secondhand) and finished in the low 60s when only 57% of the state's questions passed. Its liabilities (serial renewals, a 39% higher final-year ceiling, a rising levy, tax-number confusion and a state-level "fix it there" sentiment) explain the 69 → 63 → 60 slide.
- The biggest district-controlled levers for 2030 appear to be (a) a public record of the promised $5M in cuts and the tax-rate history ("receipts"), (b) published pass and fail budgets, and (c) a commitment device like Waunakee's state-aid pledge. These are inferences from the survey themes and session questions, not tested effects.

## 8. Comparison districts: Dane County November 2026 referendums

### Takeaway
Only two Dane County-based districts had November 2026 school questions: MCPASD (passed, about 60%) and Waunakee (both passed narrowly, about 52–53%). Lodi, whose Dane portion is small, also had two questions. **Madison, Verona, Sun Prairie, McFarland, Oregon, Monona Grove and DeForest had no November 2026 school referendum** that I could find, so a direct same-ballot comparison is limited to Waunakee. Statewide, comparable suburban districts that passed did so in the low 60s.

### Inferences
- MCPASD's roughly 7.5-point edge over Waunakee's operating question on the same ballot is consistent with several district-level differences: a non-recurring renewal versus a permanent increase, MCPASD's more Democratic electorate, and a specific failure consequence.
