# Demographic, Household and Socioeconomic Predictors of Yes and No Votes on School Operating Referendums, Levies and Budget Votes (US)

Scope note: these notes leave out partisanship, property tax level, the size of the tax increase, and having school-age children. Those appear only where a study uses them as controls. "Individual" means survey, exit-poll or voter-file evidence about individual people. "Ecological" means precinct, ward, district, county or state aggregates. Ecological correlations cannot show how a given type of person votes. Studies before 2000 are marked **[pre-2000]**.

## 1. Age and seniors (65+): how strongly do older voters oppose, does it survive controls, and do long-tenured seniors differ from newcomer seniors?

### Takeaway
Older voters *without children in the schools* are less supportive than younger voters in surveys. In referendum and spending data, though, the "gray peril" turns out much smaller and less consistent than the folk wisdom, especially for **local** (as opposed to statewide) school spending. Studies agree most on one point: **long-tenured, aging-in-place seniors are neutral or even supportive, while recently arrived seniors are the opposition bloc.** Two caveats. Aging in place lowers support when the student body is racially different from the seniors. And because seniors are overrepresented in low-turnout elections, the same preferences matter more in special elections than in a November general election.

### Cited Findings
**Individual-level (survey and exit poll) evidence**
- Brunner & Balsdon (2004, *J. Urban Economics* 56:369–388) used a California PPIC survey of 2,007 likely voters (2002). Respondents gave their vote intention on a statewide school bond (Prop 47) and on a local school bond. Among homeowners, 61% backed the state measure and 69% the local one. The model controls for ideology, race, gender, income, college, economic outlook and years at current address. Childless homeowners showed these marginal effects relative to homeowners under 35:
  - ages 45–54: **−24 pts on the state measure but only −11 pts on the local one**;
  - ages 55–64: −20 pts state, **−9 pts local (not significant)**;
  - 65+: −12 pts state, **−3.6 pts local (not significant)**.
  
  Their conclusion: "older voters without children generally oppose increases in state spending but are much more willing to support local spending." They attribute this to capitalization plus intergenerational altruism. — [Brunner & Balsdon working paper, UConn 2003-24](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2003-24.pdf); [journal record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/juecon/v56y2004i2p369-388.html)
- In the same data, the steepest drop in support came among **middle-aged (45–54) childless homeowners, not those 65+**. Among parents with children in public school, the oldest group did not vote much differently from younger parents. Parents aged 45–54 were the exception, at −14 pts on the local measure. — [Brunner & Balsdon 2003](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2003-24.pdf)
- **[pre-2000]** Rubinfeld (1977, *Review of Economics and Statistics* 59(1):30–42) surveyed voters in two Detroit-area local school elections. Once the model accounts for whether the household has children in public school, the household head's age "does not have any additional explanatory power." Wyckoff (1984), surveying another Michigan town, found the same. — [Poterba 1998 review, AER P&P](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/Demographic%20Change%2C%20Intergenerational%20Linkages%2C%20an.pdf); [Brunner & Balsdon 2003](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2003-24.pdf); [Rubinfeld record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/tpr/restat/v59y1977i1p30-42.html)
- **[pre-2000]** Opinion surveys show a large raw age gap:
  - In the 1988 ANES, 77% of 18–29-year-olds favored more federal aid to schools, against 47% of those 70+ (Vinovskis 1993). — [Poterba 1998](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/Demographic%20Change%2C%20Intergenerational%20Linkages%2C%20an.pdf)
  - In the 1986 GSS, 74% of under-35s said education spending was too low, against 50% of those 65+ (Ponza et al. 1988). — [Brunner & Balsdon 2003, fn. 3](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2003-24.pdf)
- Plutzer & Berkman (2005, *Public Opinion Quarterly*) ran a cohort-period analysis of about 35 years of survey data. They conclude that the conventional wisdom is "based on a confusion of age and cohort effects." In their data, "every cohort becomes more supportive of educational spending, rather than less, as they reach their 60s and 70s." Today's seniors were similarly conservative on school spending a generation earlier, so the gap is not caused by retirement. Plutzer adds that seniors can "be supportive of educational spending but still vote against bonds and tax increases." — [Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-graying-of-america-and-support-for-funding-the-nations-school/); [EurekAlert release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/741037)
- Lang & VanCeylon (2025/2026, *Applied Economics Letters* 33(12)) used exit polls and found Democratic affiliation the strongest predictor of school bond support. "Wealthy and middle-aged voters" were more likely to approve **local** bonds than statewide ones, which the authors attribute to visible costs and direct benefits. — [URI Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.uri.edu/enre_facpubs/204)
- Marquette Law School Poll (Wisconsin): respondents over 60 "overwhelmingly" said lowering property taxes mattered more than school funding. Only about 28% of registered voters have children in school. Only the WPR summary was seen; the crosstabs were not retrieved. — [WPR](https://www.wpr.org/news/marquette-poll-wisconsin-voters-want-lower-property-taxes-vs-funding-schools)

**Ecological evidence on referendums and budget votes**
- **[pre-2000]** Button (1992, *Social Science Quarterly*) studied precinct-level school bond referendums in six Florida counties with large elderly populations. In five of the six, the share of voters aged 55+ had a significant negative effect on approval. The 55+ share had no significant link to approval of **non-school** tax increases, so this is not general anti-tax sentiment. — [Poterba 1998](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/Demographic%20Change%2C%20Intergenerational%20Linkages%2C%20an.pdf); [Figlio & Fletcher 2010](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16137.pdf)
- **[pre-2000]** Contrary evidence from the same era:
  - Rosenbaum & Button (1989) and Button & Rosenbaum (1989) did not find strong generational conflict.
  - Deller & Walzer (1993) surveyed rural Illinois and found that "retirees support local education spending, albeit at a lower level than non-retirees."
  
  — [Tosun, Williamson & Yakovlev 2009, IZA DP 4161](https://docs.iza.org/dp4161.pdf)
- Ehrenberg, Ehrenberg, Smith & Zhang (2004, *Educational Evaluation and Policy Analysis* 26(1):111–125) studied a panel of New York school budget votes. They found "no evidence that older voters are more likely to vote against school budgets." In fact, the higher a county's share of residents over 65, the *less* likely its districts' initial budgets were defeated. Districts with more elderly residents were also less likely to fall to contingency budgets. The authors suggest districts in older counties may moderate their proposals. — [NBER w9088](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w9088/w9088.pdf); [published version info](https://www.iza.org/publications/dp/1053/imprint)
- Schlaffer (2018, *Social Science Quarterly* 99(1):118–135) studied California education facility bonds. The 65+ population share was negative but **not significant**. Its interaction with the share of **recent movers** was significantly negative: districts with more recently arrived seniors were less likely to pass bonds. He concludes the "elderly population is not uniform in its opposition." — [IDEAS abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/socsci/v99y2018i1p118-135.html); [dissertation version](https://shareok.org/bitstream/11244/44058/1/2016_Schlaffer_James_Dissertation.pdf)
- Kogan, Lavertu & Peskowitz (2018, *AJPS* 62(3):637–651) linked more than 10,000 school tax referendums to voter microtargeting data. Election "timing has the most significant impact on voter age, with the elderly being the most overrepresented group in low-turnout special elections." The resulting shifts in composition had "modest" effects on outcomes overall, and the effects varied by state. — [IDEAS abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/amposc/v62y2018i3p637-651.html)

**Ecological evidence on spending levels (not votes)**
- **[pre-2000]** Poterba (1997, *J. Policy Analysis & Management* 16(1):48–66) used a US state panel for 1960–1990. Per-child spending had an elasticity of about **−0.25** with respect to the 65+ share. A one-standard-deviation rise in the elderly share (from 10.8% to 13.0%) implied an almost 5% drop in per-pupil spending. The estimate became insignificant once urbanization was controlled. — [NBER w5677](https://www.nber.org/papers/w5677.pdf)
- Ladd & Murray (2001, *Economics of Education Review*) worked at the county level with fixed effects and found no significant elderly effect. Harris, Evans & Schwab (2001, *J. Public Economics*) worked at the district level and found the effect significant "but... small," and Fletcher & Kenny (2008) also found it small. All three studies challenge Poterba. — [Figlio & Fletcher 2010 lit review](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16137.pdf); [Ladd & Murray record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ecoedu/v20y2001i4p343-357.html); [Fletcher & Kenny record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/tpr/edfpol/v3y2008i3p283-315.html)
- Berkman & Plutzer (2004, *Social Science Quarterly* 85(5):1178–1192) studied more than 9,000 school districts. They found that "longstanding older residents represent a source of support for educational expenditures while elderly migrants lower spending." Elderly concentrations help a district financially unless they include many newcomers. This work is folded into their book *Ten Thousand Democracies* (Georgetown UP, 2005). — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/socsci/v85y2004i5p1178-1192.html); [Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/gray-peril-or-loyal-support-the-effects-of-the-elderly-on-educati/); [book, Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/books/9781589014206)
- Figlio & Fletcher (NBER 2010; *J. Public Economics* 2012) matched 1950/1960 Census tracts to postwar suburban development. The share of seniors who **age in place** was negatively related to school support, and "as a suburb ages, its support for schooling falls." The effect depends heavily on race: at mean racial mismatch, the coefficient on the 65+ share is −0.246, but at one SD above mean mismatch it is −1.175. This qualifies the Berkman–Plutzer "loyal long-timers" finding. — [NBER w16137](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16137.pdf)
- Tosun, Williamson & Yakovlev (2009) used a state and county panel with spatial methods. Their results "broadly support the presence of intergenerational conflict and age heterogeneity in preferences for education spending among elderly migrants." — [IZA DP 4161](https://docs.iza.org/dp4161.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a November general-election operating referendum like MCPASD's, the defensible prior is this: seniors as a group are modestly less supportive than younger non-parents. The local-measure survey penalty is on the order of 0–10 points before party controls (Brunner & Balsdon), and smaller still for long-time residents. The bigger senior "No" lever is probably *recent arrivals* and *turnout composition*, not age itself.
- In the one study with clean controls, the largest local-bond penalty belonged to **45–64-year-old childless homeowners** (empty nesters and people who never had children), not those 65+. A paper on MCPASD levers should not treat "65+" as the only age-based No group.
- Ward-level 65+ share (already in `drivers_by_ward.csv`) will mix long-tenured seniors with senior newcomers, such as retirees in new condos or 55+ developments. The literature predicts opposite signs for the two groups, so a near-zero ward correlation for 65+ would fit the research, not contradict it.
- November turnout dilutes the senior skew that Kogan et al. document for special elections. MCPASD's high-turnout November 2026 electorate likely worked in the Yes side's favor compared with an April or special election.

### Gaps
- No study found estimates the senior effect on a Wisconsin operating (revenue-limit) referendum with individual-level data and party controls.
- Tedin, Matland & Weiher (2001, *Journal of Politics*; a Houston study of age × race in school referendums) is widely cited for an age-by-race interaction. The original text and a verified summary could not be retrieved in this session, so no effect size is reported here.
- Button (1992) and Berkman & Plutzer (2004) magnitudes come from secondary summaries and abstracts. The full-text coefficients were not retrieved.

## 2. Homeownership vs. renting: do renters support school taxes more once party is held constant?

### Takeaway
Renters say they support local school spending somewhat more than homeowners do (about 79% vs. 69% in one California survey). This fits "renter illusion": renters do not see the property tax bill directly. But the gap is modest and mostly not significant once demographics are controlled, and it is unstable across ages. Capitalization theory (Oates; Fischel's homevoter hypothesis; Hilber & Mayer) predicts that **homeowners without children support local school spending more than renters without children** because better schools raise home values. The survey evidence fits that among older voters. The clearest homeowner effect may be on **turnout**: buying a home raises local-election turnout by about 5 points.

### Cited Findings
- In the 2002 California PPIC survey, **79% of renters vs. 69% of homeowners** backed the local school bond, and 75% vs. 61% backed the statewide one. The authors argue renter illusion cannot explain the pattern: homeowners favored local over state spending by 8 pts, renters by only 4. — [Brunner & Balsdon 2003](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2003-24.pdf)
- Within that survey, age penalties were *larger* for renters than for homeowners on the local measure:
  - renters aged 55–64 were **30 pts** less likely than younger renters to support the local bond;
  - homeowners aged 55–64 were only **9 pts** less likely than younger homeowners.
  
  This is "consistent with the hypothesis that capitalization leads older homeowners without children to prefer local over state spending." Renters 65+, however, were no less likely to support the local bond, while being 32 pts less likely to support the state one. That points to altruism as well. — [Brunner & Balsdon 2003](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2003-24.pdf)
- Hilber & Mayer (2009, *J. Urban Economics* 65(1):74–90) argue that capitalization gives childless owners a self-interested reason to fund schools, since "only about one-third of all households have children in public schools." They measure capitalization by how scarce undeveloped land is.
  - In Massachusetts after Proposition 2½, towns with little undeveloped land showed bigger house-price responses to spending and smaller construction responses.
  - Across districts in 46 states, a district with 68.1% of developable land already built spends "roughly 10 percent more per pupil" than one with 6.6% built.
  - A one-SD increase in percent elderly raises spending by 0.8–2.7% in built-out districts relative to districts with open land. The direct elderly effect is "either insignificant or negative."
  
  — [NBER w10804](https://www.nber.org/papers/w10804.pdf); [JUE record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:juecon:v:65:y:2009:i:1:p:74-90)
- In Schlaffer's California bond-passage models, the owner-occupancy share was negative but **not significant**. He notes this is "consistent with the 'renter's illusion' hypothesis" (citing Martinez-Vazquez 1983; Oates 2005). — [Schlaffer 2016 dissertation](https://shareok.org/bitstream/11244/44058/1/2016_Schlaffer_James_Dissertation.pdf)
- Hall & Yoder (2022, *Journal of Politics*) used Ohio deed and turnout records for more than 18 million people. Buying a home raised turnout in local general elections by about 5 pts, from 26% to 31%, roughly a 20% increase. The effect was more than twice as large in the top home-price decile, and nearly doubled when zoning measures were on the ballot. — [Stanford GSB summary](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/index%2Ephp/insights/if-you-lived-here-you-might-be-voter-now); [paper](https://andrewbenjaminhall.com/homeowner.pdf)

### Inferences
- With party held constant, a renter "Yes premium" of roughly 0–10 points is plausible. It is not a strong lever. The larger renter effect in practice is probably **lower turnout and higher roll-off**, given the Hall & Yoder turnout gap.
- In a built-out, high-amenity suburb with expensive housing and limited greenfield land, capitalization theory predicts stronger support from childless owners. Where large greenfield subdivisions remain, capitalization weakens, because new supply absorbs the price effect. This may help explain differences between MCPASD's urban wards and its town or rural wards.

### Gaps
- No study found that estimates the renter-vs-owner vote gap on a school *operating* referendum with party controls using individual vote data.
- Brunner & Sonstelie (2003, *JUE* 54(2):239–257) and Brunner, Sonstelie & Thayer (2001, *JUE*) study precinct returns on California voucher initiatives. Their capitalization results apply to voucher votes, not levies, and their abstracts were not retrieved here. — [Brunner & Sonstelie record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:juecon:v:54:y:2003:i:2:p:239-257)
- Fischel's "homevoter hypothesis" (2001) and Oates (1969) were not retrieved in this session. They are the theoretical anchors and should be cited from the originals.

## 3. Education attainment and income

### Takeaway
**College education is a consistent positive predictor** of support in both individual and ecological data, but the effect is moderate and shrinks for local measures once ideology is controlled. **Income has no consistent independent effect** in individual data once education and ideology are controlled. At the district level, higher-income districts in New York were *more* likely to reject budgets after controlling for education. In Milwaukee, wealthier, less child-dense wards voted more Yes. The income sign depends on context.

### Cited Findings
- In Brunner & Balsdon's California homeowner logit, a college degree raised support by **+8 pts on the statewide** bond (significant) and **+4 pts on the local** bond (not significant). Income had essentially zero effect: marginal effects of +0.001 and −0.001, not significant. Being white lowered support by 11 pts on both. Women were 6–9 pts more supportive. — [Brunner & Balsdon 2003](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2003-24.pdf)
- In New York school budget votes, "districts with higher income residents are more likely to vote down budget proposals," while districts with more college graduates are less likely to. The paper reports that a 10-pt rise in the college share is associated with a 0.2-pt drop in the probability of budget defeat. Districts with more college-educated residents were also less likely to fall to contingency budgets. — [Ehrenberg et al., NBER w9088](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w9088/w9088.pdf)
- In California bond passage, the share with a college degree was positive but not significant. — [Schlaffer 2016](https://shareok.org/bitstream/11244/44058/1/2016_Schlaffer_James_Dissertation.pdf)
- Lang & VanCeylon (exit polls) found wealthy voters more likely to approve local than statewide bonds. — [URI Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.uri.edu/enre_facpubs/204)
- Milwaukee Public Schools, April 2024 ($252M operating referendum, 51.0% Yes). Marquette's John Johnson found the referendum "garnered larger margins in wealthier areas of the city where people had fewer kids." Ward-level Yes rates by share of households with children:

  | Share of households with a child under 18 | Yes |
  |---|---|
  | Under 20% | 65.8% |
  | 20–40% | 45.9% |
  | 40–60% | 46.9% |

  — [WILL 2024 report](https://will-law.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Referenda-2024-PB-with-Cover.pdf); [Marquette Law blog](https://law.marquette.edu/facultyblog/2024/04/highlights-from-the-2024-spring-election-in-milwaukee/)
- Poterba (1997) estimates an income elasticity of state school spending of about 0.5 without the poverty control. This is spending, not votes. — [NBER w5677](https://www.nber.org/papers/w5677.pdf)
- Amiel, Knowles & Reschovsky (2016, Lincoln Institute) modeled Wisconsin revenue-limit referendums with a Heckman selection model. "Some socio-economic characteristics of the median voter are important" in whether boards *hold* referendums. Fund balances and non-school property taxes also mattered, and neighboring districts' passage boosted both proposals and passage. The page does not break out income or education effects on voter approval. — [Lincoln Institute WP](https://www.lincolninst.edu/publications/working-papers/political-economy-voter-support-school-property-taxation/)
- In Wisconsin ward data, the presidential vote gap between the most- and least-educated wards widened from 22 pts (2016) to 33 (2020) and 36 (2024). Education is now strongly entangled with partisanship. — [Marquette Law blog](https://law.marquette.edu/facultyblog/2024/11/how-wisconsin-split-its-ticket-once-again/comment-page-1/)

### Inferences
- Since about 2016, college attainment in Wisconsin wards is heavily collinear with Democratic vote share. In MCPASD ward data, much of the "education effect" will likely be absorbed by the partisan baseline already in `drivers_by_ward.csv`. Any leftover college effect beyond partisanship is probably small, in the low single digits.
- Income cuts both ways. Higher income means more ability to pay and higher home values that capitalization protects, but also higher absolute tax bills. Wealthier, low-child-density areas can be strongly Yes, as in Milwaukee's lakefront wards, when they are also Democratic and highly educated.

### Gaps
- Bergstrom, Rubinfeld & Shapiro (1982, *Econometrica*), a Michigan survey with income elasticities of school demand, was not retrieved. — [Econometrica record](https://econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1982/09/01/micro-based-estimates-demand-functions-local-school)
- No Wisconsin study found that separates education from partisanship in referendum votes.

## 4. Length of residence, newcomers, in-migration and new construction

### Takeaway
For *seniors*, newcomer status predicts opposition (Berkman & Plutzer; Schlaffer; Tosun et al.). For voters in general, years at the current address had **no significant effect** in the one individual-level study with controls. No rigorous study found compares how new-construction residents (new subdivisions) vote with how long-time residents vote. Evidence on new residents is limited to the senior-migrant literature and anecdotes.

### Cited Findings
- In the California PPIC homeowner models, "years in current residence" was not significant: −0.5 pts state, −1.6 pts local per category step. It correlates 0.42 with age among childless homeowners. — [Brunner & Balsdon 2003](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2003-24.pdf)
- Elderly *migrants* lower district spending, while long-standing elderly residents support it (9,000+ districts). — [Berkman & Plutzer 2004](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/socsci/v85y2004i5p1178-1192.html)
- A high share of recent movers among seniors lowers the probability of California bond passage. — [Schlaffer 2018](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/socsci/v99y2018i1p118-135.html)
- In 1990 suburbs, by age 45 the share of newcomers (five or fewer years in the home) levels off. Fewer than 20% of elderly households are newcomers. Under age 50, childless suburban households are about 10 pts more likely than families to be newcomers. — [Figlio & Fletcher 2010](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16137.pdf)
- Kelsey, Smith & Luloff (*Agricultural and Resource Economics Review*, vol. 24; rural Pennsylvania, a state without local tax referendums) compared in-migrant and aged-in-place seniors' attitudes toward local taxes. A *high* elderly share did not change the local tax mix, but an *increasing* share did. This is about tax structure, not school votes. — [AgEcon Search](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/31595/files/24020174.pdf)
- Anecdotal: in Idaho bond campaigns, some No voters said new residents "should bear the burden of expansion." This is reported sentiment, not measured vote data. — [Idaho EdNews (via search summary)](https://www.idahoednews.org/?p=80365)

### Inferences
- In MCPASD, new-construction areas such as new subdivisions in the City of Middleton, the Town of Middleton and Cross Plains tend to house young families, so they skew pro-school through the presence of children. New senior housing would be the exception the literature flags as likely No territory.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found measures how residents of new subdivisions vote on school referendums.
- No Wisconsin data found on newcomers versus long-time residents. ACS "year householder moved into unit" by block group could be joined to MCPASD wards to test this.

## 5. Private-school, home-school and open-enrollment-out households

### Takeaway
Theory and every author treat private-school parents as likely No voters, but **there are few direct estimates**. The ecological private-school share was a fragile negative predictor in New York. The best modern evidence is about **turnout, not vote choice**: in Michigan, households using inter-district choice (close to Wisconsin open enrollment out) voted 15% less often in school bond elections, and charter households 12% less often. No quantitative evidence was found for homeschool households.

### Cited Findings
- New York school budget votes: the district share of students in private schools became "a statistically significant negative predictor of budget pass rates" only when enrollment growth was dropped from the model. It "was not statistically significant when enrollment growth was included." — [Ehrenberg et al., NBER w9088](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w9088/w9088.pdf)
- Brunner & Balsdon set aside voters with private-school children because they "would be expected" to oppose public school spending increases. Their survey estimates therefore say nothing about this group's actual vote. — [Brunner & Balsdon 2003](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2003-24.pdf)
- **[pre-2000]** Rubinfeld (1977) is widely cited for finding lower support among private-school (largely Catholic) parents in Detroit-area school elections. Only secondary references to his survey design were seen here; the coefficient was not verified. — [Rubinfeld record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/tpr/restat/v59y1977i1p30-42.html)
- Mcilwain & Reckhow (MSU working paper, May 2025) matched Michigan enrollment records for about 100,000 households to voter files from off-cycle 2017 and 2019 bond elections. Turnout was **15% lower** in households with children in inter-district choice and **12% lower** in households with children in charter schools. The gaps are "large enough to potentially impact the outcome of very close school bond elections." Only 24% of families with children in affected districts voted at all. The design is not causal, and no vote-choice data were reported. — [EdWeek, May 2025](https://www.edweek.org/policy-politics/how-school-choice-complicates-district-bond-elections/2025/05)
- National context: about 9.5% of students attend private school (survey-based). — [ed100 summary](https://ed100.org/lessons/privateschools)

### Inferences
- In MCPASD, open-enrollment-out families and private-school families are a small share of households. Their likely effect is a few hundred votes at most, through abstention as much as through No votes. That is small next to a 4,806-vote margin.

### Gaps
- No study found estimates how **homeschool** households vote on levies.
- No study found gives vote-choice (as opposed to turnout) estimates for private-school or open-enrollment-out households in local levy elections.
- MCPASD-specific counts of open-enrollment-out students and private/parochial enrollment by attendance area are needed (Wisconsin DPI open enrollment data; ACS B14002 public vs. private enrollment).

## 6. Race and ethnicity, and the "racial generation gap"

### Takeaway
The best-replicated race finding is about **mismatch**. When older residents and voters are whiter than the student body, school spending falls (Poterba 1997; Ladd & Murray 2001; Figlio & Fletcher 2012), and older electorates are least representative in majority-nonwhite districts (Kogan et al. 2021). In vote-level data, white voters are somewhat less supportive than nonwhite voters with controls (−11 pts in California). Neighborhood context matters: support falls in neighborhoods with fast-growing Latino populations (Houston). But in California parcel taxes, racial context affected whether boards *proposed* taxes, not whether voters approved them.

### Cited Findings
- **[pre-2000]** Per-child spending falls more when the elderly and school-age populations differ by race. Holding the nonwhite share of the 65+ population constant, a 1-pt rise in the nonwhite share of the 0–17 population cuts per-child spending by about 0.5%. — [Poterba 1997, NBER w5677](https://www.nber.org/papers/w5677.pdf)
- Ladd & Murray (2001), at the county level, found no elderly effect but did find that racial heterogeneity between the elderly and children reduces spending. — [Figlio & Fletcher 2010 review](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16137.pdf)
- The aging-in-place effect is five times larger at one SD above mean racial mismatch (coefficient −1.175 vs. −0.246). — [Figlio & Fletcher 2010](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16137.pdf)
- **[pre-2000]** Anecdote: elderly white voters in Holyoke, MA "do not support programs that benefit young nonwhites," and support fell as the student body shifted (Bulkeley 1991). — [Poterba 1998](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/Demographic%20Change%2C%20Intergenerational%20Linkages%2C%20an.pdf)
- Individual-level: white homeowners were 11 pts less likely to support both state and local school bonds, controlling for ideology, income, college, age and children. — [Brunner & Balsdon 2003](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2003-24.pdf)
- Moskowitz (*Urban Affairs Review*; data from the 1996 Houston ISD bond) found neighborhood effects:
  - **Latino neighborhoods:** a non-Latino voter had a 51.85% probability of supporting the bond in a neighborhood with no Latinos, and 37.89% in one over 95% Latino.
  - **Black neighborhoods:** a white voter had a 34.61% probability in a neighborhood under 1% Black, and 51.57% in the highest-density Black neighborhood.
  
  She reads this as racial threat in the first case and contact effects in the second. — [UAR blog summary](https://urbanaffairsreview.squarespace.com/uar-archive/2021/01/28/building-public-schools-in-the-city-the-role-of-neighborhood-context-on-voter-support-for-school-bonds)
- Nations & Martin (2020, *Social Science Quarterly* 101(6)) studied 293 parcel-tax measures across 967 California districts, 1997–2010. Boards were least likely to *propose* taxes where the Latinx student share was high, or where the gap between the white student share and the white share of residents 65+ was large. Once a tax was proposed, these measures had "no measurable influence on the propensity of voters to approve it." — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/socsci/v101y2020i6p2220-2237.html)
- Kogan, Lavertu & Peskowitz (2021, *APSR* 115(3)) combined validated turnout records with microtargeting data. Most school board voters do not have children in the local public schools, and the demographic gap between voters and students is largest in majority-nonwhite districts. — [Cambridge](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/democratic-deficit-in-us-education-governance/22C2591CF26F46663D14BFAC5CBACF4C); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/apsrev/v115y2021i3p1082-1089_25.html)
- Wisconsin: in Milwaukee's 2024 referendum, majority-white wards voted 53.2% Yes, majority-Black wards 48.4%, and majority-Latino wards 49.4%. The author calls these differences small next to the gap by child density. — [Marquette Law blog](https://law.marquette.edu/facultyblog/2024/04/highlights-from-the-2024-spring-election-in-milwaukee/)

### Inferences
- MCPASD's electorate is mostly white. Its student body is more diverse than its 65+ population, but likely much less so than the districts behind the mismatch findings. The racial-generation-gap mechanism is probably a weak lever locally. Ward-level race variation inside MCPASD is small, so it will be hard to detect.

### Gaps
- No verified national figure on the "racial generation gap" was retrieved; the PolicyLink report URL returned 404.
- Tedin, Matland & Weiher (2001) on age × race in Houston school referendums was not retrieved; see Section 1 Gaps.
- No MCPASD-specific comparison was found between the racial makeup of students (DPI) and that of residents 65+ (ACS).

## 7. Household structure: grandparents, families with preschoolers, empty nesters

### Takeaway
Researchers have proposed grandparent ties and intergenerational altruism as reasons seniors support schools, but **no study found measures grandparents' votes directly**. Empty nesters and other childless middle-aged homeowners (45–64) show the largest local-measure penalty in survey data. No evidence was found on how families with only preschoolers vote.

### Cited Findings
- Ehrenberg et al. note that the gray-peril "allegation ignores the fact that many of the elderly are grand parents" with "a continued vested interest" in local schools if their grandchildren live nearby. This is a hypothesis, not a test. — [NBER w9088](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w9088/w9088.pdf)
- **[pre-2000]** Poterba suggests that weakening family links between older and younger residents may explain why the elderly-spending relationship changed over time. He cites the limited evidence on how many elderly live with or near their children (Crimmins & Ingegneri 1990). — [Poterba 1998](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/Demographic%20Change%2C%20Intergenerational%20Linkages%2C%20an.pdf)
- Older renters without children were no less supportive of the *local* bond, despite having no capitalization stake. The authors read this as intergenerational altruism. — [Brunner & Balsdon 2003](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2003-24.pdf)
- Childless homeowners aged 45–54 were 11 pts less supportive of the local bond, the largest local penalty, and 24 pts less supportive of the state bond. Parents aged 45–54 with children still in public school were also 14 pts less supportive locally. — [Brunner & Balsdon 2003](https://media.economics.uconn.edu/working/2003-24.pdf)
- About 25% of suburban householders aged 55–59, and over 10% of those 60–64, still had children at home (1970/1980 Census). Age alone is therefore a noisy proxy for having no children at home. — [Figlio & Fletcher 2010](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16137.pdf)

### Inferences
- The "recent empty nester" (45–64, children recently graduated, at peak earnings and tax bill) may be a more important persuadable or No segment than the 65+ group. This is suggestive, from one California survey.
- Parents of preschoolers are future beneficiaries and should behave like parents. No study tests this, and they tend to be younger, lower-turnout renters or new owners.

### Gaps
- No study found that measures how grandparents with grandchildren in the local district vote.
- No study found on families with only preschool-age children.

## 8. Distance from schools and attendance-area effects

### Takeaway
**No rigorous study found** measures how support for an operating referendum varies with home-to-school distance or attendance area. The closest evidence is a **polling-place effect**: voters assigned to vote inside a school were slightly more supportive of an Arizona education-funding initiative (about 2 pts raw, about 0.5 pts with controls). The rest is anecdote about siting disputes on facility bonds.

### Cited Findings
- Berger, Meredith & Wheeler (2008, *PNAS*) studied Arizona's 2000 Prop 301 (an education sales-tax increase). 56% of voters assigned to polling places in schools voted Yes, vs. 54% elsewhere. The paper's Table 1 reports controlled precinct-level estimates of about 0.5–0.6 pts, in specifications that reference schools within 0.20 and 0.40 miles; the exact specification details were seen only in a search summary. A follow-up experiment found school images raised support among non-parents. Parents supported the measure regardless. — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2449328); [Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/can-polling-location-influence-how-voters-vote); [Nature news](https://www.nature.com/news/2008/080623/full/news.2008.908.html)
- Anecdotal siting effects on facility bonds: Lake Stevens, WA residents questioned a proposed school's proximity to a creek, and the bond fell short of 60%. Some Idaho No voters cited the location of a new elementary school. — [Lake Stevens bond survey](https://www.lkstevens.wednet.edu/community/local-funding-measures/bond-survey-summary); [Idaho EdNews](https://www.idahoednews.org/?p=80365)
### Inferences
- An *operating* referendum, unlike a building bond, has no new-building site to create attendance-area winners and losers. Distance effects should be weaker than for facility bonds. The likelier geographic lever is attachment to a specific threatened program or school, plus voting in a school building.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed estimate was found for how Yes share varies with distance to an existing or proposed school, or by attendance area. An APPAM 2016 paper on "racial distance" in Texas bonds could not be accessed (host blocked in this session).
- Cellini, Ferreira & Rothstein (2010, *QJE*) is often cited for finding that facility-bond passage raises home values (regression discontinuity design). It was not retrieved here, so whether it says anything about proximity is unverified.

## 9. Cross-cutting: direction, effect size, method, era, replication; individual vs. ecological

### Takeaway
After setting aside partisanship and having children, the predictors with the most consistent, replicated signs are: **(a) senior newcomers lower support, while aging-in-place seniors are mixed and race-dependent; (b) college education raises support, modestly; (c) race-age mismatch lowers support, at least at the proposal and spending stage; and (d) seniors' outsized share of low-turnout electorates.** Renter status, income, length of residence for non-seniors, private-school households, and distance are weak, inconsistent or barely studied.

### Inferences
- Ranked by likely size of the *non-partisan, non-children* levers in a high-turnout November Wisconsin operating referendum:
  1. turnout composition, meaning who shows up;
  2. senior newcomers versus long-tenured seniors;
  3. middle-aged empty-nester homeowners;
  4. college education beyond what partisanship already captures (small);
  5. renters (mainly a turnout effect);
  6. private-school, open-enrollment-out and homeschool households (small numbers).
  
  Race and distance are likely minor in MCPASD.
- Most ward-level correlations in MCPASD (65+ share, apartment share, college share) will be confounded with partisanship and with the presence of children. The individual-level studies above are the better guide to mechanisms. MCPASD ward regressions should control for the partisan baseline before reading any demographic effect.

### Gaps
- No individual-level study of a Wisconsin operating referendum with demographic and party controls was found.
- No published School Perceptions crosstabs by age, tenure or homeownership for MCPASD or a comparable Dane County district were found.
- Several classic studies were seen only through abstracts or secondary summaries: Button 1992, Tedin et al. 2001, Rubinfeld 1977 coefficients, and Brunner & Sonstelie 2003. Their exact magnitudes should be checked against the originals before they are quoted.
