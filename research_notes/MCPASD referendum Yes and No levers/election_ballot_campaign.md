## 1. Election timing and turnout: do school referendums do better in low-turnout spring/special elections or high-turnout November elections?

### Takeaway
In Wisconsin and most states, school tax measures do **better** in high-turnout November elections, not worse. Wisconsin's best evidence finds the probability of passage is about **23 points lower in April statewide (judicial) elections** and **18 points lower in special elections** than in presidential Novembers, with **no significant penalty for midterm Novembers**. The old belief that low turnout helps the Yes side holds only where seniors are exempt from school taxes (Texas). Direction: November helps Yes. Size: large (about 15-23 percentage points in passage probability). Confidence: moderate. The data are strong, but the authors call the outcome models descriptive because districts choose their dates strategically.

### Cited Findings
- **Kogan, Lavertu & Peskowitz (2018, *AJPS*)**: more than 10,000 school tax and bond referenda in CA, OH, TX and WI, matched to Catalist voter-file data. Wisconsin data cover about 2000-2016. Wisconsin turnout, as a share of voting-age population, is **63.8% in presidential elections**, then **−9.15 pts in midterms**, **−30.12 pts in spring "general statewide" (judicial) elections**, **−29.86 pts in primaries** and **−34.86 pts in specials**. All differences are significant at p<.001. [descriptive, district fixed effects] — [Kogan et al. 2018 PDF](https://joeornstein.github.io/pols-4641/readings/Kogan%20et%20al.%20-%202018%20-%20Election%20Timing,%20Electorate%20Composition,%20and%20Policy%20Outcomes.pdf)
- Same paper, Table 6, on the probability of passage. Wisconsin's baseline in presidential elections is **0.78**. Changes from that baseline: midterm **−0.07** (SE 0.08, not significant); general statewide/spring **−0.23** (SE 0.06, p<.001); primary **−0.08** (n.s.); special **−0.18** (SE 0.06, p<.01). Other states: Ohio specials −0.10 (p<.001). California midterm −0.15, primary −0.15 and special −0.16 (all significant). Texas specials **+0.15** (p<.05). — [Kogan et al. 2018](https://joeornstein.github.io/pols-4641/readings/Kogan%20et%20al.%20-%202018%20-%20Election%20Timing,%20Electorate%20Composition,%20and%20Policy%20Outcomes.pdf)
- Kogan et al. explain Texas's opposite sign with that state's freeze on school property taxes for homeowners 65 and older, which makes school taxes "essentially free to senior citizens." Their reading is that seniors are the pivotal low-turnout bloc everywhere. Whether low turnout helps or hurts depends on whether seniors pay the tax. — [Kogan et al. 2018](https://joeornstein.github.io/pols-4641/readings/Kogan%20et%20al.%20-%202018%20-%20Election%20Timing,%20Electorate%20Composition,%20and%20Policy%20Outcomes.pdf)
- Kogan et al. warn that their outcome analysis "is descriptive rather than causal." Districts choose dates strategically, for example "to beat the bad news by scheduling a special election." They call the outcome models "suggestive, not definitive." — [Kogan et al. 2018](https://joeornstein.github.io/pols-4641/readings/Kogan%20et%20al.%20-%202018%20-%20Election%20Timing,%20Electorate%20Composition,%20and%20Policy%20Outcomes.pdf)
- **Meredith (2009, *Economics & Politics* 21(1):159-177)** studies all Wisconsin school referenda from 1990 to 2006. Holding constant the vote share of pro-education-spending candidates in high-turnout elections, boards are "significantly more likely" to hold referenda in special elections when pro-spending candidates do better in low-turnout elections. So boards time questions strategically to reach a friendlier median voter. Effect sizes were not retrievable because the PDF returned 403. [descriptive; **secondhand** on details] — [IDEAS abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/ecopol/v21y2009i1p159-177.html); [working paper](https://www.sas.upenn.edu/~marcmere/workingpapers/StrategicTiming.pdf)
- **Contested:** Meredith's strategic-timing result and Kogan et al.'s finding that specials *lower* passage in Wisconsin can both hold. Boards may use specials only where low-turnout voters are unusually supportive, and still do worse on average. Kogan et al. explicitly cite Meredith as an example of off-cycle strategic-timing claims. — [Kogan et al. 2018](https://joeornstein.github.io/pols-4641/readings/Kogan%20et%20al.%20-%202018%20-%20Election%20Timing,%20Electorate%20Composition,%20and%20Policy%20Outcomes.pdf)
- **Wisconsin law since 2017-18 largely removes the special-election option.** 2017 Wis. Act 59, the 2017-19 budget, limits school boards to two referendum resolutions per calendar year. 2017 Act 141 ties referenda to regularly scheduled election days. In even years the usable dates are the February primary, the April election, the August primary and November. — [DPI referendum procedures](https://dpi.wi.gov/sfs/reporting/safr/referendum/procedures); [2017 Act 59](https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/2017/related/acts/59); [2017 Act 141](https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/2017/related/acts/141); [WILL 2024](https://will-law.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Referenda-2024-PB-with-Cover.pdf)
- **Wisconsin Policy Forum (Nov. 2024):** "Referenda passage rates typically increase amid the higher voter turnout of presidential and midterm election years." Fall 2024 passage was **77.5%** vs **60.2%** in spring 2024. [descriptive] — [WPF Focus, Nov. 2024](https://wispolicyforum.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Focus_24_21_FallReferendum.pdf)
- WPF annual passage rates: **2018 89.7%, 2020 85.6%, 2022 80.1%**. — [WPF, "A Record Year for Referenda"](https://wispolicyforum.org/research/a-record-year-for-referenda/) **[secondhand via search summary]**. **2024 70.1%**, "the lowest passage rate in a midterm or presidential election year since 2014." — [WPF Focus, Nov. 2024](https://wispolicyforum.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Focus_24_21_FallReferendum.pdf)
- **Texas shows the reverse in recent cycles, in the same pattern Kogan et al. predict.** November 2021: 51 of 110 school bond proposals passed (46%). May 2021: 93 of 113 (about 82%). — [TASB, Jan. 2022](https://www.tasb.org/news-insights/school-bond-support-wavers)
- **Practitioner model (School Perceptions / WASB, 2021):** a Wisconsin logit model of referendum outcomes. It has a positive coefficient on turnout² and calls "The fewer people that vote, the more likely a referendum is to pass" a myth (Myth #3). May timing is negative (−1.26 logit) and October positive. [practitioner; model specification and sample not documented in the deck] — [WASB/School Perceptions deck](https://www.wasb.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Will-My-Referendum-Pass-or-Fail.pdf)
- **Related candidate-election literature:** off-cycle school-board elections are robustly linked to higher teacher pay (Anzia 2011, 2012; Berry & Gersen 2011). Anzia's Texas natural experiment found that districts forced on-cycle granted significantly smaller teacher raises. Kogan et al. counter that school employees are a small share of even low-turnout electorates. In Wisconsin, teachers are more than 4% of presidential-election voters, and school employees rarely exceed 10% even in specials. So the employee-bloc mechanism is weaker than claimed. — [Anzia 2012, *QJPS*](https://nowpublishers.com/article/Details/QJPS-11056); [Kogan et al. 2018](https://joeornstein.github.io/pols-4641/readings/Kogan%20et%20al.%20-%202018%20-%20Election%20Timing,%20Electorate%20Composition,%20and%20Policy%20Outcomes.pdf)

### Inferences
- MCPASD's choice of a November gubernatorial-midterm ballot was the Yes-favorable timing under Wisconsin evidence. The best Wisconsin estimate puts a midterm November about the same as a presidential November (−0.07, not significant) and roughly 16-23 points of passage probability above an April or special date.
- The spring penalty is likely larger for an older, high-home-value electorate. A future MCPASD renewal moved to April would face an electorate with about 8-10 points more voters aged 65+ (see Q2) at roughly half the turnout (Wisconsin spring turnout is about 30 points of voting-age population below presidential, vs. 9 points below for midterms).
- Since 2018 Wisconsin districts cannot use true specials. The real timing choice is April vs. November, and the evidence favors November for a Dem-leaning district.

### Gaps
- Meredith (2009) effect sizes could not be read (403).
- No Wisconsin study was found that isolates midterm-with-governor-race vs. presidential effects after 2016, or that covers 2018-2026 with Kogan-style controls.
- No causal (quasi-random date assignment) estimate of timing effects on Wisconsin operating referendums was found.

## 2. Who are the marginal (extra) voters in a high-turnout election, and how do they vote on school questions?

### Takeaway
Compared with every other election type, the extra voters in high-turnout presidential Novembers are younger, more Democratic and liberal, more diverse, poorer, and more likely to have children at home. In Wisconsin, a **midterm** November's edge over April is mainly **age and parenthood**, not partisanship. Kogan et al.'s estimates imply the 65+ share is about 8 points lower and the share with a child at home about 3 points higher than in April. The Democratic share in midterms is *not* higher than in spring (−3.14 vs. −2.26 relative to presidential). On average these voters lean Yes. They are also less informed and more swayed by ballot wording and tax cues, and more likely to skip down-ballot questions. Direction: helps Yes on composition, adds wording and roll-off risk. Size: composition shifts of 1-10 points. Confidence: moderate to high for composition (voter-file data); lower for how these voters actually vote.

### Cited Findings
- Kogan et al. (2018), Wisconsin, voter composition vs. presidential baseline:
  - **Democrats: 50.73%** of the presidential electorate; −3.14 in midterms, −2.26 in spring statewide, −3.65 in specials (all significant).
  - **Liberals: 40.51%**; −1.78 to −2.72 in the other election types (p<.001).
  - **Child in household: 31.66%**; −0.77 in midterms (n.s.), **−3.99 in spring statewide** (p<.001), −3.13 in primaries.
  - **65 and older: 36.09%**; +1.59 in midterms (n.s.), **+10.01 in spring statewide** (p<.001), +7.24 in primaries, +8.02 in specials.
  - [descriptive, voter file] — [Kogan et al. 2018](https://joeornstein.github.io/pols-4641/readings/Kogan%20et%20al.%20-%202018%20-%20Election%20Timing,%20Electorate%20Composition,%20and%20Policy%20Outcomes.pdf)
- Kogan et al. summarize: "less habitual voters appear to participate during presidential election years but sit out local democracy most other times, and these voters tend to be younger, less white, poorer, and more liberal." Across states, the senior share rises by 8-16 points in specials from a baseline of about 35%. — [Kogan et al. 2018](https://joeornstein.github.io/pols-4641/readings/Kogan%20et%20al.%20-%202018%20-%20Election%20Timing,%20Electorate%20Composition,%20and%20Policy%20Outcomes.pdf)
- Partisanship and passage, cross-sectional: moving Obama 2008 share from 1 SD below to 1 SD above the mean is linked to only about a **3-point** higher probability of passage. School tax measures are "generally less partisan and ideological than other issues." — [Kogan et al. 2018](https://joeornstein.github.io/pols-4641/readings/Kogan%20et%20al.%20-%202018%20-%20Election%20Timing,%20Electorate%20Composition,%20and%20Policy%20Outcomes.pdf)
- **Heller & Lyon (2026):** November electorates are those "where turnout is higher and the median voter tends to be younger and less informed." The negative effect of Texas's tax-disclaimer wording is entirely driven by November elections, with no effect off-cycle (detail in Q3). — [Heller & Lyon, EdWorkingPaper 26-1500](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai26-1500)
- **Kousser & Mullin (2007, *Political Analysis*):** in high-turnout 2000, designated mail voters rolled off down-ballot contests and propositions significantly more. There was no effect in lower-turnout 2002, which the authors attribute to "fewer marginal voters" in low-turnout elections. Marginal voters are the most roll-off-prone. [natural experiment] — [Kousser & Mullin 2007](https://www.eac.gov/sites/default/files/document_library/files/Political-Analysis-2007-Kousser-428-45.pdf)
- **Within-district geography can run against the "families vote Yes" rule.** Marquette's John Johnson found Milwaukee's $252M April 2024 referendum, which passed with 51.0%, drew larger margins in wealthier wards with fewer children. WILL reads this as higher-child, lower-income wards being less able to afford the tax. [descriptive, ward-level; **secondhand** via WILL] — [WILL 2024](https://will-law.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Referenda-2024-PB-with-Cover.pdf)

### Inferences
- In MCPASD, a strongly Democratic district (governor two-party Dem share about 69-85% across units in internal data), the extra November voters were likely net-Yes. The 2026 governor's-race turnout surge also added low-information voters. These voters (a) skip the question at higher rates and (b) are more responsive to cost cues. That fits the 2026 rise in City of Middleton roll-off (Q6).
- Wisconsin's midterm and presidential electorates differ little on age and children (Kogan: midterm effects n.s.). The 2022-to-2026 comparison (both gubernatorial midterms) is therefore close to like-for-like on composition. The 2.8-point Yes decline is better explained by the size of the ask, tax mood and roll-off than by timing.

### Gaps
- No individual-level (voter-file) study was found showing how marginal November voters vote on school questions, as opposed to who they are. The aggregate link is inferred.
- No Wisconsin-specific survey of the 2026 gubernatorial electorate's referendum vote by age or parent status was found.

## 3. Ballot wording and question design: dollar framing, "exceed the revenue limit" language, recurring vs. nonrecurring, stated tax impact, length, number of questions, operating vs. capital

### Takeaway
The most rigorous evidence is on **tax-salience wording**, and it is large. Making the property-tax consequence explicit on the ballot cuts support by **4-12 points in vote share and 9-24 points in passage probability**, with the biggest effects in **November** elections. Wisconsin ballots currently state only the dollar amount over the revenue limit, not a per-home tax impact or a "tax increase" disclaimer. That works in the Yes side's favor, and pending 2025 bills would change it. In Wisconsin, nonrecurring questions historically pass more often than recurring ones, and capital questions have recently passed more often than operating ones. Direction: tax salience helps No; nonrecurring and "renewal" framing help Yes. Confidence: high for tax disclaimers (difference-in-differences plus pre-registered experiments); moderate for recurring vs. nonrecurring (descriptive); low for question length.

### Cited Findings
- **Heller & Lyon (2026), Texas HB3 (2019)**, which requires "THIS IS A PROPERTY TAX INCREASE" on all school bond ballots. Difference-in-differences vs. municipal bonds [causal quasi-experiment]:
  - Passage probability **−9.1 points** (an 11.9% relative drop from a 76.0% control mean). Yes vote share **−7.5 points** (control mean 60.1%).
  - In **November elections**: vote share **−12.5 points**, passage **−22.8 points**. Null effects off-cycle.
  - Triple-difference with North Carolina: passage −12.4 points (−24.1 in November); vote share −8.4 (−12.1 in November).
  - More than a 50% drop in funds raised by bond-proposing districts over five years.
  - — [Heller & Lyon 2026 PDF](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai26-1500.pdf)
- Heller & Lyon pre-registered survey experiments [causal]:
  - The ambiguous disclaimer cuts support by **9.6-12.0 points**.
  - A detailed disclaimer projecting a **small** tax impact (+$1.10 per $100K) had essentially **no effect** (−1.58 points).
  - A **large** impact (+$110 per $100K) cut support by **−18.4 points**.
  - Voters treat the vague disclaimer as if it projected about **$68.40 per $100K**.
  - Disclaimers raise the share weighing property taxes (+14.1 / +16.2 points) and lower attention to building conditions (−11.8) and technology (−11.4). Salience "shift[s] attention away from the benefits."
  - — [Heller & Lyon 2026](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai26-1500.pdf)
- **Brunner, Robbins & Simonsen (2018, *Public Budgeting & Finance*)**: Minnesota (ballots must state that voting yes is voting for a property tax increase) vs. Wisconsin (no such statement), school bonds 2008-2017. Tax salience cuts the Yes share by about **4-7 points** and passage probability by about **10 points**. The result is robust to propensity weighting and to border-distance designs. [descriptive/quasi-experimental] — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/pbudge/v38y2018i4p52-73.html)
- **Brunner, Robbins & Simonsen (2021, *Public Administration Review*)**: in a survey experiment, property-tax information cuts the probability of support by **6-9 points** depending on wording. Vague disclaimers are treated like large increases. — [DOI 10.1111/puar.13201](https://doi.org/10.1111/puar.13201); summarized in [Heller & Lyon 2026](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai26-1500.pdf)
- A related UConn study found that using the word "tax" rather than "revenue" lowers support by about **6-8 points**. **[secondhand]** — [UConn DPP summary, 2021](https://publicpolicy.uconn.edu/2021/10/23/dpp-faculty-research-citizens-willingness-to-pay-for-public-services/)
- **Burnett & Kogan (2015, *Political Communication*)**: a survey experiment showing that "simple changes to the text" of ballot measures had significant effects, even on issues expected to resist persuasion. A summary notes many voters use a "defensive no" when the choice is complex. Effect sizes were not retrieved. **[secondhand]** — [DOI 10.1080/10584609.2014.894160](https://doi.org/10.1080/10584609.2014.894160)
- **Wisconsin's current ballot form** must state the dollar amount by which the levy or revenue limit is exceeded. **2025 SB 58** would add "a good faith estimate of the annual dollar amount difference in property taxes on a median-valued, single-family residence." Companion **AB 60** would also require the property value used. In committee, groups warned the estimate could confuse voters. Enactment status was **not confirmed**. — [SB 58 text](https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/2025/related/proposals/sb58); [AB 60](https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/2025/related/proposals/ab60.pdf); [WPR, May 2025](https://www.wpr.org/news/wisconsin-school-referendums-budget-limitations-proposals)
- **Recurring vs. nonrecurring, Wisconsin:**
  - WPF, 1994-2013: nonrecurring **56%** vs. recurring **34%** passage. **[secondhand]** — [WPF 2014 report](https://wispolicyforum.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/1402_School-Referenda.pdf)
  - Spring 2024: nonrecurring 60.0% (45 questions), recurring **54.5%** (22), debt 60.0% (35). — [WILL 2024](https://will-law.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Referenda-2024-PB-with-Cover.pdf)
  - April 2026: 32 of 56 nonrecurring passed (57%) vs. **5 of 6 recurring** (small n). **[secondhand]** — [Wisconsin Examiner, Apr. 2026](https://wisconsinexaminer.com/2026/04/09/wisconsin-schools-struggling-under-funding-system-consider-next-steps-after-referendum-results/)
- **Operating vs. capital, Wisconsin 2024:** operating 66.2% vs. capital **76.3%**. The 2024 operating rate was the lowest midterm/presidential-year rate since 2012. — [WPF Nov. 2024](https://wispolicyforum.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Focus_24_21_FallReferendum.pdf)
- **Practitioner model (School Perceptions / WASB 2021)** [practitioner]:
  - Negative coefficients on "Recurring" (−0.7 logit), "NR 4-yr" (−0.55), levy, dollar amount and tax impact².
  - Uses for athletics: 29.2% predicted passage vs. 75% for other uses (−45.8 points).
  - "Anchoring" raised passage from 67.4% to 87.5% (+20.1 points). "Anchoring" is not defined in the deck.
  - "Length" was among candidate predictors but does not appear in the final models.
  - — [WASB deck](https://www.wasb.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Will-My-Referendum-Pass-or-Fail.pdf)
- **Number of questions / unbundling:** Texas's 2019 law requires stadiums, performing arts, recreation, natatoriums and technology to be separate propositions. In Fort Worth ISD's November 2021 package, Prop A ($1.2B for middle schools) passed by fewer than 100 votes, while Props B-D (stadium, fine arts, athletics) failed. — [TASB 2022](https://www.tasb.org/news-insights/school-bond-support-wavers)
- **Opposition framing of design choices:** WILL argues districts "game the system" in three ways: (1) mill-rate messaging that hides valuation-driven tax growth (Monroe projected about $13 per $100K; actual increases were reportedly 10-15 times that); (2) balloon debt schedules; (3) not disclosing how much taxes would *fall* if an expiring referendum is not renewed (e.g., Racine's "Flat Tax Rate" slogan). [advocacy] — [WILL 2024](https://will-law.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Referenda-2024-PB-with-Cover.pdf)

### Inferences
- For MCPASD, Wisconsin's lack of a mandated tax disclaimer is a structural Yes advantage, worth roughly 4-10 points on Brunner et al.'s MN-WI comparison. If SB 58/AB 60-style per-home disclosure becomes law, Heller & Lyon's dose-response results suggest it would hurt most where the per-home impact is large. MCPASD has high home values and an ask escalating to $34.6M, so it would be exposed.
- The November-concentrated wording effects (Heller & Lyon) mean the high-turnout ballot that helped MCPASD on composition also made its voters more sensitive to whatever cost cue reached them. On the No side, opposition messaging that mimics a "THIS IS A TAX INCREASE" disclaimer is the closest evidence-backed lever.
- The 2026 question was nonrecurring and partly a re-authorization of the expiring $24.9M. Both features are historically associated with higher Wisconsin passage. The escalation to $34.6M by 2030-31 works the other way (Q4).

### Gaps
- No rigorous study was found on Wisconsin's statutory "exceed the revenue limit" phrasing itself, on total-dollar vs. annual-dollar framing, or on question length.
- No study was found that isolates the effect of a district-*stated* per-$100,000 impact in campaign materials (as opposed to on the ballot) in Wisconsin.
- The MCPASD per-$100K impact and the exact ballot text were not compiled in this note.
- Bowers & Lee (2013, Texas bonds) could not be downloaded to check their timing and size predictors.

## 4. Size of the ask (relative to budget or per pupil) and "renewal vs. new money"

### Takeaway
Bigger asks and bigger per-home tax impacts lower support, and renewals of existing taxes pass far more often than new money. In Ohio, renewals historically pass about 85% of the time and new levies far less often. Survey experiments show voters respond to the size of the tax impact: a trivial impact does no harm, while $110 per $100K costs about 18 points. Direction: larger and new asks help No; renewal framing helps Yes. Confidence: moderate. Evidence is mostly descriptive, plus experimental dose-response.

### Cited Findings
- Heller & Lyon (2026) survey experiments: support is unchanged at +$1.10 per $100K and falls **18.4 points** at +$110 per $100K. "Voters are sensitive to the magnitude of changes in property tax obligations." [causal, hypothetical] — [Heller & Lyon 2026](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai26-1500.pdf)
- School Perceptions/WASB Wisconsin models carry negative coefficients on levy size, referendum dollar amount (−0.018 in the vote-share model) and tax impact² (−458 in the logit model; −1,408 in the vote-share model). [practitioner] — [WASB deck](https://www.wasb.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Will-My-Referendum-Pass-or-Fail.pdf)
- **Ohio renewal vs. new:**
  - Howard Fleeter (Ohio Education Policy Institute): renewal levies historically pass about **85%** of the time over roughly 30 years, while new levies and bonds pass far less often. Renewals fell to 65% in March 2024. **[secondhand]** — [Axios Columbus, Mar. 2024](https://www.axios.com/local/columbus/2024/03/27/ohio-school-levy-fail-2024-election)
  - May 2026 Ohio primary: 24 of 66 levies passed (about 36%), vs. 64% in May 2025 and 52% in May 2024. OSBA's Tom Hosler: "Renewals are holding, but new asks are harder." — [WYSU, May 14, 2026](https://wysu.org/ohio-news/2026-05-14/why-did-so-many-ohio-school-levies-fail-in-the-may-2026-election)
  - November 2026 Ohio: **55%** of property-tax increase requests passed vs. **80%** of renewals. Only 5 of 39 new district income taxes passed. — [EdWeek, Nov. 2026](https://edweek.org/leadership/school-districts-asked-voters-for-more-funding-many-said-no/2026/11)
- **Wisconsin context on size:** the total amount requested hit a record **$5.9B** in 2024, up from $3.3B in 2022. Approval rates fell as asks grew, though WPF does not test causation. Milwaukee's $252M recurring operating referendum passed with only 51.0%. — [WPF Nov. 2024](https://wispolicyforum.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Focus_24_21_FallReferendum.pdf); [WILL 2024](https://will-law.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Referenda-2024-PB-with-Cover.pdf)
- WILL argues that waves of referenda after about 2014 reflect districts replacing expiring referenda with "it will not raise taxes" framing, and that districts don't disclose the tax cut voters would get if the referendum expired. [advocacy] — [WILL 2024](https://will-law.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Referenda-2024-PB-with-Cover.pdf)
- Minnesota practitioner commentary holds that paring down an ask makes passage "far more likely." A district survey applied an 8% discount to stated poll support. [anecdotal] — [Brainerd Dispatch](https://www.brainerddispatch.com/news/local/school-district-survey-favors-operating-levy)

### Inferences
- The 2026 MCPASD question was a hybrid. It re-authorized the expiring $24.9M (renewal-like, Yes-favorable), then escalated to $34.6M (+39%) by 2030-31 (new money, No-favorable). Ohio's 85%-vs-much-lower renewal gap suggests the renewal component carried much of the Yes vote. The Yes campaign's lever is to lead with "renew, not raise." The No campaign's lever is to frame the escalation as $118.5M of new taxes and to stress the tax cut voters would get if the levy expired (the WILL argument).
- Earlier internal benchmarks place MCPASD's ask as the second-largest operating request in the state in November 2026. Under the size findings, a 60.1% Yes is strong performance for that scale.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed estimate of passage elasticity with respect to ask per pupil or ask as a share of budget was retrieved for Wisconsin. Bowers & Lee (Texas) and Bowers, Metzger & Militello (Michigan) were not accessible.
- No Wisconsin dataset separating "pure renewal at a flat amount" from "renewal plus increase" pass rates was found.

## 5. Prior referendum history: does a past pass or fail, or repeated asks ("referendum fatigue"), change support?

### Takeaway
In Wisconsin, retries after a failure usually succeed eventually: about 71% of retried questions pass, often after the district shrinks the ask. Retry success has slipped recently (about 47% in spring 2025). No rigorous test of "fatigue" was found. Practitioner modeling calls operational-referendum fatigue a myth, while Ohio practitioners blame "levy fatigue." No study was found on whether *passing* an earlier referendum raises or lowers support for the next one. Confidence: low to moderate.

### Cited Findings

  - Since 1990, **684 of 1,081** initially failed Wisconsin referenda (58.6%) were retried within two years, and **484 (70.8%) eventually passed**.

### Inferences
- MCPASD's history (2022 passed with 62.9%; 2026 passed with 60.1%) fits a "successful incumbent renewal" pattern rather than a retry. A 2.8-point erosion while asking for more money, in a year when the statewide pass rate fell about 22-25 points, does not suggest strong fatigue in MCPASD specifically.

### Gaps

- No evidence was found on whether passing a prior referendum, or delivering on its promises, raises later support. "Anchoring" in the WASB deck may relate to this, but it is undefined.

## 6. Ballot roll-off and undervoting on local questions: who skips, how much it matters, and how campaigns reduce it

### Takeaway
Roll-off on local questions grows with ballot length and position ("choice fatigue"). Voters who reach a question tired are also more likely to default to the status quo, which is a No on a tax question. The best causal estimate (California) is that removing fatigue would cut abstention by 8% and flip 6% of failed propositions to passing. That finding is contested by a study showing no ballot-order effect on approval. In MCPASD, 2026 roll-off was 7.2% district-wide (8.9% in the City of Middleton), up from about 5%. Restoring 2022 rates would have added only an estimated 100-150 votes to the Yes margin. No rigorous evidence was found on "vote both sides" reminders.

### Cited Findings
- **Augenblick & Nicholson (2016, *Review of Economic Studies*)**: California, using variation in the number of local contests placed above a given contest. More preceding decisions significantly raise abstention and reliance on shortcuts (status quo, first-listed candidate). "Without choice fatigue, abstentions would decrease by 8%, and 6% of the propositions would have passed rather than failed." [causal, natural experiment] — [IDEAS abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/restud/v83y2016i2p460-480..html)
- **Contested:** a *Public Choice* (2016) study of 242 California propositions from 1958 to 2014 finds no robust ballot-position effect on approval rates after controlling for pre-election opinion (correlation of −0.01). It concludes decision fatigue does not create order effects except perhaps on very long ballots. A 2025 *PSRM* study finds selective abstention depends more on a proposition's net benefit to voters than on its position, though position still matters. — [Public Choice 2016](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/pubcho/v167y2016i3d10.1007_s11127-016-0340-9.html); [PSRM 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/EC79C51B5ADD31C105AB16B80FA05752/core-reader) **[secondhand]**
- Rogers & Middleton's randomized mail program (Oregon 2008) changed vote margins on initiatives but had **no effect on roll-off** or turnout. Persuasive mail does not reduce skipping. [causal] — [Rogers & Middleton HKS WP](https://appext.hks.harvard.edu/publications/getFile.aspx?Id=943)
- Kousser & Mullin (2007): roll-off was higher among mail voters in the high-turnout 2000 election, consistent with marginal voters being roll-off-prone. — [Kousser & Mullin 2007](https://www.eac.gov/sites/default/files/document_library/files/Political-Analysis-2007-Kousser-428-45.pdf)
- **MCPASD 2026 (internal computation from Dane County precinct data):**
  - Roll-off was **7.2% district-wide** (1,834 under-votes plus 5 over-votes on 25,559 ballots with the question). City of Middleton **8.9%**, which accounts for 59% of all MCPASD roll-off.
  - On comparable fully-in-district geography, roll-off rose from **5.2% (2022) to 6.8% (2026)**. It fell in the Village of Cross Plains (6.5% to 3.2%) and in Madison Wards 118-119.
  - The 2026 ballot placed **three statewide constitutional-amendment questions** before the MCPASD question. Those drew 2.5-3.7% roll-off in the same units, vs. 1-2% on 2022's county advisory questions.
  - Had the City and Town of Middleton kept 2022 roll-off rates, about 550 more Yes/No votes would have been cast, adding an estimated **100-150 to the Yes margin**.
  - Only the City of Madison agreed to add "check both sides" reminders.
  - — Internal: `/home/user/openseatsmcpasd/research_notes/MCPASD referendum 2026 vs 2022/ward_results.md`; `rolloff_by_contest.csv`; source data [Dane County 2026 results](https://elections.countyofdane.com/Election-Result/197)
- General undervote research: a presidential-election study notes undervoting rises with age beyond 50. A straight-ticket-voting study finds non-college voters more often confused, while college-educated voters more often abstain on purpose. Neither concerns local referenda. **[secondhand; weak relevance]** — [ResearchGate: "Roll Off at the Top of the Ballot"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/227617394_Roll_Off_at_the_Top_of_the_Ballot_Intentional_Undervoting_in_American_Presidential_Elections)

### Inferences
- The MCPASD pattern of roll-off concentrated in the City of Middleton, after three statewide questions, in a high-turnout year fits choice fatigue plus low-information marginal voters. A layout difference on Middleton ballots is also possible; the internal notes call it plausible but unverified. If Augenblick & Nicholson's status-quo mechanism applies, some fatigued voters who did finish the question leaned No. So roll-off reduction is a modest Yes lever (about 0.5 points of margin), not a decisive one.
- Senior over-representation in roll-off (reported anecdotally for MCPASD) cannot be confirmed from published research. General findings point to rising undervotes after 50 and confusion among less-educated voters.

### Gaps
- No Wisconsin study of back-of-ballot placement or "vote both sides" reminders was found, and no randomized test of reminder effects on local-question roll-off.
- No individual-level data were found on who rolled off the MCPASD question. Ward data cannot separate age groups.

## 7. Absentee, mail and early voting: does vote mode change referendum support?

### Takeaway
No study was found that directly estimates the effect of absentee, mail or early voting on Yes shares for school referendums. Available evidence covers participation, not direction: mail voting raised roll-off in a high-turnout election and had no effect in a lower-turnout one. Confidence: low.

### Cited Findings
- Kousser & Mullin (2007, California natural experiment): designated mail voters were significantly more likely to abstain on propositions and down-ballot races in 2000, with no effect in 2002. Mail voting slightly reduced turnout overall. — [Kousser & Mullin 2007](https://www.eac.gov/sites/default/files/document_library/files/Political-Analysis-2007-Kousser-428-45.pdf)
- An Oregon all-mail election experiment found persuasion mail shifted initiative margins without changing turnout or roll-off. Persuasion works in all-mail systems. — [Rogers & Middleton](https://appext.hks.harvard.edu/publications/getFile.aspx?Id=943)

### Inferences
- Because early and absentee ballots are cast before the campaign's final weeks, timing of persuasion matters. Kalla & Broockman find early-contact persuasion decays (Q8). Yes and No campaigns should time persuasion contacts to the absentee window as well as to Election Day.

### Gaps
- No study was found of vote mode and school-referendum Yes share, including any Wisconsin analysis. Dane County reports absentee and Election Day ballots combined by ward, so MCPASD mode effects cannot be estimated from public results.
- No Wisconsin source was found documenting partisan or age differences in absentee use in 2026.
- A search summary mentioned California research finding higher residual (blank) vote rates on propositions among absentee voters than in-person voters. The source could not be identified or read, so it is not used.

## 8. Campaign contact and messaging: canvassing, mail, yard signs, endorsements, social media, opposition campaigns; field experiments; persuasive messages

### Takeaway
Ballot-measure campaigns can persuade, unlike general-election candidate campaigns. A well-powered precinct-randomized mail program shifted initiative margins by **2.5-6 points** (about 1.25-3 points of Yes share), and yard signs add about **1.7 points** (pooled, SE 0.7). Ohio levy data link campaign spending to passage, but no randomized study of a school levy campaign was found. On messaging, cost information is the strongest No lever: telling people actual per-pupil spending cuts support for more spending by about 22 points in surveys, and tax salience pulls attention from benefits. For Yes, evidence supports keeping concrete benefits salient. Wisconsin RD evidence shows operating referenda improve student outcomes, and bond passage raises home values. Confidence: moderate for mail and signs (causal but not school-specific); low to moderate for messaging (surveys).

### Cited Findings
- **Rogers & Middleton (2013 working paper; published 2015)**: precinct-randomized test of a PAC's statewide mail program in Oregon, 2008, covering about 18% of households (320,921 registered voters).
  - Treatment changed vote margins on **10 of 12 initiatives**, by **2.5 to 6 points**.
  - No effect on turnout or roll-off, so effects came "by changing how citizens vote."
  - Two initiatives "would have passed if not for the advocacy campaign to reject them."
  - The two non-moving measures were uncontested blowouts (70.5% and 74.4% Yes), suggesting a ceiling.
  - [causal] — [HKS working paper](https://appext.hks.harvard.edu/publications/getFile.aspx?Id=943)
- **Kalla & Broockman (2018, *APSR*)**: a meta-analysis of 40 field experiments plus 9 new ones. The average persuasive effect of contact in **general elections is zero**. The draft reports "meaningful persuasive effects in primary and ballot measure campaigns, when partisan cues are not present." Early-contact effects decay before Election Day. [causal meta-analysis; ballot-measure claim per working-paper draft] — [APSR article page](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/753665A313C4AB433DBF7110299B7433); [working paper](https://escholarship.org/content/qt103775sx/qt103775sx.pdf?t=qamtj9)
- **Green, Krasno, Coppock et al. (2016, *Electoral Studies*)**: four randomized lawn-sign experiments across 376 precincts (NY, PA, VA), including one independent-expenditure campaign. Pooled effect **+1.7 points** vote share (SE 0.7), with some spillover into adjacent precincts. No single study was significant alone. [causal] — [Green et al. 2016 PDF](https://acoppock.github.io/green_etal_2016.pdf)
- **Ingle, Johnson, Givens & Rampelt, Ohio new operating levies 2007-2010** (logistic regression):
  - "Campaign spending was related to levy success, but types of expenditures were largely insignificant."
  - Employee benefits as a share of budget were negatively associated with passage; salaries positively.
  - Ingle reportedly said yard-sign spending stood out, while warning that correlation is not causation.
  - [descriptive; abstract-level, **secondhand**] — [catalog record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_1316197293)
  - Johnson & Ingle (2009) found more campaign strategies and more commercial/industrial property raised passage odds. — [ERIC record](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/eric__EJ863959) **[secondhand]**
- **Information and cost messaging (survey experiments):**
  - Howell & West (2008/09): accurate information on current spending lowered support for increases and confidence that more money improves learning. Respondents' median estimate of per-pupil spending was $2,000 vs. more than $10,000 actual. — [Education Next, Howell & West](https://educationnext.org/wp-content/uploads/2008/07/ednext_IX_3_howell_west.pdf)
  - With 2012 EdNext data, local per-pupil spending information cut the probability of supporting increases by **22 points**. Teacher-salary information similarly lowered support for raises. — [Education Next](https://www.educationnext.org/when-provided-with-accurate-information-public-support-for-increased-spending-on-schools-and-teacher-salaries-declines-researchers-find/)
  - Tax salience shifts considerations from building conditions and technology to taxes. — [Heller & Lyon 2026](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai26-1500.pdf)
- **Benefit evidence usable in Yes messaging:**
  - **Baron (2022, *AEJ: Economic Policy*)**: a Wisconsin close-election regression discontinuity. Passing an operational referendum has "substantial positive effects on test scores, dropout rates, and postsecondary enrollment." The 2019 version estimates about a **$500 (5%)** rise in per-pupil operating spending. Capital referenda show little effect. An Institute for Replication re-analysis exists; its conclusions were not checked. [causal] — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aejpol/v14y2022i1p1-39.html); [AEA podcast summary](https://www.aeaweb.org/research/school-spending-student-outcomes-wisconsin); [I4R replication listing](https://www.rwi-essen.de/i4r-discussion-paper-series/1/replication-study-of-baron-2022-school-spending-23120104)
  - **Cellini, Ferreira & Rothstein (2010, *QJE*)**: California bond passage raised home prices about 3 points right away and about 6 points within three years. Homebuyers valued $1 of facility spending at $1.50 or more. This applies to capital bonds, not Wisconsin operating referenda. [causal RD] — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v125y2010i1p215-261..html); [NBER WP 14516](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14516)
- **Practitioner messaging guidance (School Perceptions/WASB 2021):**
  - Voters need answers to: where the money goes, why it is needed, and why now.
  - An "avoiding loss" frame drove an over-performing district ("The village is the school").
  - Athletics uses sharply hurt passage.
  - Partisanship "seemed to be absent" as a stated factor.
  - The district must show need and start early; an independent advocacy group (Yes or No) tailors information to voter segments.
  - [practitioner] — [WASB deck](https://www.wasb.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Will-My-Referendum-Pass-or-Fail.pdf)
- **Opposition campaigns:** TASB's Dax Gonzalez cited polarization and outside groups running vote-no campaigns as one cause of Texas's November 2021 bond collapse. Districts themselves cannot advocate, only inform. — [TASB 2022](https://www.tasb.org/news-insights/school-bond-support-wavers). A 2014 Dallas Morning News analysis was headlined "opposition groups had little impact on Texas school bond elections" (findings not read). — [Dallas News 2014](https://dallasnews.com/news/education/2014/05/16/opposition-groups-had-little-impact-on-texas-school-bond-elections) **[secondhand]**
- Turnout (GOTV) effects of contact are covered in the companion canvass notes (`/home/user/openseatsmcpasd/research_notes/Referendum canvass volunteer plan/contact_modes_and_messaging.md`). Gerber & Green's canvassing experiments are the base reference. — [Gerber & Green 2000 PDF](https://calgara.github.io/PolS5310_Spring2021/Gerber,%20Green%20-%202000%20-%20The%20Effects%20of%20Canvassing,%20Telephone%20Calls,%20and%20Direct%20Mail%20on%20Voter%20Turnout%20A%20Field%20Experiment.pdf)

### Inferences
- On the best causal evidence, a sustained, targeted persuasion mail program on a nonpartisan local measure could plausibly move MCPASD's Yes share by 1-3 points, and yard signs about 1-2 points. That is meaningful at the margin but small against a 20-point margin. A No campaign with comparable reach could move results by the same amount in the other direction (Rogers & Middleton found effects both ways).
- The strongest evidence-backed No messages: (1) actual per-pupil spending and teacher pay; (2) explicit per-home tax dollars, especially on high-value homes; (3) the tax cut voters would get if the levy expired. The strongest evidence-backed Yes counters: (1) keep concrete benefits salient (staff, programs), since tax salience otherwise crowds them out; (2) Wisconsin causal evidence that operating dollars improve outcomes (Baron 2022); (3) "renew, not raise" framing; (4) avoid athletics or amenity uses in the ask.

### Gaps
- **No randomized field experiment of a school levy or school referendum campaign was found**, for either persuasion or GOTV. All school-specific campaign evidence is descriptive or practitioner.
- No rigorous evidence was found on endorsements, social media or door hangers for local school measures specifically.
- No experiments were found that directly compare teacher-retention, class-size or property-value messages for operating referendums.
- No documentation of MCPASD 2026 Yes or No campaign spending or tactics was retrievable (Wisconsin Examiner returned 403).
